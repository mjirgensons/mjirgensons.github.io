It is late, and you have one question for ChatGPT. A large client wants your cleaning company on a new contract. Saying yes means hiring people before the first invoice is paid.

Yes or no?

The reply arrives in seconds. It is long, polite and well organised. It lists cash flow, staffing and growth. It closes by saying the decision depends on your circumstances.

You knew that. That is why you asked.

You asked one question and got a reading list. The machine wrote more in a minute than your accountant writes in a month. It did not tell you one thing you did not already know.

What an owner needs from ChatGPT, or from any AI for small business, is not more text. It is a colleague with rules.

## What AI for small business usually gets wrong

A language model has to say something, so it does. Nothing tells it what matters to you. Nothing tells it where your red lines are. So it covers everything, ranks nothing and sounds sure the whole time.

That is a talker. A talker is good company at dinner. You cannot run a business with one.

Now picture the best colleague you ever had. They asked for the file before giving an opinion. They told you first what your own numbers already showed. They said "I am guessing here" when they guessed. They asked what you would give up first if things went wrong, and then let you decide. They pointed at the one thing that could sink you. And they told you how the next months would look if you did nothing, and if the plan worked.

They did not write more. They wrote less, in the right order.

## The colleague test for your AI business assistant

Run this test on your current assistant. You need one real question and a quarter of an hour.

1. Write your situation in a few plain sentences, with your own numbers and where each one comes from.
2. Send it to ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini the way you always do.
3. Read the reply against the six questions below. Give one mark for each clear yes.

Here is the test filled in for the cleaning company. The right column is an illustration of an assistant with no rules.

| Question | What a colleague does | What the cleaning company got |
|---|---|---|
| Did it help first? | Opens with what your numbers already show and one safe action | Opened with a list of things to consider |
| Did it mark its guesses? | Says "I am assuming..." and what changes if the guess is wrong | Used a staff cost the owner never gave it, stated as fact |
| Did it ask what you would give up last? | Proposes an order of your goals and asks you to confirm it | Put growth first for you, in the opening paragraph |
| Did it name the most dangerous thing? | One event, with a date, that could cause damage you cannot undo | Named a long row of risks, all of equal weight |
| Did it give two forecasts? | "If nothing changes" and "if the plan works", each with a range | Gave one cheerful outlook |
| Did it ask only what matters? | A few questions at the end, each able to change the decision | Asked nothing at all |

All yes: you have a colleague. Mostly no: you have a talker with good grammar.

The cleaning company's reply scored zero. Not because the AI is weak. Because nobody gave it rules.

## What a talker costs you

The danger is not the long answer. You can skip a long answer. The danger is the one confident sentence inside it that you act on.

Picture the next six months. You ask, the assistant agrees, you act. It agrees with the hire, the discount and the new van. Each answer feels like a second opinion. None of them is one, because none was checked against what you would give up last.

Then a Monday comes when three things break at once. You spend the day on the loudest fire, not the most dangerous one. The assistant helps you write a fine email about the wrong problem.

A second opinion that always agrees is not a second opinion. It is an echo.

## Better wording will not fix it

The usual advice is "write better prompts". Better wording gets you a better essay.

It does not give the assistant a model of management: what a goal is, where a red line sits, what you can change yourself. So it still fills the gaps in your data with something plausible. That is how ChatGPT makes things up: not out of malice, but because a smooth answer needs no gaps.

The fix is a rulebook, not a cleverer question.

## Where the colleague stops

The files change how the assistant behaves, not what you bring to it. The rules guide the assistant. They do not make it perfect. Check its numbers against your own, every time.

And the real cause of a bad answer sits on your side of the desk. A colleague with rules asks two questions only you can answer. What would you give up first, and what last? Where does a number stop being a worry and become an emergency?

The assistant proposes answers to both. It will not choose them for you, and it should not. If your answers are vague, its analysis is precise about the wrong thing.

## FAQ

### Can I use ChatGPT for small business decisions?

Yes, as an analyst, not as the one who decides. Give it your numbers and fixed rules. Let it compute and propose. You confirm the order of your goals and make the call.

### Why does ChatGPT make things up about my business?

Because a gap in your data invites a smooth guess. Without rules, the assistant fills the gap and states the guess as fact. A rulebook that allows only your facts, shown arithmetic and labelled assumptions closes most of that door.

### What is the best AI for business decisions in a small company?

The best AI for business is the one that works by rules, whichever model runs it. Out of the box, ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini write broad advice for no one.
