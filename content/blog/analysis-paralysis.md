It's 1 a.m. You've compared the same three options for the fourth night in a row.

The laptop is the only light in the room. Your partner sleeps with their back to you. Nine tabs are open. Each one makes a good case, and none of them decides.

**Nearly half of owners lie awake at night worrying about the business.**

You're not short of brains. You make fifty small calls before lunch without blinking. You're short of something else: a rule that says which goal wins when two of them collide.

So every choice lands on you, alone, at night, with nothing to lean on. That is the weight. Not the work. The work you can do. What breaks you is carrying every decision on your back with no system under it.

The Jirgenson Model gives the answer to this pain. It sets the order of your goals once, in a calm hour. Then the night has nothing left to decide. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What is analysis paralysis?

Analysis paralysis is the state where you keep studying a choice and never make it. You collect one more quote, read one more review, run one more scenario. The cost of waiting grows quietly in the background.

The usual cure is "get more data" or "trust your gut". More data gives each option a better argument. Your gut at 1 a.m. is your fatigue in disguise.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> analysis paralysis is a symptom of a goal list with no order. When five goals are "equally important", every option serves one of them, so no option can win. The cure is the refusal order: your goals ranked by what you would give up first, written down before the choice arrives.</div>

Here is the irony. You can quote your rent to the cent. But you can't say which matters more to you, the cash reserve or your Sunday. So the spreadsheet grows, and the decision doesn't.

## Why more options make it worse

Take two plans for Andrew's coffee shop at the end of the year. The numbers are the book's own illustration.

| Goal | Plan X | Plan Y |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Cash reserve (goal 2.0 months) | 2.0, met | 1.8, not met |
| 2. Margin (goal 15%) | 15%, met | 16%, met |
| 3. Regular customers (goal 40%) | 32%, not met | 42%, met |
| 4. Complaints a week (goal 2 or fewer) | 2, met | 1, met |
| 5. Owner's hours a week (goal 40 or fewer) | 40, met | 38, met |

Count the goals met, and the plans tie: four out of five each. This is the exact spot where you lie awake.

Now read the table by order. The first row where the plans differ is row 1, the cash reserve. Plan X meets it, Plan Y doesn't. Plan X wins.

Counting goals and ordering goals give different answers. The model goes with the order. That one rule ends most of the nights.

The list also needs a ceiling. The model keeps nine goals at most for a person working without software. It echoes Miller's "seven, plus or minus two" but makes no claim about memory. The point is plainer. Ten priorities are not priorities. They are a list.

## The refusal test: end analysis paralysis in 15 minutes

Asking "What matters most to me?" is useless. The truth comes from a harder question: if you had to give up your goals one at a time, which would go first?

Take a sheet of paper. This takes 10 to 20 minutes. Do it in daylight, not at night.

### 1. Write five goals, each with a number and a deadline

A goal needs a number, a unit and a deadline. "A good profit" fails. "15% by month 6" passes. Here is Andrew's list, with M0 as today:

| No. | Goal | Value | Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cash reserve | at least 2.0 months of expenses | M12 |
| 2 | Margin | 15% | M6 |
| 3 | Regular customers | 40% of receipts | M12 |
| 4 | Quality | no more than 2 complaints a week | M3 |
| 5 | Owner's time | no more than 40 hours a week | M12 |

The six fields that make each goal checkable are in [how to set measurable goals](/blog/how-to-set-measurable-goals/).

### 2. Throw out the methods

Andrew's first draft had eight items. Three were methods, not goals: loyalty cards, a second barista, a new coffee machine. He asked of each one: what for? Loyalty cards serve regular customers. The barista and the machine both serve quality. Three items folded into two goals already on the list.

A goal says what you want. A method says how. A method on your goal list picks a road before you've seen the map.

### 3. Halve your resources and cross out one at a time

Imagine you have half the time and half the money. Cross out the goal you'd drop first. Then the next. Keep going until one is left.

Andrew put his own time last and surprised himself. He'd known for ages he was tired. He had never said out loud that he would trade his rest for the shop.

### 4. Number the list in reverse

The goal you crossed out last gets number one. For Andrew, that was the reserve. Without money in the bank the shop closes, and then nothing else on the list matters.

### 5. Put the sheet where the choices happen

Tape it by the till or pin it in your phone. Its job is to be there at 1 a.m. so you don't have to be.

The same test works far from a coffee shop. Alex, thirteen, wants a €300 bicycle by 1 September, football twice a week and unlimited console time. Games go first, football second, the bicycle stays to the end. Elena, a freelance designer, uses the order as a permission. Her time goes first: up to 55 hours a week while she writes her book, for three months only. Her income goes last.

## Priorities at work when everyone pulls the other way

The order is a choice, not a truth. Andrew showed his list to Sara, his senior barista. "I'd have put quality first," she said. "Customers see quality. They don't see the reserve."

Neither was wrong. She faces the customers; he answers for her salary. Someone else's order fits you no better than someone else's shoes. Where staff and partners are involved, you reconcile your order with their rights. You don't hand it over to them.

The common objection: "My business is different. Everything here matters." Everything matters in every business. That is why the order exists. When two goals [cannot both win](/blog/competing-priorities-conflicting-goals/), the list settles it before anyone raises their voice.

This is also where finding focus stops being a mood. Focus is the top line of your list, visible on a bad day. More on cutting a long list down is in [too many goals](/blog/too-many-goals/).

## What six more months of paralysis cost

Without an order, fatigue picks for you. Picture Andrew with five "equally important" goals. The supplier raises the price of milk. Sara asks for another barista on Fridays. He says yes, because he's tired and doesn't want to argue. A month later he runs out of money before he runs out of time.

Now the numbers. At the old 9% margin, Andrew's reserve reaches two months at M16. With the margin plan working, it gets there at M10. That's six extra months with no cushion. At 9%, one equipment breakdown costs him a full month of progress. At 15%, it costs 0.57 of a month.

Every month the margin stays at 9% instead of 15%, the shop earns €2,700 instead of €4,500. That's €1,800 a month left on the table while the decision waits.

And six more months of 1 a.m. sessions while the person you love sleeps with their back to you.

One plain line on health. A sheet of paper sorts business choices; it does not treat insomnia, anxiety or low mood. If the sleepless nights last more than a bad week, see a doctor. In a crisis, call a crisis line (988 in the US and Canada).

## Where the refusal test stops

Be honest about what you built. The order tells you which goal wins. That treats the symptom: the endless weighing stops.

The cause of the next bad night is still there. You know the reserve comes first. You don't yet know how low it can fall before you must act. And when three numbers slide in one week, the order alone can't tell you which fire is the most urgent.

Two more steps close that gap:

- **Zones and the red protocol.** The line where each number turns red, set in a calm moment, and the sequence of action when several are red at once. That's Chapter 4. The idea behind the colours is in [KPI zones](/blog/kpi-zones/).
- **Levers.** The short list of things you can change yourself, today, for each goal, and which way each one moves the number. That's Chapter 5.

Without zones, the order waits for a signal that never comes. Without levers, you know what to save and have nothing to save it with. The order is the first link of a whole loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. The model's full rules are public in [the Jirgenson Model on GitHub](https://github.com/mjirgensons/jirgenson-model).

## FAQ

### What is analysis paralysis?

It's the state where you study a decision so long that you never make it. In a business it usually means your goals have no order. Every option serves some goal, so none can win. A longer walk through the test is in [the refusal test guide](/blog/set-priorities-refusal-order/).

### Why do I start overthinking at night about business decisions?

Night is when fatigue is highest and the day's open choices come back. Write tomorrow's decisions on paper before bed, so they leave your head. Then let your ranked goal list decide them in the morning. If sleep stays broken for weeks, talk to a doctor.

### How do I set priorities at work when everything matters?

Ask which goal you would give up first if you had half the resources, then the next. Number the list in reverse. Keep it to nine goals at most, each with a number and a deadline. A tired yes often comes from [decision fatigue](/blog/decision-fatigue-small-business/), and a written order is the fix.
