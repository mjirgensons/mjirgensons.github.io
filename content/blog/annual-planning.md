Andrew's first annual plan had eight lines. Three of them weren't goals.

Launch loyalty cards. Hire a second barista. Buy a new coffee machine. Good ideas, all three. None of them said what he wanted. Each one said how.

Now read your own list for next year. "New website." "Hire a salesperson." "Grow." Ask each line one question: what for?

That is the irony of the yearly planning day: an afternoon of slides, and a shopping list of methods with no order. By March, the loudest problem of the week decides which line gets the money.

The answer to that comes from the Jirgenson Model: a goal list with an order, framed by limits you never trade. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What annual planning is, and where it breaks

Annual planning is the yearly job of choosing what a business should achieve in the next twelve months, and how.

It breaks in two places. Methods sneak in where goals belong. And the surviving goals stay "equally important" until the first bad week, when fatigue sorts them for you.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> the annual plan is a goal vector inside a frame. The vector holds at most nine measurable goals, ranked by the order in which you would give them up. Below it sit the constraints and minimum levels you never trade. Above it sits a rule for spending what is left. The vector may change over the year, but at any moment it is fixed and written down.</div>

Four steps build that page, each with Andrew's coffee shop entry.

## Step 1: Strip the methods out of the list

A goal says what you want to achieve. A method says how you will do it. Write a method into the plan, and you choose your road before you have seen the map.

Take each line and ask "what for?" until a measurable result appears.

| Line on Andrew's draft | Goal or method? | What for? | The goal behind it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Launch loyalty cards | Method | To bring customers back | Share of regular customers |
| Hire a second barista | Method | To stop the Friday complaints | Quality |
| Buy a new coffee machine | Method | To keep morning shifts from falling apart | Quality |

Three methods collapsed into two goals already on the list. That freed a place for the line that sat last on the draft: money in reserve. It became number one.

Then test each survivor for a number, a unit and a deadline. "A good profit" has no number. "15" has no unit. "15%" has no deadline. "15% by month 6" is a goal.

## Step 2: Rank what is left

Andrew ended with five goals. Months count from the night he wrote them: M0 is today, M12 is a year out.

| Rank | Goal | Value | Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cash reserve | At least 2.0 months of expenses | M12 |
| 2 | Margin | 15% | M6 |
| 3 | Regular customers | 40% of receipts | M12 |
| 4 | Quality | No more than 2 complaints a week | M3 |
| 5 | Owner's time | No more than 40 hours a week | M12 |

The rank comes from one question: if you had to drop these goals one by one, which would go first? Andrew put his own hours last, and it surprised him. The reserve went first, because without it nothing else survives.

Sara, his senior barista, would have put quality first. Neither of them was wrong. Someone else's order fits you no better than someone else's shoes.

## Step 3: Frame the goals with limits and a spending rule

A ranked list says what matters more. It does not say how much you may sacrifice. So the page gets a layer below the goals and one above.

| Layer | What it holds | Andrew's entry |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Constraints | Never traded: law, safety, contracts, the load a person can carry | Do not cut the quality of the beans. Do not delay wages |
| 2. Minimum levels | Below them, the business is no longer viable | His red lines: reserve under 1.0 month, margin under 5% |
| 3. Improvement goals | At most nine, in rank order | The five goals above |
| 4. Spending rule | How spare money and hours are shared: urgency, risk, cost | While the reserve is below a month, no spending that does not bring it back up |

The fourth row is Andrew's own sentence to Sara, who wanted loyalty cards. He said no and felt no guilt. The rule decided, not his fatigue.

One law holds the layers together: if saving the main goal would break a constraint, the constraint wins. Skip the machine service to save money this month, and you raise the odds of a breakdown next month.

## Step 4: Check the list for five defects

A plan can have every field filled and still be built wrong. Check for five defects.

| Defect | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Goals that exclude each other | The lowest prices on the street and a 15% margin |
| A missing requirement | Growth listed, taxes on time forgotten (that one belongs in the constraints) |
| Unstable goals | The ranking changes every Monday |
| A broken order | Expensive beans for "quality" while the reserve is below its minimum |
| Duplicates | "Satisfied customers" and "few complaints" as two lines |

The first check is the one with teeth. Test "10% cheaper than today and a 15% margin" against Andrew's costs. At those costs, customer numbers would need to rise by 31%. His room and his baristas could handle 10% more at most. One goal had to go before any work began.

## Quarterly planning inside the annual plan

A good annual planning template spreads the deadlines. Andrew's do that on their own: one goal due in the first quarter, one in the second, three at year end.

| Quarter | Goal due |
|---|---|
| Q1 (M3) | Complaints, no more than 2 a week |
| Q2 (M6) | Margin 15% |
| Q3 (M9) | None: check the year-end goals |
| Q4 (M12) | Reserve, regular customers, owner's hours |

Each quarter asks two questions. Is the plan working? Did the forecast match the facts? That is the whole quarterly planning template. The goals themselves, and their order, get a review every six months or once a year.

Between reviews, a goal changes only by a written decision. Andrew later raised his hours limit to 45 for six months, while opening a second shop. He wrote it down with an end date.

## Personal goal setting: the same page for a life goals list

The four layers work for a person too. Alex is thirteen. He wants a €300 bicycle by 1 September, football twice a week, and unlimited time on his games console. Football camp leaves him two free evenings a week, so the order becomes easy. Games go first, capped at 10 hours a week. The bicycle stays to the end. His constraints: no borrowing, no skipping school to earn money.

A life goals list fails the way a business plan does: too many lines, no order, methods dressed as dreams. The same four steps fix it.

## What a year without the frame costs

Picture Andrew with all five goals "equally important". A week in, the milk supplier raises prices. Sara asks for another barista on Fridays. Andrew is tired, so he agrees. A month later he runs out of money before he runs out of time.

With the plan, the reserve reaches two months of expenses at M10. If nothing changes, at M16. Six extra months one breakdown away from a closed door.

The year-end review hides the damage too. Version X of Andrew's year meets the reserve and misses regular customers: 32% instead of 40. Version Y misses the reserve, at 1.8 months, and meets the rest. Count the goals met, and the two years tie at four out of five. Read them by rank, and X wins, because X met goal number one. A review that counts goals rewards the wrong year.

"I have no time for a four-layer plan." Writing and ranking his goals took Andrew fifteen minutes. The number, unit and deadline test takes a minute per line.

## Where one page stops

The page treats one symptom: the plan without an order. Next year's crises will meet a ranking you chose with a clear head.

The cause of bad decisions sits one step further on. Every goal compares a target with a starting number, and that number is often a memory. Alex thought he had about €55 saved. He counted €40. And a ranked list cannot tell you when a goal slips from fine to trouble.

Two steps close that gap:

- **The measured starting state.** One source for every number, and the whole state measured in one evening. Chapter 2.
- **Zone thresholds and the red protocol.** Where each goal turns yellow and red, and the order of action when several turn red at once. Chapter 4.

Without them, the page tells you what matters most. It does not yet tell you where you stand, or when to act.

The page is the first link in a loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. To turn it into a working year in three passes (tonight, this week, this month), see [how to set goals for next year](/blog/goals-for-next-year/).

### Key takeaways

- Ask "what for?" of every line. Methods collapse into the goals behind them.
- A goal needs a number, a unit and a deadline.
- Rank the goals by what you would give up first and defend last.
- Frame them: constraints and minimum levels below, a spending rule above.
- Check five defects before January. Drop one of two goals that exclude each other before work begins.

## FAQ

### What is annual planning?

The yearly job of choosing what a business should achieve over the next twelve months, and how. A useful annual plan names at most nine measurable goals in a clear order.

### What should an annual planning template include?

Four layers. Constraints you never break. Minimum levels below which the business stops being viable. Up to nine ranked goals, each with a number, unit and deadline. And a rule for spending what is left. *Steer, Don't Guess* adds the fields, zones and review rhythm for each goal.

### How does quarterly planning fit into an annual plan?

Spread the goal deadlines across the year, then review the plans every quarter with two questions. Is the plan working? Did the forecast match the facts? Review the goals themselves, and their order, every six months or once a year.
