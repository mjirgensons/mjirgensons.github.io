Your shop can lose one customer in eight and still pay every bill. Lose one in seven, and you're working for free.

Do you know which line you're standing on?

Picture Monday morning. The supplier has raised prices again. You're thinking about a price rise of your own, and one fear stops you: the regulars who'll walk out.

So you spend an evening comparing your latte with the one across the street. You don't spend fifteen minutes on the one sum that says how many customers you can afford to lose.

The fear has a number. It takes one division to find it.

Pricing by fear guesses at the one thing you can calculate. The Jirgenson Model answers that pain with a control system: every lever gets a test in euros before you pull it. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What a break even point is

The break even point is the level of sales at which revenue covers all costs. Profit is zero. Below it you lose money; above it you keep some.

It rests on one split. Some costs move with the number of customers: coffee beans, milk, pastries. Some don't: rent, wages, insurance. Each sale first pays its own variable cost. What's left pays the fixed costs. Once they're covered, the rest is profit.

The formula: break even revenue = fixed costs ÷ (1 − variable costs ÷ revenue).

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> the break even point is a line under a lever, not a goal. Before you pull any lever, you ask how far the result can move against you before the lever stops paying. That distance decides whether you pull it, and how hard.</div>

## Break even analysis in 15 minutes

Take last month's accounts. Each step sits next to Andrew's entry from his coffee shop, "The Corner".

**1. Split the costs into two piles.** Variable costs rise and fall with customers. Fixed costs stay put.
Andrew: ingredients €9,000 (variable); rent, wages and the rest €18,300 (fixed). Revenue €30,000.

**2. Work out what each euro leaves behind.** Revenue minus variable costs, divided by revenue.
Andrew: (30,000 − 9,000) ÷ 30,000 = 0.70. Each euro at the till leaves 70 cents to pay the fixed costs.

**3. Find the break even revenue.** Fixed costs divided by that share.
Andrew: 18,300 ÷ 0.70 = about €26,143 a month.

**4. Turn it into customers.** Break even revenue as a share of today's revenue.
Andrew: 26,143 ÷ 30,000 = 87%. He needs 87 of every 100 customers he has today, at today's prices.

**5. Write your margin of safety.** Today's revenue minus break even revenue.
Andrew: €30,000 − €26,143 = €3,857, or 12.9% of revenue.

Check the hook against those figures. Lose one customer in eight, and profit falls from €2,700 to €75. Lose one in seven, and the shop runs €300 a month in the red.

That's the whole tool. Two piles, one division, one line.

## When to raise prices: the break even point of the lever

Here is the use of break even analysis that pays for itself. You can calculate a break even point for every lever, not only for the business.

Take a price rise. Andrew never asked whether to raise prices. He asked how many customers could leave before the rise stopped paying.

The test keeps today's profit of €2,700 as the line. Let v be the share of customers who stay. At a price 5% higher, profit is 31,500 × v − 18,300 − 9,000 × v. Set that equal to €2,700:

v = 21,000 ÷ 22,500 = 0.933.

The rise pays as long as fewer than 6.7% of customers leave.

| Price change | Customers the shop can lose before profit falls |
|---|---|
| +5% | 6.7% |
| +10% | 12.5% |

Divide the second column by the first. You get about 1.3 both times. That's the shop's rule of thumb: the price lever works while fewer than about 1.3% of customers leave for each 1% on the price.

Now the planned case. Prices up 5%, customers down 2%. Revenue becomes €30,870, ingredients €8,820, profit €3,750. The margin moves from 9% to about 12%. One lever delivered half of the six points Andrew needed.

The rise also moves the break even point. At the new prices, he can lose 18.7% of customers before profit hits zero, up from 12.9%. Plain arithmetic: 18,300 ÷ 22,500 = 0.813.

## How to raise prices without guessing

Before any lever, ask three questions. Can I move it myself, today? How long before the effect shows? Can I undo it if it fails?

Andrew's answers for prices:

| Question | Prices |
|---|---|
| Myself, today? | Yes |
| When the effect comes | In weeks; customers take time to adjust |
| Rollback | Hard: customers remember the jump |

A lever that's hard to undo needs a small step and an early signal. So Andrew wrote two rules into his plan. No more than 5% in one step. And one leading indicator: the number of receipts in the first two weeks after the change.

Here's the contrast, as an illustration. The usual way: raise prices 15% on the first Monday, "to be sure". Some customers leave, others get confused, revenue drops. Nobody can tell how much of the drop came from the price and how much from the season.

The model's way: 5%, a known line of 6.7%, and a receipt count every week. The step makes the experiment readable. At the first check, in month two, the margin stood at 10.5%. Not proof yet. A signal that the plan was working.

The true elasticity of your customers isn't known in advance. You test it with a small step. You don't guess it from the shop across the street.

## Pricing strategy for a small business: the cheapest-on-the-street trap

The break even sum also kills a popular plan before it costs you anything.

Suppose Andrew wants two things: the lowest price on the street, 10% under today, and a 15% margin. With prices down 10%, each customer leaves less behind. Customer numbers would have to rise by 31% to reach the 15% margin.

If his room and his baristas can handle no more than 10% more customers, the two goals can't live together. One of them has to go before the work begins.

Even holding today's €2,700 needs 16.7% more customers after that cut: 21,000 ÷ 18,000 = 1.167. A discount is a lever with a break even point too. It sits much further away than it feels.

One warning for the price list itself. A target margin and a markup on cost are different numbers, and mixing them up makes the test lie. The difference, with the same shop's figures, is in [margin vs markup](/blog/margin-vs-markup/).

## The cost of six more months at a thin margin

Stay at 9% and do nothing, and the sums keep running.

On the same €30,000, Andrew's target of 15% would leave €4,500 a month. His real 9% leaves €2,700. Over six months, the missing €1,800 a month adds up to €10,800.

The cash reserve feels it first. At 9%, it reaches two months of expenses at month 16. With the margin plan working, at month 10. And with a margin of safety of 12.9%, a 13% drop in customers wipes out the whole profit.

The human side is blunt. An owner working 65 hours a week for a profit that one in eight customers can erase.

Your enemy is not the price list, and it's not you. It's the price change made on a feeling, with no line drawn under it. A jump "to be sure". A discount "to win them back".

## What break even does not tell you

Be clear about what you have now. You can draw the line under each lever. That treats the symptom: you stop changing prices on a feeling.

The cause is still in place. A margin of 12.9% above break even: is that fine, a reason to act this week, or an emergency? The sum can't say. And one lever covers half the gap at best. Who decides which levers come next, what's off limits, and what happens if the first check falls short?

Two steps close that gap:

- **Zone thresholds.** Where the margin's normal zone ends and where an emergency begins, set in a calm hour. Chapter 4.
- **The one-page plan.** Five questions per goal, a check that the levers together cover the gap, and a backup lever written in advance. Chapter 6.

Without them, you know what one lever is worth. You still don't have a plan.

Break even sits in the levers stage of a closed loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. Each stage has its own tool.

## FAQ

### How do you calculate a break even point?

Divide your fixed costs by the share of each sale left after variable costs. With €18,300 of fixed costs and 70 cents left from each euro, the break even revenue is about €26,143 a month.

### What is break even analysis used for?

To see how far sales can fall before profit hits zero, and to test a lever before you pull it. The same sum shows how many customers a price rise can lose and still pay.

### When should a small business raise prices?

When the sum says the rise pays at a realistic loss of customers, and you can test it in one small step. The full method of turning that test into zones, a plan and a backup lever sits in *Steer, Don't Guess*, on Kindle.
