A car hits ice on a bend and starts to slide.

The driver has two options. Get angry at the road crew who did not grit the road. Or ease off the accelerator and steer into the skid.

The ice is not a lever. The steering wheel, the accelerator and the brake are. The driver who argues with the ice ends up in the ditch.

Now picture a Monday meeting about falling profit. Ten minutes on rent, the weather, the street and "the market". Not one minute on the price list. That is shouting at the road crew while the car is still sliding.

## What business levers are

Business levers are the parameters you can reach and change by your own decision. Prices. The menu or product range. Purchasing terms. Portion size. Payment terms. Promotion channels. Process steps.

The result you want is never on that list. Margin, sales and new customers move when you move a lever. You cannot order them to move.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> every number around a goal falls into one of three groups. Controlled parameters are your levers, and together they form the control vector. Free parameters shift on their own as your levers change. Environmental factors act from outside: you allow for them, you do not work on them.</div>

Take a heated room. The thermostat dial is the lever. The room temperature is the result. The humidity drifts with the temperature and the weather: a free parameter.

You leave the humidity alone until it does damage, such as mould. Then it earns a place among your goals and a lever of its own: a humidifier.

## Sort your numbers into three bins

This is the tool. It takes one sheet of paper and about ten minutes.

1. **Pick one goal in trouble.** Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, picked margin: 9% now, 15% wanted by month 6.
2. **Write every number and condition that touches it.** Do not filter yet.
3. **Put each item in a bin.** Lever, free parameter or outside factor. Ask of each one: does it obey me?
4. **Write what you do with each bin.** Levers you pull. Free parameters you watch. Factors you predict and allow for.

Andrew's sheet looked like this:

| Item | Bin | What Andrew does with it |
|---|---|---|
| Prices of lattes and cappuccinos | Lever | Raises them, never more than 5% in one step |
| Menu | Lever | Drops the items that sell poorly and cost a lot |
| Milk contract | Lever, through negotiation | Talks to the supplier |
| Portion size | Lever | Adjusts it, up to the point where quality suffers |
| Share of espresso drinks in sales | Free parameter | Watches it; he cannot order customers to drink fewer milk drinks |
| Rain | Outside factor | Predicts it from the weather forecast |
| Supplier price rises | Outside factor | Does not fight them; adjusts his own price |
| Rent, taxes, foot traffic | Given conditions | Off the sheet |

The free parameter row is where effort leaks. The espresso share moved every time the menu or the prices moved. Steering it directly would have burned energy and changed nothing.

The same sort works far from a coffee shop. Elena, a freelance designer, is launching a book. The share of page visitors who buy is a result. Her lever is what the page says and the order of its blocks. She rewrites the headline and watches the share respond.

## How strong is a business lever? Test it in euros

A lever on the sheet is a candidate, not a decision. Before you pull it hard, find out how strong it is. Strength means how much result one unit of the lever buys you, and under what conditions.

Price is the lever most worth testing, because its side effect is customers who walk away. Andrew's monthly figures: revenue €30,000, ingredients €9,000 that rise and fall with customer numbers, other costs €18,300, profit €2,700.

The useful question is not "will anyone leave?" Some will. The useful question is how many can leave before the rise stops paying.

| Price rise | Share of customers who can leave before profit falls |
|---|---|
| 5% | Up to 6.7% |
| 10% | Up to 12.5% |

Divide the second column by the first. Both rows give about 1.3. That is this shop's break-even rule: the price lever works while fewer than about 1.3% of customers leave for each 1% added to the price.

Nobody knows that ratio in advance for their own customers. So test it with a small step and an early count: the receipts in the two weeks after the change.

Andrew capped each step at 5%. Picture the other route: a 15% rise on the first Monday, "to be safe". Some customers leave, others are confused. Now he cannot tell how much of the drop came from the price and how much from anything else. The small step kept the experiment readable.

## Pull one business lever at a time

Emma and Paul ran a family budget €400 short every month. Their plan had two levers: cut subscriptions and eating out by €180, then add a side job worth €300.

They started with the cuts alone, so they could see that lever work. The side job came a month later.

Two levers pulled on the same day give you one result and no idea which lever earned it.

The same rule holds in a crisis. When a number has crossed its red line, move only the levers that act on that number. Everything else waits.

## What pushing on results costs

Skip the sort and you get the meeting from the opening. The talk aims at margin, sales and the market. Nobody leaves with a task for tomorrow.

The cost is measurable. Andrew's margin gap of 6 points is €1,800 of profit missing every month: €2,700 earned against the €4,500 the goal would bring.

Leave the levers unnamed and his cash reserve reaches its two-month goal at month 16, not month 10. That is six extra months short of a safe cushion, where one breakdown costs him a full month of progress.

And there is the owner. Effort poured into things that do not budge returns nothing but fatigue.

## Where the lever sheet does not reach

The sheet treats a symptom: effort spent on things that do not obey you. It does not remove the cause behind wasted Mondays.

You now know what you can move. You do not yet know which lever goes first, with how much time and money, and what stays off limits even when it would work. Nor do you know when a number is bad enough to justify pulling anything at all.

## FAQ

### What are examples of business levers?

Prices, the menu or product range, purchasing terms and portion size. Payment terms, prepayment and reminders. Promotion channels, the wording of an offer, process steps and capacity. Each one is something you change by your own decision.

### How to focus on what you can control in a business?

Write down everything that touches your goal. Put each item into one of three bins: lever, free parameter or outside factor. Work the levers, watch the free parameters, and plan around the factors.

### What is the difference between a lever and a result?

A result is what you want, such as margin. A lever is what you do to move it, such as prices or the menu. Steer, Don't Guess calls the full set of your levers the control vector.
