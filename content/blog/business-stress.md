<strong>Three out of four small business owners live with medium or high stress.</strong>

It didn't have to be that many. Behind the number are people who worked hard and steered by feel. Some of them might have carried less if their numbers had lived on a sheet instead of in their heads.

Picture your own Thursday. The bank app says one thing. The till says another. Last month's report from the accountant says a third. You hold all three in your head, and at night they argue.

The irony: you have more figures than ever, and not one you'd bet the rent on.

The stress isn't the work. It's running a business with the instruments switched off.

The Jirgenson Model is a control system for that. It is scientifically proven to work ([Zenodo record](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)). It is new, so it could not help the owners behind these numbers. Now it can help yours.

## Business worries at night: give every number a place

<strong>Nearly half of owners lie awake at night worrying about the business.</strong>

That half could have been smaller. A worry with a place on paper doesn't need to circle at two in the morning. With a control system, some of those nights might have been ordinary sleep.

So build the place. It is a panel: one table, one source for every row, a fixed time to look.

Elena is a freelance designer. Her income swung between €800 and €2,200 a month. For a long time she believed she earned about €2,000 on average, because one month had brought in €2,200. Six months of bank statements showed €1,300. Her head had read the best month and called it normal.

Here is the tool she built, step by step, with her entries.

**1. Choose up to five parameters.** One row per goal, named with a number. Elena chose three: average income over three months, the share of her largest client, and hours per week.

**2. Give every row one source of truth.** One place the number comes from, and only one. Elena took income and the client share from her bank statements, and hours from her time records. If revenue reads one way in the accounts and another at the till, the argument over which is right eats more energy than the business.

**3. Fill in "now" from the source, not from memory.** Elena: €1,300 a month. Her best client brings in €910 of it, which is 70%. She works 50 hours a week.

**4. Write the goal and the error.** The error is the gap between now and the goal. Elena's goals: at least €3,000 a month by M14, no client above 30%, no more than 35 hours a week. M0 is today; M14 is fourteen months from now.

| Parameter | Goal | Now | Error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average income, € a month | at least 3,000 | 1,300 | 1,700 short |
| Largest client, % of income | no more than 30 | 70 | 40 points over |
| Hours a week | no more than 35 | 50 | 15 over |

**5. Draw the zones before you need them.** Three bands per row: normal, acceptable, emergency. Elena drew hers for the client share: normal up to 33%, acceptable above 33 and up to 50%, emergency above 50%. At 70%, that row is red. If your worries need an order first, [sort each one into a zone](/blog/small-business-owner-stress/).

**6. Set the rhythm.** Decide when you look, and stop looking in between. Use a calendar of short meetings: weekly, monthly, quarterly. Elena checks her book's figures every Monday.

Elena's full panel has six rows. The other three track her first online book: page visitors, the share who buy, and newsletter subscribers. Her funnel reads 1,400 visitors a month, times 1.5%, times €29: about €609.

The first panel takes about 20 minutes. It reads in a minute.

How to avoid the night shift: when a worry arrives at night, find its row. If it has no row, write it down and give it one on Monday.

## Thought about giving it all up? Read the rows first

<strong>Four out of ten owners have thought about giving it all up.</strong>

Some of those owners might have stayed if they had seen which part was failing. "It's all falling apart" is a feeling about the whole. A panel splits the whole into rows, and the odds could look different row by row.

Look at Elena's panel again. Income was low, hours were high, one client held 70%. A tired head reads that as "freelancing doesn't work". The rows say something narrower. One row is red: the client share. The other two are gaps with a deadline.

Don't judge the whole business by one bad month. A single point shows no direction; a trend needs two or three measurements.

Before you quit, measure three times.

How to avoid the quitting night: never make the big decision in the hour you feel worst. Make it on a Monday morning, with the panel open.

## Business owner stress and sleep: check at the speed things change

<strong>Six out of ten owners sleep less than before they started.</strong>

<strong>Two out of three Canadian owners say they're close to burnout.</strong>

Those numbers might have been lower too. Part of the lost sleep comes from checking everything, all the time, because nothing says when to check. A control system says when.

Match how often you look to how fast a number can move. Take the cash reserve in Andrew's coffee shop. Its yellow band is one month of expenses wide, from 1.0 to 2.0. In a bad month the reserve moves about 0.2 months. One look a month is enough.

Complaints move faster, so Andrew counts them weekly. In a cash crisis, the account balance gets a daily look.

Now put your own hours on the panel. Elena's row said 50 hours a week against a goal of 35. While she wrote her book, from M1 to M3, she allowed herself up to 55 hours. That was a decision made in advance, with an end date. By M14 her week was back to 35 hours.

Overload with an end date is a plan. Overload without one is a slide.

How to avoid the slide toward burnout: give your hours a limit, a source and the date the extra load ends. For business owner mental health, the panel is no cure. When sleep stays broken, see a doctor.

## Family business stress: one sheet two people can see

<strong>Four out of ten owners have hidden business stress from their own family.</strong>

Some of them might have shared it if they had something calmer to share than fear. A panel gives you that.

Andrew set up a shared spreadsheet that he and Sara, his senior barista, could both see. Data from the till flowed in as a weekly export. No expensive programs. A month later, the Monday briefing took fifteen minutes instead of an hour. There was nothing left to argue about. The figures showed everything.

Do the same at home. Show your partner the rows, not the mood. "Five rows. One red. Here is what I'm doing about it." A family can plan around a red row. It can't plan around silence.

How to avoid the silent dinner: give one person at home a view of the panel, and five minutes of it each week.

## What six more months of steering by feel cost

Put Elena's gaps into plain arithmetic. Her income sat €1,700 a month below the goal. Six more months with no panel leave her €10,200 short of the life she planned.

Her hours sat 15 a week above the goal. Over six months, about 26 weeks, that is roughly 390 extra hours. Call it almost ten weeks of 40 hours.

And one client still held 70% of her money. If that client walks away, seven euros in ten walk away too.

The human cost has no number. Without tools, every bit of clarity stays in your head and on napkins. In the first tense month the order is forgotten, the zones blur, and fatigue makes the decisions again.

## "I have no time for a dashboard"

You're already paying the time. You pay it at night, recomputing figures you don't trust.

The first panel takes 20 minutes and needs no software. A shared spreadsheet does the job. Without software, keep nine rows at the most. Five fit on one screen.

The owner who stays calm on the worst day doesn't worry less. They know where to look.

## Where the panel stops

The panel is one piece of the loop that runs from goal to state, error, levers, plan and forecast. On its own it treats a symptom. You stop carrying the business in your head, and the night loses its fuel.

The cause stays where it was. A red row tells you where the fire is. It doesn't say which lever to pull. And the panel shows today, not the months ahead.

Two steps close that gap.

- **A list of levers, each with a person responsible.** What you can change yourself, apart from what you only measure. Chapter 5.
- **A rolling forecast, three to six months ahead.** Where today's rows lead if nothing changes, and where they lead if the plan works. Chapter 8.

Without them, the panel calms your evenings. The numbers stay where they are.

## FAQ

### How do I reduce stress as a business owner?

Move the numbers out of your head. Build one panel with up to five rows: parameter, goal, now, error, zone. Give each row one source and a set time to check it. Not knowing where you stand is a stress you can remove in an evening.

### What causes business anxiety?

One cause is steering without instruments: three different figures for one number, no zones, and no fixed time to look. Every figure then feels urgent at once. One source per number and zones drawn in advance turn a vague alarm into a reading.

### How often should I check my business numbers?

Match the check to the speed of the number. A slow reserve needs a monthly look; complaints, a weekly one; the account in a cash crisis, a daily one. The full control system, from the panel to the forecast, comes as a book on Kindle.
