It was one of those months. The supplier raised prices by eight per cent. The coffee machine broke down. A barista quit. All in the same few days.

Andrew's first impulse was to decide something immediately. Anything. Hire a full-time replacement tonight, find a new supplier tomorrow, cut prices, raise prices. When everything lands at once, action feels like control.

It is usually the opposite. In a tangled situation, the main mistake is rarely choosing the wrong decision. It is choosing it too early.

## Business triage: judo, not boxing

A boxer hurls himself at his opponent and spends his strength on every blow. A judo master first steadies his breathing, then senses where the opponent is moving, then uses that movement. Triage in a business works the same way: calm, read, then act with the current rather than against it.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> the seven steps of entering control are used when a situation is tangled or unfamiliar: calm the noise, read the state without distortion, restore the trajectory, break down the processes, find the enclosing system, build a multi-variant forecast, then decide and accept the consequences. You can start at any step and repeat them.</div>

## A business triage template: seven steps, filled in

Here are the seven steps with what Andrew actually did in his bad month:

| Step | What to do | Andrew's bad month |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Calm the noise | Check for immediate danger to people, safety, mandatory payments or the law. If none, decide nothing tonight | No immediate danger. He arranged to meet his senior barista in the morning and went to bed |
| 2. Read the state | Pull the real figures, not opinions | Margin down from 15% to 12%: about 2.4 points from the supplier's prices, the rest from overtime. Machine repair cost about 0.1 months of reserve. Reserve: 1.56 months |
| 3. Restore the trajectory | 12 to 36 months of history: speed and direction, not one point | Purchases had been rising about 1% a month for a while. Not news, a trend that had just sharpened |
| 4. Break down the processes | What is improving, what is worsening | Purchases dearer; guest numbers up but the share of regulars falling; team unstable |
| 5. Find the enclosing system | Industry, region, regulation | Prices rising at every supplier in the region. Guests could see it all over town |
| 6. Build scenarios | Three options with numbers and dates | See the table below |
| 7. Decide and accept the consequences | One lever, then wait for its effect | Prices +5%, nothing else for now |

Copy the first two columns and fill in the third the next time a bad week arrives.

## Which problem first? Count the weeks to red

When several things are going wrong, attention tends to go to whichever problem was discussed most loudly that morning. A better rule: first anything dangerous, then anything already red in your order of goals, then the yellow ones by how soon they will turn red.

Andrew's bad month had no reds, but four yellows moving in the wrong direction. Here is how fast each was heading for its red line:

| Number | Now | Moving | Red line | Time to red |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Complaints per week | 5 | +1 a week | 8 | about 3 weeks |
| Owner's hours per week | 48 | +2 a week | 70 | about 11 weeks |
| Margin | 12% | about −1.5 points a month | 5% | about 5 months |
| Regular customers | 34% | −1 point a month | 20% | about 14 months |

The reserve, at 1.56 months, was also yellow, but it was growing, moving away from red, so it did not join the queue. The order of attention became: complaints, hours, margin, regulars. Not the order of panic, the order of the calendar.

## Three scenarios, one lever

For step 6, Andrew put three options side by side:

| Scenario | Margin | Reserve reaches 2.0 | Holds for three months |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Change nothing | 12% | month 12 | month 14 |
| 2. Price +5% | 15% | month 11 | month 13 |
| 3. Price +5% and a part-time barista | 13.5% | month 11 | month 13, with higher quality |

He chose scenario 2 and started with a single lever, so he could see its effect. Step 5 had told him something important: prices were rising across the whole region, so raising his own price meant swimming with the current, not against it. A part-time barista would add fixed costs while the reserve was still yellow; if quality slipped within two months, he would return to scenario 3.

Three weeks later he noticed, to his surprise, that he could not remember the last time he had panicked.

## What would have happened if he had decided that night

He would most likely have hired a full-time barista to plug the hole and started hunting for another supplier at the same time. Two levers at once, both expensive, and no way of telling afterwards which one had worked. That is the hidden cost of deciding fast: you lose the ability to learn from the result.

The second half of step 7 is the one most often forgotten. To make a decision is also to accept its consequences: the effect will not come at once, and an earlier error takes time to work itself out. Whoever expects instant results changes the decision at the very moment it is starting to work.

## If your hands are shaking: the Red Card

Some days a call, a letter or a piece of news narrows your attention to a single point, and the mind refuses to do sums. For those days, keep a one-page card where you can see it. Its first two steps:

1. **Check for danger.** Is there an immediate risk to people, to safety, to mandatory payments, or a legal risk? If so, protect first and alert those who can help. If not: three slow breaths, a glass of water, a minute of silence. Irreversible strategic decisions can wait until you are steady.
2. **Write down the facts.** Three lines: what happened (a fact, not a judgement); what the numbers are now (money, reserve, deadlines); what is already on fire.

When a number is genuinely red, the next moves follow a strict order, set out in [what to do first in a business crisis](/blog/business-crisis-what-to-do-first/).

## Where this does not reach

Seven steps are for a tangled situation, not for every small problem; a single yellow number usually needs only its cause found. Where there is immediate danger to people, safety or the law, protection and escalation come before any analysis. And if a business is genuinely failing because its whole market is collapsing, triage will show it, but the answer then lies outside tactics: a different place, format or goal. Legal and financial advisers belong in that conversation.

<div class="takeaways" markdown="1">
### Key takeaways

- In a bad month, check for immediate danger first; if there is none, decide nothing in the first hour.
- Read real numbers, the trend of the last months, and the wider market before choosing.
- Order your attention by time to red, not by volume of complaint.
- Build three scenarios, move one lever at a time, and give it time to work.
</div>

## Twenty minutes in hindsight

The exercise: think back to your last bad week or month and walk through the seven steps as if it were happening now. What would you have done differently? It takes about twenty minutes, and the next bad month will feel different because you will already know the route.

## FAQ

### My business is failing, what do I do first?

Check for immediate danger to people, safety, payroll or legal obligations and protect those first. If there is none, do not make irreversible decisions on the first day: get the real numbers, look at the trend, and build three scenarios before acting.

### What is business triage?

A short, ordered process for when several problems hit at once: calm the noise, read the real state, look at the trend and the wider market, build scenarios, and act on the problem that will turn red soonest, one lever at a time.

### How do I decide which business problem to solve first?

Danger first, then anything already past its red line in your order of goals, then the rest by how soon each will cross its red line at its current pace.
