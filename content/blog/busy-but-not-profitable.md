## The queue is not a number

Andrew's coffee shop on the corner has been open for two years. Customers keep coming, and every morning there is a queue at the door. Yet by the end of each month the money has somehow vanished, and he cannot remember when he last ate lunch at home. He is not lazy and not incompetent. He does not know what exactly is wrong.

That is the opening of *Steer, Don't Guess*, and it describes a state most owners recognise: busy, by every visible sign, and not profitable, by the only sign that counts. The book's answer is not a motivational one. It is a measurement.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model, the state vector</strong> is the same list of goals you already have, filled in with measured facts: not "many", "few" or "normal", but values, each with one definition, one main source and one owner of the calculation. Panic and bravado both replace facts with feelings; the state is accepted as it is.</div>

## Busy but not profitable: where the money went

When Andrew finally counts, three things are missing from his picture.

**Write-offs.** Milk past its date, pastries thrown out at closing, the free coffee for the regular who "always comes back". None of it is in the spreadsheet, because none of it was a payment. Counted, it moves his margin from the 12 to 13% he felt to 9%.

**His own hours.** He works the bar when a barista is sick and does the books at night. At zero cost in the accounts, at full cost in his life. The model puts the owner's time, health and money into the goal list on purpose, so that it cannot disappear.

**The reserve, in the wrong unit.** He knows the bank balance. He does not know how many months of expenses it covers, which is the only unit that tells him how long he has. In the book's running numbers: revenue of €30,000 a month, a margin of 9%, expenses of €27,300, a reserve of half a month. Half a month is the red zone by any sensible threshold.

| | What Andrew felt | What the numbers show |
|---|---|---|
| Margin | "about 12 to 13%" | 9% once write-offs are counted |
| Monthly profit | "a few thousand" | €2,700 |
| Reserve | "the account is fine" | 0.5 months of expenses: red |
| Owner's time | not counted | counted, and over the limit |

Nothing here required an accountant. It required one honest count from one source per number.

## Three rules for reading your state

1. **One indicator, one definition, one source, one owner.** If margin is counted two ways by two people, you do not have a margin; you have an argument. Other sources may be used to reconcile, never to replace.
2. **Ask of every number: who counted it, how, for which period.** A number without an answer to those three questions is not a fact.
3. **No source means the first task is to build one.** If you cannot say what your write-offs are, the first action is not to reduce them; it is a notebook by the bin for two weeks.

And one zone the book insists on: **undefined**. When data is missing, stale or contradictory, the parameter is neither green nor red. It is unknown, and unknown is never treated as fine.

## What changes once the state is honest

With the margin at 9% and the reserve at half a month, Andrew's plan is not "work harder". It is two levers he can move with his own hands: prices, and the write-offs themselves. In the book's model, raising the margin step by step to 15% takes the reserve to two months of expenses by month 10. With nothing changed, the same reserve is reached in month 16. Six months of difference, made of numbers he could not see while he was busy.

The five numbers an owner can check weekly, in the spirit of the book: cash reserve in months of expenses; operating margin, write-offs included; overdue receivables, the amount and the oldest date; one leading indicator for revenue (bookings, orders in the pipeline, foot traffic); and the owner's own hours. Each with a source and a red line.

The book states its limits: this is an illustration, not a financial model of a business. A real calculation includes cash flow after taxes and debt payments, a payment schedule, provisions and seasonality.

## The chapter that makes a full room add up

There is a particular kind of tiredness that comes from a business that looks successful. The queue is real, the compliments are real, and the bank balance at the end of the month is also real. Chapter 2 of *Steer, Don't Guess* is written for exactly that tiredness, and its answer is refreshingly unglamorous: stop reading the room and start reading the numbers, one source at a time.

What the chapter does brilliantly is show how little it takes. Andrew does not hire a consultant or buy software. He counts what goes in the bin for two weeks, writes his reserve in months instead of dollars, and puts his own hours on the list. Three small acts of honesty, none of which takes more than an evening, and the fog lifts: a margin of 9%, half a month of reserve, a red zone he had been living in without a name for it.

## FAQ

### Why is my business busy but not making money?

Usually because the costs that never become a payment are not counted: write-offs, free extras, the owner's own hours. Once they are, the margin is lower than it felt, and the reserve in months of expenses shows how long you have. That number, not the queue, is where a plan starts.

### How many months of cash reserve should a small business have?

The book's coffee-shop example uses zones of normal from two months of expenses, acceptable between one and two, and red below one. Your own thresholds depend on where damage becomes hard to reverse; set them in a calm moment and revise them rarely.

### What is the first number to fix?

The one in the red zone, which for most busy-but-not-profitable businesses is the reserve. The levers that act on it fastest and reversibly, prices and write-offs, come before anything that needs hiring or spending.
