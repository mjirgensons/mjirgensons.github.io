Customers queue at the door every morning. By the end of the month the money's gone. Where did it go?

Andrew asked himself that at two in the morning, at his kitchen table, going over the same numbers for the third time. His coffee shop had been open two years. He couldn't say what was wrong. He only knew something was.

Picture your own version. Sales feel good. The bank app says something else. A supplier calls, a barista asks for more hours, and you answer both from a feeling about the account.

You know the price of every pastry on the counter. You don't know how many weeks of rent sit in the bank.

That isn't a cash problem yet. It's a reading problem.

Cash flow problems start long before the account runs dry. They start the day nobody can say where the money stands. The Jirgenson Model answers that pain with a control system, and its second step is an honest reading of where you are. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What cash flow management means

Cash flow management is the habit of tracking the money that comes in and goes out, and when, so the account never surprises you.

The usual advice stops at "watch your cash". It doesn't say what to watch, from which source, or how to know the figure is true.

The model starts one step before any forecast. First you write down where you stand today. Row by row. As a number.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> the reading of where you stand is the state vector. It holds the measured value of each goal, from one named source. It mirrors your goal list row for row. An instrument shows; it does not scold.</div>

## Cash flow problems start with three lying numbers

Numbers let you down in three ways.

1. **Memory.** You remember the loud days and forget the quiet ones. "Good on the whole" almost always looks better than it was.
2. **Rounding.** "About twenty" is easier to say than "nine and a half". So that's the figure that survives.
3. **What nobody writes down.** A cost that never enters a record doesn't exist for the report. It still exists for the money.

Andrew met all three in one evening. He was sure his margin held at around twenty per cent. The accounts said 9%. The gap was waste: sour milk and pastries thrown out at the end of each shift. He had never counted them in his cost of goods.

That same week he'd planned to hire a second barista and launch an advertising campaign. Both rested on a margin that didn't exist. Within a few months the money would have run out, and nobody would have known why.

That's what a cash crunch looks like from the inside. Not one bad day. A good feeling, built on a number nobody checked.

## Build your cash reading in one evening

You need one spreadsheet tab, the bank statement and last month's accounts. Andrew needed two hours. Here are six steps, each with his entry next to it.

### 1. Write the cash rows

List the goals that touch money, in the order you chose for them. Give each row three columns: parameter, where to get it, value.

Andrew wrote two money rows: cash reserve and margin. If you invoice clients, add a third: the customer payment period.

### 2. Give every row one source

Each row gets one definition, one primary source and one person who calculates it. Other sources serve only as a cross-check.

| Row | Definition | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Cash reserve | Bank balance in months of expenses | Bank statement |
| Margin | Operating profit as a % of revenue, waste included | Accounting system fed by the till export |
| Payment period (if you invoice) | Days from invoice to money in the account | Invoice list and bank statement |

Where there is no source, write the date by which you'll have one. A line that reads "source undefined" is already a result. You've found a hole in your management.

### 3. Ask three questions of every figure

Who counted it? How, exactly? Over what period? A figure with no answer to all three isn't a number. It's an impression.

Andrew's "twenty" failed the second question. Nobody had counted the write-offs. A receipt in the till beats anyone's memory of the receipts.

### 4. Convert cash into months

A balance in euros tells you little on its own. Divide it by one month of expenses.

Andrew's revenue is €30,000 and his margin 9%, so his expenses run at €27,300 a month. The account held €13,650. That's 13,650 / 27,300 = 0.5 months.

Half a month. With two months in the bank, a broken coffee machine is a nuisance. With two weeks, it's an emergency.

### 5. Write down the instrument's error

No figure is exact. The question is whether its error is small enough for the goal.

Andrew knew his balance to within €500, because payment dates shift. That's about 0.02 months, small enough to steer by. His old margin was off by 11 points. That gauge couldn't tell a calm month from an emergency.

### 6. Turn the gap into money

Write the goal next to the reading, then the distance in euros.

| Line | Andrew's entry |
|---|---|
| Reserve now | 0.5 months, €13,650 |
| Goal | 2.0 months, €54,600 |
| Shortfall | €40,950 |
| One month at a 9% margin adds | €2,700, about 0.10 months |

That's the whole tool. Rows, sources, three questions, months, error, gap. Copy it tonight.

## 13 week cash flow: where it fits

A 13 week cash flow is a table of the cash you expect in and out, week by week, for the next quarter. It's a well-known tool, and a good one.

It has one weak spot: the first row. Every week in the table starts from the week before it. The first week starts from today's balance. If that opening figure is an impression, the other twelve weeks inherit the error.

The model turns the 13 week view into a control step with two rules. Each line gets one named source, like the rows above. And the opening balance comes from the bank statement, measured tonight, never from memory.

Once the reading is true, you can draw where it goes. One line if nothing changes, one if the plan works. That next step has its own walkthrough: [how to forecast cash flow](/blog/forecast-cash-flow/).

## Late payments: a result, not a lever

"Get paid faster" sounds like an action. It isn't. The payment period is a result, and you can't order a result to shrink.

What you can move are the levers behind it: payment terms, prepayment and reminders. Write those three next to the payment row. Then the Monday talk about late payments stops being ten minutes about the weather.

The same split works for every cash row. Margin is a result; prices, menu, purchasing and portion size are its levers. The reserve is a result too; the margin feeds it.

So here are the only cash flow tips that survive a bad week. Measure first. Name the lever. Then pull it.

## The cost of six more months of guessing

Leave the reading blank, and the numbers keep moving without you.

At Andrew's real 9% margin, the reserve grows by about 0.10 months each month. If nothing changes, it stays under one month, the red line, until month six. It reaches the goal of two months at month 16.

With a plan that works, the reserve clears the red line at month four. It reaches two months at month 10.

That's six extra months one repair away from trouble. At 9%, a breakdown worth 0.1 months of expenses costs a full month of progress. At 15%, it costs about half.

And the false twenty per cent would have cost more. A new hire and a campaign, paid from a reserve of half a month.

The human cost is plainer. The owner opens the bank app before he opens the shop.

## Two enemies of an honest reading

This step has two enemies, and they look like opposites. Panic says all is lost. Bravado says we'll pull through. Both put a feeling where a fact should be.

The panicker sees a catastrophe where there's only a weak row. The brave face doesn't see the row at all.

Andrew's first impulse at the 9 was to close the spreadsheet and go and make coffee. He didn't. He told himself this was an instrument reading, not a verdict. For the first time in two years, he saw the whole shop.

"I have no time for this." The first version takes one evening, on paper. A rough table today beats a perfect system in six months.

## What the reading does not fix

Be clear about what you built. The reading tells you where you stand. It treats the symptom: you stop steering by feel.

The cause of the surprise is still in place. You can see 0.5 months, but you don't know where normal ends and an emergency begins. When the reserve and the margin both slip in one week, you don't know which to save first. And the reading shows today. It doesn't show month six.

Two steps close that gap:

- **Zone thresholds and the red protocol.** Where each row turns yellow or red. The order of action when several rows slip at once. Chapter 4.
- **Two forecasts, then control by forecast.** "If nothing changes" against "if the plan works", in numbers and dates. You act before the damage lands. Chapters 8 and 9.

Without them, you know where you are. You don't yet know what to do on the worst Monday.

The reading is one stage of a closed loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. Each stage needs its own tool.

## FAQ

### What is cash flow management?

Tracking the money that comes in and goes out, and its timing, so you see a shortfall before it arrives. In the Jirgenson Model it starts with an honest reading: cash in months of expenses, from one named source.

### How do I fix cash flow problems in a small business?

Measure first. Take the reserve from the bank statement, count the margin with waste included, and turn the gap into euros. Then pull the levers you control: prices, menu, purchasing, payment terms, prepayment and reminders.

### What is a 13 week cash flow?

A week-by-week table of expected cash in and out for the next quarter. It works only when its opening balance and every line come from a named source. The method that turns it into zones and forecasts is in *Steer, Don't Guess*.
