Elena never fired her biggest client.

She earns almost the same from them today as she did a year earlier. And yet that client's share of her income fell from 70% to 30%.

How? She stopped treating the client as the problem. The problem was everything else.

Picture your own version. One client pays most of your bills. Their email lands, and your stomach drops before you open it. You say yes to the rush job, the extra round, the weekend. You tell yourself it is loyalty.

It is not loyalty. It is client concentration, and it decides for you.

## Client concentration in one number

Elena is a freelance designer. Before she set any goal, she measured. She did not trust the feeling of a "good month" after a big order. She took six months of bank statements.

Here is what they showed:

| What she measured | Number |
|---|---|
| Monthly income | swinging between €800 and €2,200 |
| Average income | €1,300 a month |
| Best client, average | €910 a month |
| Best client's share | 70% |
| Working hours | 50 a week |

That one share, 70%, is the easiest number to know as a feeling and never write down. A feeling cannot be steered.

Now do the sum Elena's statements invite. If that one client leaves, €910 of €1,300 leaves with them. What remains is €390 a month.

That is not a quiet risk in a spreadsheet. That is rent.

## Why "find more clients" fails

The usual answer to client dependency is a wish: more clients, more visibility, more posts. Elena's first list of actions looked the same: more social media, increase sales, be more visible.

Every item sounded businesslike. Not one could be done tomorrow.

The wish fails for a plain reason. It has no number, no deadline and no order. So the first rush job from the big client beats it every time.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> a goal is a row in an ordered list. It holds a measured parameter, a target, a deadline, a current value and a line where normal ends. The order says which goal you give up first when they collide.</div>

## Write the largest client share as a goal

A goal becomes real once six fields are filled in. Here is Elena's client share goal, field by field:

| Field | Elena's entry |
|---|---|
| Parameter | Largest client's share of income |
| Definition and source | Best client's income divided by total income, from bank statements |
| Target and deadline | No more than 30% by month 14 |
| Now | 70% (€910 of €1,300) |
| Where normal ends | Up to and including 33% |
| Owner and rhythm | Elena, at each month's close |

The rhythm row is our illustration; the other entries are her numbers. Measure the share over several months, never one. A single fat invoice makes any month look dangerous or safe.

## Put the goals in order

Client share was not Elena's only goal. She wrote three, and then she ranked them:

1. **Average income:** at least €3,000 a month over three months, by month 14.
2. **Largest client's share:** no more than 30%, by month 14.
3. **Hours:** no more than 35 a week, except during the three months spent on her book.

The test behind the order is short. If things got bad, which goal would you give up first? Elena gives up time first. She defends income to the last. The full method is in [the refusal order](/blog/set-priorities-refusal-order/).

Here the order works as a permission, not a ban. From month 1 to month 3, while she writes her book, Elena may work up to 55 hours a week. Not a lapse. A decision made in advance, with an end date.

That matters with a big client. When the rush job arrives in month 2, she already knows the answer. Hours bend. Income holds. The share goal stays on the page.

## Levers, not wishes

Elena then asked one question of every action: what exactly do I do with my own hands? Her levers for income:

- the price of her service packages;
- letters to former clients offering a repeat order;
- two new channels for finding clients;
- her own ebook, with its sales page.

She also wrote what she would not do, even if it saved the goal. No emails to people who had not subscribed. No undercutting on price.

Look at what is missing from that list. Nothing says "drop the big client".

## The arithmetic that cuts dependency

Here is Elena's income at month 14:

| Source | Monthly income |
|---|---|
| Main client | €900 |
| Service packages | €1,200 |
| Repeat orders | €300 |
| Ebook | about €600 (€609 before commissions) |
| **Total** | **€3,000** |

The main client went from €910 to €900. Almost nothing changed there. The total grew around them, and the share fell to 30%. Her hours came back to 35.

That is the whole trick of client concentration. You rarely fix it by subtraction. You fix it by building a floor under the big client, so their next email is a request, not a verdict.

Two months in, Elena saw that packages sold better than one-off jobs. She rebuilt the plan around them. A written goal made that turn easy: the target stayed, the route changed.

## What this goal does not fix

Be clear about what you now have. The six fields tell you where you stand. The order tells you what bends first. That treats the symptom: you stop feeling your way around one client.

The cause still sits there. Two questions remain open.

First, when does the share stop being yellow and turn red, and what do you do then? Worse: what if a second number turns red the same week? Picture Elena's account frozen by the tax office while the client share is already red. Which to save first follows a fixed protocol, and the zone lines decide when it starts. That is Chapter 4.

Second, how do you know the new income will arrive in time? Elena did not guess her ebook's sales. She sent a trial stream of visitors to its page, counted the buyers, and checked the forecast against the facts every week. How to build that forecast is Chapters 8 and 9.

Without these, your goal is a good map with no weather report.

## FAQ

### What is client concentration?

It is the share of your income that comes from your largest client. Divide that client's income by your total income, using several months of bank statements, not a single month.

### What is a safe largest client share for a freelancer?

There is no single safe number for everyone. Elena set her goal at no more than 30%, with normal ending at 33%. Pick a line in a calm hour, write it down with a deadline, and check it every month.

### How do I reduce client dependency without losing my biggest client?

Grow the rest of your income instead of cutting the big client. Raise package prices, ask former clients for repeat work, open new channels or build a product. Elena's main client stayed near €900 while her total rose to €3,000.
