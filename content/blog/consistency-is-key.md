Monday, six in the morning, you make the promise again. This week, every day, no excuses.

By Thursday the promise is gone. By Sunday night it comes back as guilt. You lie awake and count what slipped: the sales calls, the side project, the evening you swore to keep free for the kids. Your partner asks what's wrong. You say "nothing, long week".

Six out of ten owners sleep less than before they started.

Here's the strange part. You can work. You've proved it a hundred times, on the worst weeks, with a fever. What you don't have is a system that keeps going when you're empty. So the whole week rides on willpower. And willpower runs out by Thursday.

"Consistency is key" is true. It is also the advice that never says how. You get the slogan on a mug, and you still miss Thursday.

The Jirgenson Model gives the answer in plain terms: consistency is not a mood you hold. It is a rule you run. A slot in the calendar, one number that moves first, and a check line with dates. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## Why consistency is key, and why willpower can't carry it

Willpower behaves like a tank with no gauge. You start the week full. A late client drains some. A sick child drains more. A bad review at nine at night drains the rest. On Thursday the plan stops, and you blame yourself, not the tank.

The fix is not a bigger tank. It is a different engine.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> consistency means the same rule run the same way every week, good week or bad. The rule lives on one page. It names your levers, your time and money, and what you will never do. It names the number that shows the plan works before the deadline, and who checks it when. Your mood is not on the page.</div>

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, tested this on his croissant order, with two weeks of daily sales. "A hundred every day" missed by 100 croissants over the second week. "As many as I sold yesterday" missed by 130. The fastest rule chased every random jump and did worse than the fixed plan. "As many as on the same day last week" missed by 76. That rule knew the pattern. The full test is in [reactive vs proactive management](/blog/reactive-vs-proactive-management/).

Effort driven by mood is the "as yesterday" rule. A great Monday, so you double the plan. A bad Tuesday, so you drop it. You chase noise, and noise wins.

Chasing yesterday's mood does worse than a dumb fixed plan.

## How to stay consistent: one page, five answers

The tool is the plan for one goal, five questions on one page. Each answer below sits next to Elena's. She is a freelance designer who works fifty hours a week for clients and wants to write and launch a book. Her goal: an income of €3,000 a month by M14, the fourteenth month. That is the hardest kind of consistency: a big project on top of a full week.

1. **Levers, in order.** What will you do with your own hands? Elena: write the manuscript, build the book's sales page, collect a mailing list, set the price. Each one is a thing she can start tomorrow.
2. **Resources, booked in the calendar.** How many hours, how much money? Elena: about 120 hours over three months, roughly ten a week. Five come on top of her usual fifty, within her own cap of 55. Five come from turning down small orders. Plus €200 for the payment service and the mailing tool. The hours go into fixed slots. A slot doesn't ask how you feel.
3. **Constraints.** What will you not do, even if it would be faster? Elena: never email people who have not subscribed; never undercut on price. Her cap of 55 hours is a constraint too. It protects her evenings.
4. **Leading indicator.** Which number tells you it's working before the deadline? Elena: 350 visitors a week to the book's page, and the share who buy. It moves weeks before her income does.
5. **Deadline and owner.** Who checks, and when? Elena herself. Launch at M4, first check at M5, the income target at M14.

Write it on one sheet. Set aside 15 minutes. If one answer stays blank, you don't have a plan yet. You have a wish, and the blank line is your next task. The longer version of this page, with Andrew's margin plan, is the [one page business plan](/blog/one-page-business-plan/).

## The check line: what to do on a bad week

A plan needs a line to compare against. Draw it by date, then read it on a fixed day.

Alex is a teenager saving for a bicycle: €300 by 1 September. He had €40 on 1 May. His line: €105 on 1 June, €170 on 1 July, €235 on 1 August, €300 on 1 September. His leading indicator is the number of odd jobs lined up for the coming week. If Friday shows no jobs booked, Sunday's count will be short, and he knows it two days early.

On 1 June he finds €90 instead of €105.

The usual reaction is a verdict: "I failed, I'll start again next month." The model's reaction is arithmetic. He saves €50 a month, so €90 plus three months makes €240. The scooter sale adds €60. Total: exactly €300. The forecast holds, with nothing to spare. That is a signal to act now: line up one more job this week. No guilt, one lever. His whole plan is in [how to save for a goal](/blog/save-for-a-goal/).

An owner works the same way. Andrew's leading indicator for his margin plan is the average margin per receipt, every week. He does not wait six months to learn if the plan worked. If you need help choosing yours, read [what is a leading indicator](/blog/what-is-a-leading-indicator/).

## Finding focus when every plan wants Tuesday

Lack of focus often hides a clash between plans. You have three good plans, and each one wants the same Tuesday evening.

Andrew found one on his own pages. He planned to raise prices. He also planned discount cards. Sara, who works with him, asked: "Don't those two contradict each other?" They did. He added a rule: card discounts start only once the margin reaches 14%.

So put the hours of all your plans into one table. Plans that look fine in pairs can still claim the same hours and the same money. Then decide which goal comes first when they collide. The [refusal test](/blog/set-priorities-refusal-order/) sets that order on a calm day.

Focus at work then stops being a fight. You know what this hour is for. Everything else waits for its own slot.

## What six more months of on-and-off effort cost

Money first. Andrew's margin stood at 9% against a goal of 15%. On revenue of €30,000 a month, that gap costs €1,800 of profit every month: €2,700 instead of €4,500. Six months of "I'll get to it" is €10,800.

The cash reserve pays too. If nothing changes, his reserve reaches its goal at M16 instead of M10. A breakdown or surprise bill worth a tenth of a month of expenses pushes the goal back by about a month at a 9% margin.

Then the cost you can't see in a report. Without a weekly look, Andrew would have dropped a menu item that, it turned out, brought his regulars in. Observation every Monday caught that error in the second month rather than the sixth.

And at home, your family gets the version of you that is empty by Thursday and guilty by Sunday.

A plan is not a cure for exhaustion, and this post does not diagnose anything. Signs of burnout and burnout from work are questions for a doctor. If the sleepless nights or the low mood last longer than a bad week, see a doctor. If it feels urgent, call a crisis line. The model treats the chaos in the business, not an illness. For the owner's own hours as a number on the panel, see [business owner burnout](/blog/business-owner-burnout/).

## Where the page stops

"I need a plan, but I have no time to plan." The page takes 15 minutes. You already spend longer than that every Sunday night on guilt.

The page gives you rhythm. It treats a symptom: the missed Thursday. The cause runs deeper. You don't know when a reading is bad enough to drop everything else. And you don't know if your check line is honest at all.

Two steps of the system fix that, and the page fails without them. The zone thresholds and the red protocol, in Chapter 4, tell you when a number turns red. They also say what to do when two turn red in the same week. The backward move, in Chapter 15, walks from the deadline back to today and shows what must be true on each date. Without zones, every short week feels like a disaster. Without the backward move, the line may promise what the calendar can't hold.

The page is one part of a loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. Consistency is what you see from outside when that loop runs every week. The rules of the loop are public in the [model's GitHub repository](https://github.com/mjirgensons/jirgenson-model), and short videos walk through them on the [Steer, Don't Guess YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@SteerDontGuess).

### Key takeaways

- Willpower runs out by Thursday. A written rule doesn't.
- Answer five questions on one page: levers, resources, constraints, leading indicator, deadline and owner.

- Put all your plans' hours in one table, so two plans never fight over the same evening.

## FAQ

### How do you stay consistent when motivation is gone?

Stop relying on motivation. Write one page: the levers, the hours booked in fixed calendar slots, what you will not do, one leading indicator and a check date. Then read the number on a fixed day. The rule runs the week, so a bad mood can't cancel it.

### Is losing consistency a sign of burnout from work?

It can have many causes, and only a doctor can say what is happening to your health. If low mood, exhaustion or sleepless nights last longer than a bad week, see a doctor or call a crisis line. In the business, a missing system is a common cause you can fix yourself: one page, one number, one weekly check.

### How do I take control when I need a plan but have no time?

Start with one goal, not all of them. Answer the five questions of a plan in 15 minutes and book the hours in your calendar. Then pick the one number that moves first and look at it every week. The full method, with zones and the backward move, is in Steer, Don't Guess.
