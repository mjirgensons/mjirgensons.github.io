The thermostat on your wall runs a tighter loop than your business does.

Every few minutes it compares the room with the dial. If the gap opens, it switches the heat on, long before anyone reaches for a jumper. Now think of your margin. You learn it weeks after the month closes. You discuss it at the next meeting. Something changes, maybe, a week after that.

Here is the irony. The heating in the back room is run by feedback. The business that pays for the heating is run by hope.

Engineers have a name for the cure: control theory. Its central idea fits on a napkin. The hard part is translating it for a shop with a queue, a supplier and a tired owner. This page does that, line by line.

## What control theory is, in one paragraph

Control theory is the branch of engineering that keeps a room warm, a car at a set speed and an aircraft on course. Its core claim: a system reaches its target by measuring the gap and correcting it, over and over. Not by following a perfect plan. The circuit of target, measurement, gap and correction is a feedback loop. When the loop is closed, the system notices its own drift. When it is open, nobody notices until the drift becomes a crash.

A business is not a thermostat. People react to your decisions. Data arrive late and sometimes wrong. So treat the engineering terms as a frame, not a promise that your shop will behave like a boiler.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> control means keeping the gap between your goals and your measured state inside limits you set in advance. You close it with levers you can move yourself, in the order your priorities dictate, and you check it on a fixed rhythm.</div>

## Control theory for business: the translation table

Every engineering term has a plain business twin. The right-hand column is Andrew's coffee shop: €30,000 of revenue a month, a 9% margin, and a cash reserve of half a month of expenses.

| Engineering term | Business twin | Andrew's entry |
|---|---|---|
| Setpoint | Goals, in a fixed order, nine at most | Cash reserve: 2.0 months of expenses |
| Sensor | The measured state, one source per number | Bank statement: 0.5 months |
| Sensor error | How far the figure can be off | About €500, because payment dates shift |
| Error signal | The gap, read through zones set in advance | Minus 1.5 months: red |
| Actuator | Levers: what you can change yourself | Prices, menu, purchasing, portion size |
| Disturbance | Environmental factors | Rain, supplier prices, a broken machine |
| Phase lag | Delay from event to effect | About 45 days for a menu or price change |
| Stability margin | The shock you can absorb | The reserve itself |
| Feedforward | Acting on a forecast, not on a past result | Two forecasts: month 10 or month 16 |

Column two alone is a management system in plain words.

## Four engineering ideas that change how you run a shop

**1. In the green zone, do nothing.** A good controller does not twitch while the reading is inside tolerance. Fixing what works is the most common sin in management. It costs money and attention and moves nothing.

**2. Faster is not better.** Shorten the reaction time without a rule for which gaps deserve a response, and you start correcting noise. Andrew tested ordering rules on two weeks of pastry sales. "Order what sold yesterday", the fastest rule, missed by 130 pieces. A fixed hundred a day missed by 100. The rule that knew the weekly pattern missed by 76.

**3. Build the margin before the shock.** A broken coffee machine is a nuisance for a shop with two months of reserve. For a shop with two weeks, it is an emergency. Engineers call the cushion a stability margin. Owners call it the money that lets them sleep.

**4. Act on the cause, not only on the result.** A heater can switch on when the outside temperature drops, before the room cools. In a business, that means a forecast: you see the gap coming and move before it arrives.

## Where the engineer needs a human: priorities

A thermostat has one setpoint. You have five, and they pull against each other. Cash wants fewer hours on the rota. Quality wants more. No formula settles that for you.

The model answers it with one question, asked in a calm moment. If circumstances forced you to give up your goals one at a time, which would go first? Which would you defend to the end? The last one standing is number one. Andrew put his own hours last and the cash reserve first. That ranking is the model's answer to how to set priorities in business and life. No engineer can design it for you.

When two numbers turn red at once, the order decides. Not the louder phone call.

## The loop check: map one number in fifteen minutes

Here is the tool. Pick the number that worries you most. Fill in eight lines. Andrew's cash reserve is filled in beside each one.

1. **Setpoint.** The target, with unit and deadline. *2.0 months of expenses by month 12.*
2. **Sensor.** One source, named. *The bank statement.*
3. **Sensor error.** How far can the figure be off? *About €500, roughly 0.02 months.*
4. **Error now.** Target minus today. *Minus 1.5 months, or €40,950.*
5. **Actuator.** One lever you can move yourself this week. *Margin, through menu, prices, purchasing and portions.*
6. **Disturbances.** What pushes the number off course? *Repairs, supplier price rises, a bad season.*
7. **Delay.** Days from noticing a change to seeing the effect of your response. *About 45: a week to notice, a day to decide, a week to act, a month for guests and the books to respond.*
8. **Margin.** How big a shock could the number take today? *Half a month of expenses. One large repair.*

An empty line is a break in the loop. Repair that one first. In Andrew's case, line 3 nearly broke everything: his margin figure had been off by eleven points until he counted the waste.

Line 7 has a second use. Never judge a lever before one full turn of the loop. Andrew set his first check at month 2, not month 1, because a 45-day loop has not finished a single turn by then.

## What an open loop costs, month by month

An open loop does not fail on the day you skip a check. It fails by the calendar.

Andrew's reserve grows by about a tenth of a month for every month at 9%. Change nothing, and it reaches two months at month 16. With the margin plan working, at month 10. Six extra months with almost no cushion. At 9%, one unplanned repair costing a tenth of a month's expenses pushes the goal back a full month. At 15%, half a month.

And for those six months, the owner reads every call from the bank as a verdict.

The map shows your loop and its broken line. It treats the symptom: you stop steering blind.

The cause of most bad decisions is still in place. You can read the gap, but you have not decided where yellow turns red, or what to do when three numbers cross at once. And your loop still looks backwards: it reacts to last month, not to the next six.

Two steps close that gap.

- **Zones, thresholds and the red protocol.** Where each red line sits and why, and the fixed order of action when several readings cross it. Chapter 4.
- **Two forecasts and control by forecast.** "If nothing changes" against "if the plan works", each with numbers, a range and a date. You move before the gap arrives. Chapters 8 and 9.

Without them, you have a sensor and a dial. You do not yet have a controller.

## FAQ

### How is control theory used in business?

As a feedback loop. Set ordered goals, measure the real state, and read the gap through zones set in advance. Correct it with levers you control, on a fixed rhythm, and act on a forecast where you can.

### What is feedback control management?

Managing by measured gaps, not by plans alone. You compare where you are with where you meant to be, and correct the difference regularly. Its weak spot is delay, which is why the model adds forecasts.

### Do I need maths to apply control theory to a small business?

No. A goal table, one source per number, zones and a weekly check run the whole loop on paper. The formulas are optional.
