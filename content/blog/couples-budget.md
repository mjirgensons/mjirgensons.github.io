You're not fighting about money. You're fighting about two different numbers.

One of you says "we're fine". The other says "we're sinking". Both of you are guessing, and both of you are sure.

Here is the irony. You can split a dinner bill to the cent in ten seconds. You cannot agree what the two of you spent last month.

So the talk starts with a bank alert and ends with "you always". Nobody lies. Each of you carries a different picture in your head, and pictures can't be checked.

The fix is not more discipline. It is one page you both read. The answer to this pain comes from the Jirgenson Model: the same control loop that runs a coffee shop runs a household. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## Why money fights start

A money fight has three causes. None of them is your partner.

First, there is no shared fact. One of you counts from memory, the other from the banking app on a good day.

Second, there is no shared order. You both want the reserve, the holiday and the Friday dinners. Nobody has said which goes first when there isn't enough for all three.

Third, there are no levers on paper. "Spend less" is a mood, not a move.

The second cause is the one nobody sees. Put the same goals in two different orders and you get two different decisions in a crisis. That is the whole fight, in one line. Two people steer the same boat towards two ports.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> a couples budget runs on the same loop as a business. A goal you both chose. A state from one source. The gap between the two. Levers you can pull yourselves. Only the units change.</div>

## The four-line couples budget

Every task with a goal has the same frame. What you want. What you have. The gap. What you can move. A coffee shop, an exam and a family budget are built this way. Only the contents change.

So the couples budget is not a spreadsheet of forty categories. It is four lines and a weekly check. Here they are, filled in with Emma and Paul, the household in [the book](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0HPCJCWKF).

### 1. Write one shared goal

One number, one deadline, agreed by both. Not "save more".

Emma and Paul's goal: a reserve of at least three months of expenses.

### 2. Take the state from one source

Pick one source you can both open. Three months of bank statements work. Memory doesn't.

Emma and Paul's state: income €4,000 a month, expenses €4,400, savings €2,000. Their expenses rose by 10% in one quarter. Their income stayed flat.

### 3. Measure the gap in one unit

Use a unit that ends the debate. [Months of expenses](/blog/cash-reserve-months-of-expenses/) work well: savings divided by monthly expenses.

Emma and Paul: 2,000 / 4,400, about 0.45 months, against a goal of three. In euros, three months is €13,200. They hold €2,000. The gap is €11,200. The zone is red.

Read that line twice. "We're fine" and "we're sinking" can't both survive it.

### 4. List only levers you control

A [lever](/blog/levers-of-control/) is a move the two of you can make yourselves. Rent, energy prices and the bank's rates are not levers. You can watch them. You can't pull them.

Emma and Paul found two: cut subscriptions and eating out by €180, and a side job bringing in €300 a month. The cut came first, alone, so its effect showed on the next statement. The side job followed a month after that.

### 5. Check the page together, once a week

Same page, same source, same day. Look at the line before anyone gives an opinion.

| Line | What to write | Emma and Paul |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | One number and a deadline | Reserve of at least 3 months of expenses |
| State | From one source you both open | €4,000 in, €4,400 out, €2,000 saved (bank statements) |
| Gap | Goal minus state, in one unit | 0.45 months against 3; €11,200 short; red |
| Levers | Moves you control | Cut €180 first; side job of €300 a month later |
| Check | Who looks, and how often | Both, once a week, the same statement |

With both levers, the monthly result turns from €400 short to €80 ahead. The fight about facts is over. The numbers are on the page.

## Family goals in your refusal order

The fourth line tells you what you can move. It doesn't tell you what to protect first. For that, you need your family goals in order.

Use [the refusal test](/blog/set-priorities-refusal-order/). Imagine circumstances force you to give up your goals one at a time. Which goes first? Which goes second? Which is the last one standing? That last one is goal number one.

Here is the couples version. It takes fifteen minutes.

1. Each of you writes three family goals on a separate sheet, with today's value next to each.
2. Each of you puts them in refusal order. Alone. No peeking.
3. Swap the sheets.

As an illustration: one sheet says reserve, holiday, Friday dinners. The other says Friday dinners, reserve, holiday. Now you know why last month's talk went the way it did. You were never arguing about the restaurant bill. You were arguing about the order.

4. Agree one order and write it on the shared page, while nobody is angry.

The order is yours to choose. No adviser can choose it for you, because it says what matters to the two of you. But once it's on paper, the next tense evening has a referee.

## What six more months of guessing cost

Leave the page blank and the arithmetic runs anyway.

At €400 short a month, Emma and Paul's €2,000 lasts five months. Less, if the rise in expenses goes on. Six more months of the same is €2,400 out the door. That is more than the account holds.

So the reserve does not stay at 0.45 months. It goes to zero, and the gap to the goal grows to the full €13,200.

Every one of those month-ends holds the same evening. A bank alert, a closed door, two people who love each other keeping score.

## What the shared page does not fix

Be honest about what you built. The page covers four parts of [the loop](https://github.com/mjirgensons/jirgenson-model): goal, state, gap and levers. The plan and the forecast are still missing. So the page treats the symptom. You stop fighting about facts, because the facts sit in one place.

The cause sits one level down. You still have no agreed point where yellow becomes red for your family, so one of you will panic early and the other late. And you can't see where the money stands in six months under each option. So the next big decision, the car, the move or the new job, will still be a guess.

Two steps close that gap:

- **Zone thresholds and the red protocol.** [The lines you agree in a calm hour](/blog/kpi-zones/), and the order of action when two numbers turn red in the same month. Chapter 4.
- **Two forecasts and who is at the wheel.** [The do-nothing line next to the plan line](/blog/forecast-cash-flow/), in numbers and dates, and which of you looks ahead for which area. Chapter 8.

Without them, the page tells you where you stand. It doesn't yet tell you what to do on the worst month.

What to cut first when the account is already in deficit is in [household budget in deficit](/blog/household-budget-deficit/).

## FAQ

### How do couples make a budget together?

Agree one goal with a number and a deadline. Take the state from one source you both can open, such as three months of bank statements. Write the gap in one unit, list only levers you control, and check the page together once a week.

### How do you stop money fights with your partner?

Move the facts out of your heads and onto one page first. Then each of you ranks your shared goals in refusal order, alone, and you compare the lists. The difference in order is the argument. The numbers only carry it.

### How do you set family goals?

Write three goals, each with today's value. Ask which you would give up first if you had to, and which would be the last one left. That last one is goal number one. The whole loop behind it, with three levels of examples, is in Steer, Don't Guess.
