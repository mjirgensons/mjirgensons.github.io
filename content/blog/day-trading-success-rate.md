<strong>Of every hundred day traders who stuck with it, ninety-seven lost money.</strong>

It's 9:25 in the morning. Coffee, two screens, the balance in the corner. Last month you told yourself this would be the month.

You can name your entry price to the cent. Can you name the goal you would give up first if the balance kept falling?

Here is the irony. A trader watches prices by the second. The order of what matters in their own life sits in their head, untested, until the worst afternoon tests it for him.

## Day trading success rate: the plan starts before the chart

Day trading statistics give you the odds. They can't give you an order. That part is yours, and it takes ten minutes.

Write down everything you want from the next year: money, time, family, health, the trading itself. Then run the refusal test. Suppose circumstances force you to give up your goals one at a time. Which goes first? Which second? Which is the last one left?

The last one left is your goal number one. The one you drop first gets the last number. From that moment, fatigue and the last losing trade no longer get a vote.

How to avoid deciding in the middle of a storm: choose your port before you put to sea. A captain who loves two ports dashes back and forth, burns fuel and arrives nowhere.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> the goal vector is a short list of measurable goals in a fixed order. The refusal test sets the order: what you would give up first gets the last number. Without that order, a crisis decides for you.</div>

## A trading plan template that starts with the refusal test

This is the tool. One sheet, ten to twenty minutes. Six steps, each with a real entry beside it.

The entries come from Andrew, who runs a coffee shop. His goals are not yours. His method fits any list, a trading account included.

### 1. Write the constraints first

Constraints are not goals. They are the law, safety, contracts and the most a person can carry. You never give them up in turn. They decide which moves are allowed at all.

A coffee shop's entries: taxes paid on time. No saving on equipment maintenance that raises the risk of a breakdown tomorrow.

Your row, as an illustration: the rent and the family budget stay outside the trading account.

### 2. List your goals, nine at most

Work through six areas: finance, clients, sales, processes and quality, people, and you, the owner. That last area holds your own time, health and money. People forget it most.

Keep nine goals at most. For a person, ten priorities are no longer priorities. They are a list.

Andrew's entry: five goals. Cash reserve, margin, regular customers, quality, his own hours.

### 3. Run the refusal test

Cross the goals out one at a time, as if things turned bad. Number them in reverse.

Andrew put his own time in last place, and it surprised him. He would defend money to the last: without a reserve, the shop would close. More on the test itself is in [the refusal test for setting priorities](/blog/set-priorities-refusal-order/).

| No. | Goal | Value | Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cash reserve | at least 2.0 months of expenses | M12 |
| 2 | Margin | 15% | M6 |
| 3 | Regular customers | 40% of receipts | M12 |
| 4 | Quality | no more than 2 complaints a week | M3 |
| 5 | Owner's time | no more than 40 hours a week | M12 |

### 4. Fill the six fields of the top goals

A goal becomes real when six fields are filled in. Here is Andrew's margin row.

| Field | Andrew's entry |
|---|---|
| Parameter | Margin |
| Definition and source | Operating profit as a % of revenue, from the accounts fed by the till |
| Target and deadline | 15% by month 6 |
| Now | 9% |
| Permissible deviation | Once reached, never below 14% |
| Owner and rhythm | Andrew, every week |

### 5. Cross out the methods

A goal says what you want. A method says how. "Trade every morning" is a method, so it doesn't belong on the list.

Andrew's draft had three methods: loyalty cards, a second barista, a new coffee machine. He asked each one "what for?" They collapsed into two goals already on the list.

Check each goal in a minute. "A good profit" has no number. "15" has no unit. "15%" has no deadline. "15% by the sixth month" is a goal.

### 6. Write the stop line now

For each top goal, write the minimum below which the result is no longer viable. Then write what makes you stop, revise or carry on, as a number you can check.

That is the whole template. Constraints, nine goals at most, an order, six fields, no methods, a stop line. Andrew needed fifteen minutes.

## Quit day trading or keep going: settle it on a calm evening

<strong>Three out of four new day traders quit within two years.</strong>

Decide the question of quitting before the hard day, not during it. Write the condition down while your head is clear. Then the worst afternoon meets a decision that already exists.

Andrew saw what happens without one. Suppose all five goals had stayed "equally important". A supplier raises the price of milk. Sara asks for another barista on Fridays. Andrew agrees, because he is tired and doesn't want to argue. A month later he finds he ran out of money before he ran out of time. Fatigue made the choice.

Elena, a freelance designer, used the order the other way, as a permission. Her time goes first. While she writes her book, from month 1 to month 3, she may work up to 55 hours a week. That is not a lapse. It is a decision made in advance, with a clear time limit.

How to avoid quitting by exhaustion, or staying by hope: give every permission an end date. Give every goal a check rhythm.

## Trading losses and the risk of ruin: order beats count

Here is the contrast, in Andrew's numbers. Two plans for the end of the year.

| Goal | Plan X | Plan Y |
|---|---|---|
| Cash reserve, goal 2.0 | 2.0, met | 1.8, not met |
| Margin, goal 15% | 15%, met | 16%, met |
| Regular customers, goal 40% | 32%, not met | 42%, met |
| Complaints, goal 2 or fewer | 2, met | 1, met |
| Hours, goal 40 or fewer | 40, met | 38, met |

Count the goals met, and the plans tie: four out of five each. Plan Y even looks better on four rows. By the refusal order, Plan X wins. It meets the one goal that keeps the doors open.

Counting goals and ordering goals give different answers. The model goes with the order.

The same rule protects the constraints. If saving the top goal would mean breaking a constraint, the constraint wins. Find another way, or stop and change the conditions.

This is one piece of a loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. The order comes first, because every later step asks it which way to turn.

"I'm a trader, not a coffee shop." The test doesn't care. Alex, thirteen, used it for a €300 bicycle. Games went first, football second, and the bicycle stayed until last. An order you can stick to beats one that looks good to others.

## The cost of six more months without an order

Look at Andrew's money first. At his real 9% margin, if nothing changed, the reserve would reach two months at M16. With the plan working, at M10. That is six extra months one breakdown away from trouble.

And the breakdown itself costs more at the low margin. At 9%, one costs a full month of progress. At 15%, about half of one.

An unordered list does the same to any account. Every hard day reopens the question, and the answer depends on how tired you are.

The human cost is one sentence. The biggest decision of the year gets made at the worst hour, by the most tired version of you.

## What the goal table does not fix

The table tells you what to defend. It treats a symptom: you stop deciding by fatigue.

The cause of bad calls stays in place. You still don't know where you stand, row by row, without wishful rounding. And you don't know the exact point where a falling number stops being a warning and becomes an emergency.

Two steps close that gap:

- <strong>The state from measured data.</strong> One source per number, so "roughly fine" becomes a figure you can trust. Chapter 2.
- <strong>Error and the three zones.</strong> Where each row turns yellow and red, and the red protocol for the day two rows go red at once. Chapter 4.

Without them, the table tells you what matters. It doesn't tell you when to act.

## FAQ

### What is the day trading success rate?

The two lines in this post give the odds for everyone who starts. They say nothing about your own plan. Write your constraints, your goals in order and a stop line, and you can judge your own year against your own numbers.

### What should a trading plan include?

On the goal side, four parts. Constraints you never break. Nine goals at most, in refusal order, with six fields each. And a stop line set in advance. The book covers that side of the plan. It gives no trading signals.

### When should you quit day trading?

When the condition you wrote in advance is met. Not on the worst afternoon, and not because you are tired. Write the condition as a number with a date, and check it on a fixed rhythm.
