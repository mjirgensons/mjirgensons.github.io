The car sleeps on the street now. The garage belongs to the boxes.

You walk past them every night on the way in. Your partner doesn't say anything any more. They look at the stack, then at you. You both know whose savings paid for it.

When a young business hits trouble, two out of three owners pour in their own money.

So you lie awake and do the sum in your head. So many boxes, at so much each, makes a number that sounds like an asset. In your head, the garage is a savings account. On the market, it may be worth half. Or nothing.

Here is the turn. You can sell. You sold enough to fill a garage and order more. What you don't have is a system that tells you, in numbers, what those boxes are worth today. So you carry the whole guess on your back, and it gets heavier every month.

The Jirgenson Model gives the answer: dead stock is not a storage problem. It is a wrong number in your picture of where you are. Fix the number first, then pull one lever. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What is dead stock? The meaning in plain words

Dead stock is goods you bought to sell that will not sell at a price that covers what they cost you. Unsold inventory that is still moving is not dead. A box nobody wants at any sensible price is.

The exact definition matters less than the effect. Those boxes sit in your books at the price you paid. So the books say you own more than you do. Every plan built on those books starts from the wrong place.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> dead stock is a measurement error in the state: value that the records count and the money will never see. You can manage only what you can observe, so until it is counted, every decision rests on a number that does not exist.</div>

Whatever is not written down anywhere does not exist for the report. It still exists for the money.

## Excess inventory: a number that lies

Here is what happens in a company that never counts. Its books show €100,000 of goods in the warehouse. A physical stocktake finds half of it out of date or spoiled. The real state is €50,000.

Now the plan: grow sales by half, with the credit lines fully drawn and no money to buy raw materials. Two numbers were wrong at once. The stock was half what the books said. And the goal could not live with its own limit, no new capital.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, met the small version. He was sure his margin held at around twenty per cent. Then he measured it: 9%. He had never counted waste in his cost of goods. Milk that went sour. Pastries written off at the end of the shift. Every write-off ate the margin and never showed up in a report. The same blind spot is mapped in [waste in small business](/blog/waste-in-small-business/).

That same week, he had planned to hire a second barista and start an advertising campaign. The whole plan rested on a margin that did not exist.

Too much inventory is rarely the first mistake. The first mistake is the number you believed.

## How to find dead stock in one evening

This is the stock count. It takes one evening, a sheet of paper and your stock list. Each step sits next to a filled-in example.

1. **Define "dead" before you count.** Write the definition next to the number, so next month you count the same way. Pick a period with no sale that fits your goods: days for pastries, months for clothes. Andrew's case: anything thrown away, gone sour or written off at the end of a shift.
2. **Name one source and one person.** Ask three questions of the figure. Who counted it? How, exactly? Over what period? Andrew took his margin from the accounting system that the till feeds. A stock list counted by hand beats a memory of "about forty boxes".
3. **Count two values.** The book value: what you paid. The shelf value: what it would sell for this month. The warehouse above: €100,000 on the books, €50,000 on the shelf.
4. **Put the gap into your cost.** Recompute the margin with the write-offs included. Andrew's moved from the twenty he believed to the 9% he had. Once write-offs were counted, his margin moved only about one point from month to month. The number became fit to steer by. The full method of logging each write-off is in [how to track write-offs](/blog/how-to-track-write-offs/).
5. **Draw the red line on a calm day.** Andrew set his margin zones one evening while his head was clear. Green: 14% and above. Yellow: from 5% up to 14%. Red: below 5%. Set yours the same way, and work back to the write-off total that would push your margin across the line. The red line goes where damage becomes hard to undo. Setting the three lines is covered in [KPI zones](/blog/kpi-zones/).
6. **Pull one lever, fast and reversible first.** Ask three questions of any lever. Can I move it myself, today? How soon does the effect show? Can I undo it? Andrew's menu lever passed all three: yes, quickly, and an item can come back. His plan dropped the 2 most loss-making items, worth an estimated 1.5 points of margin. Its backup lever, if the plan fell short, was tracking and cutting write-offs.
7. **Check the slow item before you cut it.** Andrew planned to drop one item, and the Monday check stopped him. That item brought his regulars in. A slow seller can be the reason people come for the fast ones.

One evening of honest counting beats six months of hope. For the margin arithmetic itself, see [how to calculate profit margin](/blog/how-to-calculate-profit-margin/).

## Low profit margin: what six more months cost

Money first. Andrew's margin stood at 9% against a goal of 15%. On revenue of €30,000 a month, that gap is €1,800 of profit a month: €2,700 instead of €4,500. Six more months of not counting is €10,800.

The cash reserve pays as well. At 9%, his reserve reaches its goal at M16. With the plan working, at M10. And at 9%, one breakdown worth a tenth of a month of expenses costs about a month of time. At 15%, it costs about half a month. Boxes nobody counts keep you at the slow speed. What a month of reserve means is in [months of runway](/blog/months-of-runway/).

Then the human cost. The car stays on the street, and the silence at home gets longer.

Stress about money can keep anyone awake. If the sleepless nights, the dread or the low mood last longer than a bad week, see a doctor. If it feels urgent, call a crisis line. The model treats the chaos in the business, not an illness.

## FAQ

### What is dead stock?

Dead stock is inventory you bought to sell that will not sell at a price that covers its cost. It still sits in the books at what you paid, so your records show more value and a higher margin than you have.

### What should I do with excess inventory?

Count it first: define what counts as dead, then record what you paid and what it would sell for this month. Put the gap into your cost and recompute the margin. Then pull one fast, reversible lever and check the result before you pull another.

### Why is my profit margin low when sales are good?

Often because a cost never reaches the report: waste, spoiled goods, items written off. A coffee shop owner believed his margin was around twenty per cent; with write-offs counted, it was 9%. Steer, Don't Guess shows how to set zones and levers for the real number.
