You paid extra on the card on the first. By the twentieth, the groceries had put it back.

That's not a payoff. It's a treadmill.

Here is the irony. You know the interest rate on every card to the decimal. You don't know whether this month will add new debt or remove it.

So the plan gets rebuilt every few months. A new app, a new spreadsheet, a new resolve. The balance doesn't care about resolve. It follows the gap.

The answer to this pain comes from the Jirgenson Model. A plan is not an order of debts. It is a page with five answers. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What a debt payoff plan is, and what it skips

A debt payoff plan is a written list of every debt, with balance, rate and minimum payment. It also sets the order for extra money. Two orders are well known. The avalanche pays the highest rate first. The snowball pays the smallest balance first.

Both answer one question: where does the extra money go?

Neither answers the two questions that decide the outcome. Where does the extra money come from? And what stops new debt from arriving while you pay off the old?

Pick the best order in the world, and a monthly gap still refills the card. The order is the last step, not the first.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> a plan for one goal is called the control concept. It answers five questions on one page: levers, resources, constraints, a leading indicator, and a deadline with an owner. If one question has no answer, you have a wish, not a plan.</div>

## The one-page debt payoff plan template

Here is the tool, filled in with a real case. Emma and Paul are the household in [Steer, Don't Guess](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0HPCJCWKF). They have no loan yet. They have the thing that makes one.

Their income is €4,000 a month. Their expenses rose to €4,400 over one quarter. They hold €2,000 in savings. Each month leaves them €400 behind. How they agreed on those numbers together is in [couples budget](/blog/couples-budget/).

That €400 is debt in the making. When the savings run out, it lands on a card, an overdraft or a loan. So their plan is a debt payoff plan written before the debt. Yours may start after it. The page is the same.

### Before the five questions: list the debts

Write every debt with its balance, rate and minimum payment. Pick your order. Every template has this part. Keep it, then answer the five questions that make it work.

### 1. Levers, and in what order

A [lever](/blog/levers-of-control/) is a move you can make yourselves. Rent and the bank's rates are not levers. You can watch them. You can't pull them.

Emma and Paul's levers: first, cut subscriptions and eating out by €180 a month. Second, €300 a month from a side job, added a month later. One lever at a time, so each effect shows on its own statement.

### 2. Resources

How much time and money each lever takes. The cut costs nothing but attention. The side job costs evenings. Write the hours down, or the plan will borrow them from sleep.

### 3. Constraints

What will you not do, even if it would be faster? Write it in a calm hour. Under pressure, the mind grabs fast and foolish moves. A written line stops them faster than any argument.

As an illustration, two lines from the book fit here. Alex, thirteen, wrote "do not borrow money" for [his bicycle fund](/blog/save-for-a-goal/). And for a business in a cash squeeze: "do not take an urgent high-interest loan". For a debt plan, the first constraint writes itself: no new borrowing to cover the gap.

### 4. Leading indicator

[One number](/blog/what-is-a-leading-indicator/) that tells you the plan works before the deadline does. For Emma and Paul: the account balance every week, and the monthly result on each statement. If the result is still €400 short after the cut, they know in one month, not in five.

### 5. Deadline, owner and the revision point

Who owns each lever, when is the first check, and what result there means "stop and revise"?

As an illustration for Emma and Paul: one name per lever. First check, the next statement after the cut. If it doesn't show the €180, they fix the cut before the side job starts.

Here is the page:

| Question | What to write | Emma and Paul |
|---|---|---|
| Debts | Balance, rate, minimum, order | None yet; the €400 gap is the debt in the making |
| Levers | Which, and in what order | Cut €180 first; side job €300 a month later |
| Resources | Time and money per lever | Attention for the cut; evenings for the side job |
| Constraints | What you will not do | No new borrowing to cover the gap (illustration) |
| Leading indicator | A number that moves early | Balance every week; result on every statement |
| Deadline and owner | First check and revision point | Next statement; no €180, no side job yet (illustration) |

## The enough test: do your levers cover the gap?

A page with answers can still fail. So test it. The sum of what your levers bring must cover the gap, with a buffer to spare.

Emma and Paul: €180 plus €300 is €480. The gap is €400. The buffer is €80 a month. The plan passes.

Now look at a plan that passed with almost nothing left. Andrew, the coffee shop owner, needed his margin to rise by 6 points. His four levers added up to 6.1. It passed, but the buffer was 0.1 of a point. So he did two things. He made the first checkpoint mandatory. And he wrote a backup lever into the plan in advance.

Copy that rule. If your levers only match the gap, write [the backup lever](/blog/contingency-plan-template/) today. A plan with no buffer turns red on the first bad month.

Here is where the debt order comes back in. Emma and Paul's €80 is the payoff engine. Avalanche or snowball only decides where that €80 goes. Without the first job, there is no €80 to aim.

| The usual payoff plan | The one-page plan |
|---|---|
| Starts with the order of debts | Starts with the monthly gap |
| "Pay more where you can" | Two named levers, one at a time |
| No line it will not cross | No new borrowing, in writing |
| Checks the balance at year end | Checks the result on every statement |
| Hopes the money appears | Tests that the levers cover the gap, with a buffer |

The usual plan is a map of the debts. The one-page plan is the engine that moves you along it.

## The cost of six more months

Here is what doing nothing costs, with Emma and Paul's numbers.

Six months at €400 short is €2,400. The savings hold €2,000. They cover five months. The sixth month's €400 goes on a card or an overdraft, and the reserve is zero.

Carry on to the end of the year and the arithmetic gets blunt. Months six to twelve add €400 each. That is €2,800 of new debt, before a cent of interest. Sooner and more, if expenses keep rising the way they did.

And the human cost has a sound: checking the balance in the queue at the till, before you tap the card.

"I don't have time for this." The page takes fifteen minutes. The cost of skipping it is €400 a month.

## What the page does not fix

Be honest about what you built. The page stops new debt and gives the surplus a target. It treats the symptom: the treadmill.

The cause sits deeper. Placed in [the whole loop](https://github.com/mjirgensons/jirgenson-model) of goal, state, gap, levers, plan and forecast, this page is the plan step. Two parts around it are still missing.

- **Zones and the red protocol.** [The line](/blog/kpi-zones/) where "a bit behind" turns into "act now", agreed in advance. And the order of action when the reserve and the debt both turn red in the same month. Chapter 4.
- **The three offsets.** [The check before you start](/blog/why-business-plans-fail/): is the goal contradictory, is the starting balance wrong, is the ground under the plan moving? A flawless plan built from the wrong floor still lands in the wrong place. Chapter 11.

Without them, the page pays down the debt. It doesn't yet tell you what to do the month the car breaks down.

The method does not create money. It shows early whether the money you can create is enough. For serious financial trouble, it helps you see the situation, and it does not replace professional advice.

## FAQ

### How do I make a debt payoff plan?

List every debt with its balance, rate and minimum. Then close the monthly gap first. Name your levers and the time they take. Write what you will not do, one early number to watch, and a first checkpoint. Aim the surplus at one debt at a time.

### Is there a simple debt payoff plan template?

Yes. One page, six rows: the debts, the levers in order, the resources, the constraints, a leading indicator, and a deadline with an owner. Then add the sum of the levers and check that it beats the gap, with a buffer.

### Should I use the snowball or the avalanche method?

Either works as an order. The avalanche pays the highest rate first; the snowball pays the smallest balance first. Neither works while a monthly gap keeps refilling the debt, so close the gap before you choose.
