Most of the mistakes in Andrew's coffee shop came from its most experienced person. Late at night.

He worked sixty-five hours a week. The late hours were where the mistakes lived: figures mixed up, a call to the supplier put off, deadlines that turned out wrong.

Meanwhile Sara, his senior barista, knew to the minute when the Friday wave from the office next door would hit.

The owner held the wheel. The person who could see the road stood behind the counter.

"Delegate" is a tip that fits on a sticky note. It never says to whom.

## Delegating to the right person: whoever sees furthest

Here is the rule. In each area of the business, the person at the wheel should be the one who sees furthest ahead. Not the one with the biggest title. Not the one with the most free hours.

The same restaurant is easy to read for an experienced manager and a mystery for a novice. Same kitchen, same guests, same Friday. What changes is how far the person in charge can see.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> predictability belongs to a pair: the work plus the person at the wheel. You can control exactly as much as you can foresee. So never ask only about the process. Ask who runs it, and what they can see coming.</div>

This turns delegation from a favour into a design choice. You are not offloading work. You are moving each area to the head that predicts it best.

## Who should not be at the wheel, even when it is you

The rule has a sharp edge. Sometimes the person who sees worst is the owner.

Fatigue bends a forecast the way a faulty gauge bends a reading. At sixty-five hours a week, Andrew was the right person for the margin in the morning. Late at night he was the wrong person for almost anything.

That is why his own hours sit on his goal list. Not only for rest. A cap on hours protects the quality of every decision he makes.

He learned to act on it. On the night of a bad month, with prices up and a barista gone, he checked one thing first. Was there a direct danger to people or to mandatory payments? There was not. So he decided nothing, set a meeting with Sara for the morning, and went to bed.

## The wheel test: four questions for each area

This takes about fifteen minutes with a sheet of paper. Here are the steps, and then Andrew's filled-in sheet.

1. **List the areas where things go wrong.** The Friday rush, supplier prices, the late-night decisions. Start with the ones that hurt most often.
2. **Ask who sees it coming.** For each area, name the person or source that can say what happens next, before it happens. What looks like weather to you is predictable to someone.
3. **Check the state of whoever holds it now.** Hours this week, time of day, what else they carry. A tired expert forecasts worse than the same expert rested.
4. **Hand it over with a map.** Write down what the best person watches: the time, what happens, what to do five minutes before. That page is a process map.

Andrew's sheet (an illustration, built from the coffee shop's own scenes):

| Area | What must be foreseen | Who sees it coming | At the wheel after the test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Friday evening shift | When the office wave lands; which order is next | Sara | Sara |
| Supplier prices | A price rise before the invoice | The supplier's account manager; the weekly margin per receipt | Andrew, with a regular call to the account manager |
| Decisions after a bad day | What the real figures say | Andrew, rested, the next morning | Nobody at night; Andrew and Sara in the morning |
| Cash reserve and margin | Where the money will be in weeks | Andrew, checking weekly | Andrew |

Look at the second row. Andrew did not wait for the bank statement to learn prices were rising. He heard it in a conversation with the supplier's account manager, and saw it in the margin per receipt. The person who saw it first was not on his payroll.

Look at the third row. The best decision for a bad night was a time slot, not a person.

## Turn one person's foresight into a process map

Handing over the wheel is half the job. The other half is making sure the knowledge does not walk out with one person.

A week after Sara took the Friday shifts, she began coaching the trainee. She did not teach him to work faster. She taught him what to watch. Together they put Friday on one sheet of paper.

That sheet is the cheapest upgrade to predictability in business. What sat in one head now sits on a wall. A trainee can glance at it instead of guessing. The skill becomes a process the whole team can run.

## What keeping the wheel costs

Without the test, a tired owner keeps every wheel. And a tired owner says yes.

Picture Andrew without his ranked goals. Sara asks for a second barista on Fridays. He would agree, because he is exhausted and does not want to argue. The money would come out of a reserve of 0.5 months: about €13,650, roughly two weeks of expenses. A month later the money would run out before the energy.

The slower cost shows in the forecast. At a 9% margin his reserve reaches two months at month 16. With the margin plan working, at month 10. At 9%, one breakdown worth a tenth of a month of expenses pushes the goal back a full month.

A year later, Andrew's hours stood at 44 instead of 65. Still above his goal of 40, and he says so. But for the first time in three years he ate lunch at home on Sundays.

## "I run the business alone"

Then you are at every wheel, and the test still works. It tells you where your forecast is weakest.

Elena, a freelance designer, was launching her first book with little experience. She measured a trial stream of visitors: 1.5 buyers per 100. Then she compared that forecast with real sales every week. She could not hand the wheel to someone who saw further. So she made her own sight sharper, one week at a time.

## Where the right driver is not enough

The wheel test puts the right person in each seat. It treats one symptom: decisions made by whoever is closest, or most tired.

The deeper cause remains. Sara can read a Friday perfectly and still not know at what point Friday is bad enough to call Andrew. Andrew can see his margin and still be reacting to last month instead of the next three.

## FAQ

### How do you choose the right person to delegate to?

Pick the person who can best predict what happens next in that area. Test it: can they tell you what comes next week before it happens? Seniority and spare time are weaker signals.

### Should an owner delegate areas they know best?

Not always. But check the hours and the time of day. Fatigue corrupts forecasts, so a late-night call after a 65-hour week belongs to the morning, or to someone rested.

### Who should run what in a one-person business?

You run everything, so measure where your forecasts miss. Compare each forecast with the real number every week. *Steer, Don't Guess* shows how to build those forecasts for every goal.
