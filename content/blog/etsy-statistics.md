**Six out of ten Etsy shops ever opened have gone dark.**

That number didn't have to be so high. Some of those shops might still be open if their owners had known, early and plainly, which problem could end them. With a control system, the count could look different.

Now picture your own Tuesday. A slow week of orders. A three-star review. A supplier email about new prices. All three land before lunch, and you fix whichever one shouted last.

Here is the irony. The shop pings you for every new favourite. Nothing pings when the cash drops below one month of costs.

The problem isn't effort. It's the order of the work.

## Etsy statistics need a gauge, not a verdict

Look at an engine temperature gauge. Three coloured sectors. Green: drive on. Yellow: pay attention. Red: stop. You don't strip the engine. You look at the sector.

Run your shop the same way. For each goal, subtract the goal from today's value: actual minus goal. That gap is the error. It is the main signal for every decision you make.

A gap alone doesn't say whether to act. You need lines drawn in advance.

| Zone | What it means | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | The gap is within tolerance | Leave it alone |
| Acceptable | Worse than normal, no damage to the structure yet | Watch it, find the cause, prepare a move |
| Emergency | Past the permissible line, nothing irreversible yet | Get back to acceptable; everything else waits |
| Undefined | No data, old data or data that contradict each other | Measure first; this is not normal |

## Set the zones in one calm evening

This is the tool. About 15 minutes, one sheet. Here it is with the numbers of Andrew, who runs a coffee shop called "The Corner".

**1. Write each goal next to today's value.** Andrew's reserve: goal 2.0 months, now 0.5. Margin: goal 15%, now 9%.

**2. Set three zones for every row.** Andrew did it that same evening, while his head was clear.

| Row | Normal | Acceptable | Emergency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash reserve, months | 2.0 or more | 1.0 to under 2.0 | Under 1.0 |
| Margin, % | 14 or more | 5 to under 14 | Under 5 |
| Regular customers, % | 36 or more | 20 to under 36 | Under 20 |
| Complaints a week | 2 or fewer | 3 to 8 | Over 8 |
| Owner's hours a week | 42 or fewer | Over 42, up to 70 | Over 70 |

**3. Draw the red line where damage gets hard to reverse.** Andrew put the reserve's red line at one month. A month gives him time to negotiate a deferral, find money and decide. Under a month, there is no time for any of that.

**4. Make no zone narrower than your measuring error.** If your margin records drift by a point, a green zone half a point wide is noise.

**5. Subtract and sort.** Andrew's errors: reserve minus 1.5 months, red. Margin minus 6 points, yellow. Regular customers minus 15 points, yellow. Complaints plus 3, yellow. Hours plus 25, yellow.

**6. Write the lines down and leave them.** Moving a threshold mid-crisis bends the ruler to fit the result.

One red row and four yellow ones. Now Andrew knew where to look first.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> the error vector is the gap between each goal and its measured value, row by row. Each row has three zones set in advance: normal, acceptable and emergency. The zone, not the size of the gap, tells you whether to act.</div>

## Online store tips for a short life: the red protocol

**The typical online store lives about six months.**

Six months didn't have to be the whole story. Some of those stores might have reached a second year if their owners had seen the red row early and dropped the rest. With a written order for bad days, the number could look different.

When a row turns red, follow four steps.

1. **Check for a direct threat.** People, safety, mandatory payments, the law. If there is one, protect and escalate first.
2. **Freeze secondary work.** Anything that doesn't help the red row waits.
3. **Put time, money and attention on the red row.** And on the levers that act on it.
4. **If several rows are red, start with the nearest irreversible damage.** Then, other things equal, save the goal you would give up last.

Andrew's first thought at that table was loyalty cards, to win customers back. The table said no. Cards cost money: printing, discounts, setting up the till. That money would come out of a reserve already under a month.

"But you wanted loyalty cards," Sara said that evening.

"I did," Andrew replied. "But the reserve is in the red zone."

Regular customers drift away slowly. A red reserve can end everything quickly. He put the cards aside and, for the first time, felt no guilt. The order he chose with a clear head made the call, not his fatigue.

## Ecommerce failure rate in year one: priority, not a queue

**Two out of three Shopify stores vanish within their first year.**

Some of those owners might have kept going with one habit. Sort problems by priority, not by arrival, and the year-one count could look different.

A queue of incoming problems is run by chance. A queue of decisions is run by priority. When a new problem lands, put three questions to it before you touch it:

1. Which goal does it belong to?
2. Which zone is that row in now?
3. Is it more important than what I'm doing right now?

Elena, a freelance designer, shows why. One client brings her 70% of her income. Her goal is no more than 30%, and above 50% is her emergency line. That row is red. Then the tax authority freezes her account. Two reds at once.

Income is her goal number one, and the frozen account hits it directly. So she puts the client problem on hold and turns everything to the account.

When everything is urgent, nothing is managed.

## Ecommerce mistakes the green zone prevents

The green zone guards against a costly habit: fixing what works.

Suppose that a few months later Andrew's complaints drop below two a week. He is tempted to fine-tune the service. It could always be better. But the row is green. Correcting it costs effort and buys nothing.

So leave green rows alone.

The yellow zone asks for the opposite. Nothing is on fire, so it is easy to wait. Don't. Find the cause while it is still cheap. Ask: what has changed since last time? Since when? Which lever affects it? Andrew wrote for his margin: waste and unprofitable menu items. For regular customers he had no cause yet, so he wrote "find out" in his task list.

## Ecommerce margin: what the error costs in money

Turn the gaps into money and the zones stop being abstract.

Andrew's monthly expenses are €27,300. His reserve error of 1.5 months equals €40,950 missing from the cushion. His margin error of 6 points on €30,000 of revenue is €1,800 of profit lost every month: €2,700 instead of €4,500. Six more months like that cost €10,800.

The gap also decides how hard one bad day hits. A broken coffee machine is a nuisance for a shop with two months of reserve. For a shop with two weeks, it is an emergency.

The human cost fits in one sentence. Every new problem gets decided by whoever is loudest that day, and the owner goes home with all of it.

## "Zones are too much for a small shop"

They take about 15 minutes.

Alex, a boy saving for a bicycle, keeps three zones for his savings. By 1 July his schedule says €170. He has €140. The gap is minus €30. Up to €10 behind is normal for him, 11 to 40 is acceptable, more than 40 is an emergency. So he is in yellow. No panic. He knows the cause: the scooter isn't sold yet.

If a schoolboy can run three zones on a piggy bank, a shop can run them on five rows.

## Where the zones stop

The zones treat a symptom: you stop answering problems in the order they arrive.

The cause is still in place. You know the reserve is red. You don't yet know which action will move it, how fast, or whether you can undo it. And the zones show today, not the month after next.

Two steps close that gap.

- **Levers.** What you can change by your own decision, with three questions asked before you pull. Chapter 5.
- **Two forecasts.** "If nothing changes" against "if the plan works", so you see a red row coming. Chapter 8.

The zones are the third step of one loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. Etsy statistics describe thousands of shops. The zones describe yours.

## FAQ

### How do I know if my Etsy shop is failing?

Etsy statistics can't tell you. Don't judge by the mood of the week either. Set three zones for each number, subtract the goal from today's value and see which zone each row is in. A red row is a signal to act now, not a verdict.

### Which Etsy shop tips matter first?

Know your cash in months of costs, draw its red line, and freeze everything else when it crosses that line. Then work through the yellow rows by finding their causes. Leave the green rows alone.

### What ecommerce margin should I aim for?

Set your own goal, with a number and a deadline, then draw the zones around it. Andrew's goal was 15%, with normal from 14% and emergency below 5%. The complete method, from the goal list to the forecast, is in the full control system.
