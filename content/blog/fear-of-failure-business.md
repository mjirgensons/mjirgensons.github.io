**Only one business application in ten ever turns into a company that hires anyone.**

Picture the other nine. A name registered on a Sunday night. A domain, a logo, a folder of ideas. Then Monday comes, the salary lands, and the folder closes for another year.

The form was never the hard part. The decision was, and fear makes it late at night with no numbers on the table.

That is the habit to break. Not the fear itself. The habit of letting it decide.

## Business applications without a goal table

Look at that first number again. Skip the post-mortem on the nine. Put your own start on paper first.

Turn your idea into a goal table before you turn it into a company. Take a sheet of paper. Write the five things you want from the business, each with a number and a deadline. "A good income" has no number. Elena, the freelancer in the book, wrote an average income of at least €3,000 a month by month 14. That is a goal.

Then run the refusal test. If things got bad, which goal would you give up first? Which would be the last one left? Number them in that order.

Andrew, the coffee shop owner in the book, did this in fifteen minutes. He put his own hours last and the cash reserve first. For the first time in two years he felt solid ground under his feet.

**How to avoid it:** a form records a name. A goal table records an order. File the second one first.

## Fear of failure: the loudest voice in the room

**Half of all adults say the fear of failing would stop them from starting.**

Fear is loud, and it has never seen your numbers.

So do not wait for courage. Write the order while you are calm. Set your goals, your constraints and the line you will not cross on a quiet evening. Then, on the bad night, the decision follows your own order, settled long before. It does not follow the knot in your stomach.

**How to avoid it:** never make a start decision in the hour the fear is loudest. Sleep on it, then run the seven steps below.

## Seven steps before starting a business

This is the tool. It works for any tangled situation, and a first start is the most tangled one there is. The main mistake is rarely the wrong decision. It is the decision made too early.

Here are the seven steps, each with the coffee shop's entry from a bad month. The supplier raised prices by 8%, the coffee machine broke down, and a barista left. All in the same week.

| Step | What to do | Andrew's entry |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Calm the noise | Check for immediate danger. If there is none, decide nothing tonight | No danger to people or payments. He booked a talk with Sara for the morning and went to bed |
| 2. Read the state | Pull the real figures from the systems | Margin down from 15% to 12%. Reserve at 1.56 months |
| 3. Restore the trajectory | Look at 12 to 36 months of history: speed, not a single point | Purchases had been rising by about 1% a month. Old trend, not news |
| 4. Break down the processes | What is getting better, what is getting worse | Purchases dearer; guests up; share of regulars down; team unstable |
| 5. Find the enclosing system | The largest system you belong to: industry, cycle, rules | Prices rising at every supplier in the region |
| 6. Build scenarios | Two or three options with numbers and dates | Change nothing: margin 12%, reserve 2.0 at M12. Price +5%: margin 15%, M11. Price +5% and a part-time barista: 13.5%, M11 |
| 7. Decide and accept | Act, or choose not to. Expect the effect to take time | Price +5%. One lever only, so he could see what it did |

Three weeks later Andrew could not remember the last time he had panicked.

Now run the same steps on your own start:

1. **Danger.** Is any debt, rent or mandatory payment at risk if you start? Protect it first. If nothing is at risk, decide nothing tonight.
2. **State.** Write today's numbers from your bank statements: savings, monthly costs, free hours a week.
3. **Trajectory.** Read the last 12 to 36 months. Are your savings growing or shrinking? Have your free hours held?
4. **Processes.** What is improving: skills, contacts, first paying customers? What is slipping?
5. **Enclosing system.** Is your chosen market growing or fading?
6. **Scenarios.** Write three: do not start; start small beside the job; start full time. Give each a number and a date.
7. **Decide.** Pick one. Then give it time to work before you judge it.

That takes an evening. It replaces a year of "maybe next spring".

Here is the contrast. Had Andrew decided that same night, he would most likely have hired a full-time barista and hunted for a new supplier. Two levers at once. He could never have told which one worked.

## Entrepreneur fear and the trend you cannot change

**More people are scared to start a business today than five years ago.**

Do not argue with the climate. Read it. That is steps 3 and 5: the trajectory over years, and the system you sit inside.

Then use judo, not boxing. Find what is already moving in your direction and amplify it. Andrew did not fight rising prices across the region. He moved his own price with the current, and guests could see the same thing all over town.

**How to avoid it:** judge your market by its direction over 12 to 36 months. A scary headline is one point, not a trend.

## What six more months of waiting cost

Drift has a price, and the coffee shop puts it in months.

At a 9% margin, Andrew's reserve reaches two months of costs at month 16. With his margin plan working, at month 10. Same shop, same hours, six months apart. And at 9%, every breakdown worth a tenth of a month of costs pushes the goal back a full month. At 15%, about half a month.

The family budget example shows the same arithmetic from the other side. Emma and Paul ran €400 short each month. Change nothing, and their €2,000 of savings would run out in five months.

Waiting is not free. It is a plan too, the one called "if nothing changes", and it has a date.

And every one of those months, the idea sits in a folder while the job you wanted to leave keeps your evenings.

## Your first business: the one that never opens

**The biggest failure isn't the business that closes. It's the one that never opens.**

Do not cancel the start, and do not push it to "better times". Tie it to a launch condition: a measurable event, not a date.

Andrew did this with his second location. Margin had reached 15%, but the reserve stood at 1.35 months. He wrote: the manoeuvre starts once the reserve has held at two months for three months in a row. Sara asked if that meant waiting. "No," he said. "We do whatever brings the condition closer."

Write your own the same way. For example: "I hand in my notice when savings have covered two months of costs for three months in a row". Add a second line for first paying customers. Then write the conditions for stopping too. A start with an exit line is far less frightening than a leap.

**How to avoid it:** a written condition cannot be postponed by a bad mood. A wish can.

## "My situation is different"

It is not, and the steps do not care what you sell. Emma and Paul used them on a family budget. They took their state from three months of statements, wrote three scenarios and chose one. Then they started with a single lever, so they could see its effect.

No time? The basic start takes 30 minutes: five wishes, the refusal test, today's number on each, one lever for tomorrow.

## Where the seven steps stop

You now have the tool, and it is complete. It treats the symptom: you stop deciding in panic.

The cause is still in place. You do not yet see where your start leads in six months, in numbers. And you have no route from the goal back to this week's first task.

Two steps close that gap.

- **Two forecasts.** "If nothing changes" against "if the plan works", with figures and dates. The fear then meets a picture of the future, not a guess. Chapter 8.
- **The backward move.** From the finished goal back to this week, with resources checked at every step. Chapter 15. A short version is in [backward planning](/blog/backward-planning/).

The seven steps are the entry ramp. The road is one loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast.

## FAQ

### How do you overcome fear of failure in business?

Do not wait for it to pass. Write your goals in order. Then run seven steps: calm the noise, read the state and the trend, break down the processes, find the larger system, build scenarios, decide. Fear stays. It stops deciding.

### Is it normal to be afraid of starting a business?

Yes. The second number in this post shows how common it is: half of all adults say it would stop them. The question is not whether you feel it. It is whether your start rests on a written condition or on a mood.

### What should I do before starting my first business?

Write five goals with numbers and deadlines and put them in refusal order. Take today's figures from your statements. Write three scenarios. Then set a launch condition you can measure, such as months of costs saved and paying customers.
