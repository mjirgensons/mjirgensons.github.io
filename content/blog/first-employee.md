Andrew nearly hired a barista on a margin that didn't exist.

He was sure his coffee shop kept about twenty per cent. That same week he planned a second barista and an advertising campaign. Then he measured. The real margin was nine.

You may be at that point now. Every shift is yours. The queue is out the door. A job ad sits in your drafts, and it feels like the only way out.

Here is the odd part. You'll spend a week choosing the right candidate. Then you'll spend five minutes checking whether the business can carry one.

A hire is a fixed cost that arrives every month, in good months and bad. The answer to "can I hire now?" comes from the Jirgenson Model: three checks that catch a plan before it fails. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## When to hire your first employee: the three checks

Diligence does not save a plan that was broken before it started. Every plan rests on three anchor points: the goal, the starting point and the world around it. Shift any one, and the best effort lands in the wrong place.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> an offset is a shift of one of the plan's anchor points away from reality. Offset (a): goals that cannot be met at once. Offset (b): a wrong starting number. Offset (c): an environment that is fading. Each one can be spotted before you spend a cent.</div>

Three pictures make them easy to remember. One man tries to board two trains leaving in opposite directions. Another, lost in a building, runs for an open window, which is on the tenth floor. A third buys a ticket for the maiden voyage of the finest liner afloat. The ship is the *Titanic*.

Here is the test for a first hire.

| Offset | The check before you hire | If the answer is bad |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Clashing goals | Does this hire fight a goal I rank higher? | Return to your goal order; drop or delay one goal |
| (b) Wrong start | Do my numbers match the bank and the accounts? | Audit the data first; hire on the real figure |
| (c) Fading world | Is the demand behind this hire growing or shrinking? | Change where you play, not who you hire |

## Check (b) first: the margin you only believe in

Start here, because it is the cheapest to fix and the most expensive to miss.

Andrew's belief rested on one blind spot. He had never counted waste in his cost of goods. Sour milk and pastries written off at closing ate the margin but showed up in no report.

Now run his hire both ways. His shop turns over €30,000 a month. A second barista costs €1,800 a month.

| | Margin he believed | Margin he had |
|---|---|---|
| Profit per month | €6,000 (20%) | €2,700 (9%) |
| Left after the barista | €4,200 | €900 |
| Monthly costs after the hire | | €29,100 |
| Reserve added per month | | about 0.03 months |
| Time to a two-month reserve | | almost 50 months |

Without the hire, the same shop adds about 0.10 months of reserve each month and reaches the goal in about fifteen months. With it, the road stretches to four years.

An eleven-point error in one number turned a sensible hire into a four-year hole.

You can see the same thing in margin points. At €30,000 of turnover, €1,800 is six points of margin. At twenty per cent, that is a cost. At nine, it eats two thirds of the profit.

Freelancers meet the same trap at smaller scale. Elena, a freelance designer, believed she earned about €2,000 a month, because one month had brought in €2,200. Six months of bank statements showed €1,300. An assistant priced against €2,000 would have been paid from money she never had.

The fix takes one evening. Pull three to six months of bank statements. Count waste and write-offs as costs. Then do the hire sum on that figure, not on your memory.

## Check (a): goals that pull the hire apart

The second check asks whether the hire fits your goals or fights them.

Begin with a sharper question: is "hire someone" a goal at all? Andrew asked "what for?" about his own list. A second barista was there to stop Friday complaints. So the goal was quality: no more than two complaints a week, against five at the time. The hire was only one way to reach it.

That changes the hire or outsource question. Once the goal is named, a full-time hire is one lever among several. Name at least one other before you choose. A wrong lever paid every month is hard to undo.

Then check the order. Andrew ranked his goals by what he would give up last. The cash reserve came first. His own hours came last. A hire that drains a reserve of half a month serves the last goal by attacking the first. That is two trains, two directions.

The clash does not mean "never". It means "in sequence". First the reserve, then the hire, with the order written down so a tired evening cannot rewrite it.

## Check (c): the ship, not the deckchair

The third check looks past your business at the system that carries it. Is it growing?

Andrew asked about his street. New homes, offices, students? A university building was going up nearby. The world around him was on his side. That is no guarantee, but it is a fair wind.

The same month an agent offered him a renovated unit in a shopping centre, at an attractive rent. Andrew asked about the ship, not the deckchair. Footfall at the centre had fallen for the third year running. The anchor tenant had left. The units next door stood empty. He turned it down.

For a hire, ask the same four questions about the demand you are hiring for:

1. Has it grown for several periods in a row, or only this month?
2. Are the strongest players in your field growing or leaving?
3. Do the numbers add up in at least one niche you serve?
4. What would it cost to be wrong?

If the extra demand is one busy season, the hire is a bet on weather.

## Andrew's sheet, filled in

Here is the test as one page. It takes fifteen minutes with your real numbers.

| Check | Andrew's answer | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Goals | The hire serves quality but drains the reserve, and the reserve ranks first | Clash: hire later, in sequence |
| (b) Start | Believed 20% margin, real 9%; the hire leaves €900 a month | Fix the margin first |
| (c) World | Neighbourhood growing; university building nearby | Fair wind |

Two of three said "not yet". So Andrew worked on the margin and the reserve first. The hire did not vanish from his plans. Months later, with the margin plan working, he hired a replacement for a barista who had left.

## What hiring blind costs

The money comes first. A €1,800 hire on a nine per cent margin turns a fifteen-month road to safety into one of almost fifty. For four years, the shop runs one breakdown away from a crisis.

The human cost is simpler. It is the night before payroll, when the salary is due and the till says no.

"But I'm drowning, I need help now" is the honest objection. Then answer the three questions tonight. A hire the business cannot carry does not rescue you. It puts a second person in the water. On his worst month, Andrew's first urge was to plug the hole with a full-time barista and switch suppliers at once. Two levers together, and he could never have told which one worked.

## After the hire: you become a first time manager

The day the contract is signed, your job changes. You stop being the best pair of hands and start running a team of two.

The best new manager tip here is plain. Give each area to the person who sees furthest ahead in it, not to whoever has the most free hours. That rule, with a fifteen-minute test of its own, is in [delegating to the right person](/blog/delegating-to-the-right-person/).

## Where the three checks stop

The test is complete for its job. It catches a hire that would fail on a bad goal, a wrong number or a fading market. Use it before every hire, not only the first.

But it treats a symptom: a hire made at the wrong moment. The cause is that the moment has no rule. "When things calm down" is not a condition. Neither is "after the busy season".

Two steps of the system fix that. They are not taught here.

- **Two forecasts.** "If nothing changes" against "if the plan works", in numbers and dates. They show the payroll month before it arrives. Chapter 8.
- **The launch condition.** A hire is a manoeuvre. It starts when a measured condition holds, never on a hopeful date. Chapter 10 shows how to write it.

Without them, the three checks tell you "not now". They do not tell you "now".

### Key takeaways

- A first employee is a fixed cost every month. Check the business, not only the candidate.
- Run offset (b) first: hire on the margin in your bank, not the one in your head.
- "Hire someone" is a method. Name the goal it serves, then compare levers.
- Check the ship, not the deckchair: is the demand behind the hire growing?
- Two "not yet" answers mean sequence, not never.

## Your hiring decision

You have two choices. Post the job ad tonight on the margin you believe in, and find out on payroll day what the real one was. Or spend fifteen minutes on three questions first.

The three checks stop the wrong hire. The two forecasts and the launch condition tell you the month when the right one can start.

## FAQ

### When should you hire your first employee?

When three checks pass. The hire fits your goal order. Your real margin, from the bank and the accounts, can carry the cost every month. The demand behind it has grown for several periods, not one season.

### Should I hire or outsource?

First name the goal the hire serves, such as fewer complaints or fewer owner hours. Then compare every lever that reaches it. A full-time hire is one option, and the hardest to undo.

### How do I know if I can afford my first employee?

Subtract the monthly cost from your real monthly profit, then see how fast your cash reserve still grows. *Steer, Don't Guess* shows the sum with a coffee shop: a nine per cent margin turned a fifteen-month road into almost fifty.
