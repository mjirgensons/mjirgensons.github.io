Working alone? Three out of four solo businesses are gone within eight years.

Picture Thursday, ten at night. You're on the fourth round of edits for the client who pays most of your bills. Your income chart looks like a heart monitor. Somewhere in a folder sits the plan you wrote in January. It has a logo, a mission line and a forecast for year five.

It has no line for tonight.

That's the irony of the usual plan. It knows your revenue in five years. It can't tell you whether to say yes to this job.

A freelance business plan has to answer that question on a Tuesday morning. The answer to this pain comes from the Jirgenson Model: one page for each goal, and one budget for your hours. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What a freelance business plan has to answer

Forget the pitch deck. Nobody is lending you money. You need a plan that turns a goal into this week's work.

So write one page per goal. Each page answers five questions:

1. **Levers.** Which ones, and in what order?
2. **Resources.** How many hours, and how much money?
3. **Constraints.** What will I not do, even if it would be faster?
4. **Leading indicator.** Which number shows the plan works before the deadline?
5. **Deadline and owner.** Who checks, when is the first checkpoint, and what result means stop and revise?

If a question has no answer, you hold a wish, not a plan. Finding that answer is your next task.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> the plan for one goal is its control concept. All your plans together form the general concept. Control needs two things to be clear: the order of your goals and the means you allow yourself.</div>

That second part is the one freelancers skip. They write what they will do. They never write what they won't.

## Elena's starting point

Elena is a freelance designer. Her six months of bank statements told a plain story. Her freelance income swung between €800 and €2,200 a month and averaged €1,300. One client paid 70% of it. She worked fifty hours a week.

She wrote three goals and put them in order:

1. A three-month average income of at least €3,000 a month by month 14.
2. One client's share of income: 30% at most, by month 14.
3. A 35-hour week, with one exception: the three months of writing her book.

Time is the first thing she gives up when goals collide. Income is the last. How she cut the share of one big client without dropping them is in [client concentration](/blog/client-concentration-freelancer/). Here we follow the plan behind the first goal.

## Elena's plan, filled in

Her strongest lever for income was a product that doesn't depend on her hours: her first online book. She fitted the whole plan on one page.

| Question | Elena's answer |
|---|---|
| Levers | Write the manuscript; set the price; build the book's sales page; start a mailing list |
| Resources | About 120 hours over three months; €200 for the payment service and the mailing tool |
| Constraints | No emails to people who have not subscribed; no undercutting on price |
| Leading indicator | 350 visitors to the book's page a week, and the share of them who buy |
| Deadline and owner | Elena herself. Launch at month 4, first check at month 5, target income at month 14 |

Read it as a tired freelancer would. Every line is something she can start tomorrow. None of it says "be more visible".

Now do yours. Take the goal that worries you most. Give it fifteen minutes and one sheet:

1. **Write the levers.** Test each one: can I do this with my own hands tomorrow? "More clients" fails. "Letters to five former clients offering a repeat order" passes.
2. **Count the resources.** Hours per week, as a number. "When I have time" is not a resource.
3. **Write the constraints.** Ask in a calm hour what you will never do for this goal.
4. **Pick the leading indicator.** A number you can see every week, long before the deadline.
5. **Set the checkpoint.** Write the date of the first check and the result that sends you back to the page.

## The hours budget: where freelance burnout starts

Here is the step that turns five answers into a plan you can live with. A freelancer's scarcest resource is not money. It is hours.

Elena's book needed about ten hours a week for three months. She did not pretend they would appear. She found them:

| Source of the book's ten hours | Hours a week |
|---|---|
| On top of her usual fifty, within a written limit of 55 | 5 |
| Freed by turning down small orders | 5 |
| **Total for the book** | **10** |

The limit of 55 hours had an end date: months 1 to 3. After that, her plan bends the week back down to 35 by month 14.

Burnout is a budget nobody wrote down.

Now the part most plans miss. Your pages compete for the same evenings. Elena's income page also holds client levers: package prices, repeat-order letters to past clients, two new channels for finding clients. Each of those eats hours too.

So put every page's hours and money into one table. Two plans can look fine side by side and still claim the same Thursday night. Add the hours. If the total crosses your line, drop a lever or move a deadline. Never borrow the hours from sleep.

## Freelance pricing: write the constraints first

The constraints row protects you from yourself. In a bad month the mind offers quick, foolish moves. A written ban stops them faster than any argument at midnight.

Freelance pricing is where this bites hardest. Elena's price for her service packages is a lever. She may raise it, test it, rebuild packages around what sells. Undercutting is off the list, written down before the first slow week.

A discount wins the job this week. It also sets your rate for the next client who hears about it. That trade looks cheap on a Thursday and expensive by spring.

The same row guards your freelance clients and your name. Elena writes only to people who asked to hear from her. Former clients get a letter offering a repeat order. Strangers get nothing in their inbox.

## The usual plan and the one-page plan

Here is the same goal, written two ways. The usual column is an illustration; the second column is Elena's.

| | The usual freelance plan | One page per goal |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Grow income this year | At least €3,000 a month, three-month average, by month 14 |
| Actions | Post more, network, raise rates | Manuscript, sales page, mailing list, package prices |
| Hours | Whatever it takes | 10 a week for the book, 55 at most, ending at month 3 |
| Limits | None written | No undercutting, no cold emails |
| Check | End of the year | Every week: 350 page visitors and the share who buy |

The left column fails in the first rush job. It has no number, so the urgent email wins every time.

"I'm a one-person business. I don't need a plan." You don't need a folder. You need a page per goal. Three goals take under an hour. The hours table comes last.

## The cost of six more months without one

Run the numbers on Elena's starting point. Her average was €1,300 a month. Her goal was €3,000. That is €1,700 a month below the line.

Six more months of the January plan cost her €10,200 in income she had decided she needed. At fifty hours a week, those six months also hold 1,300 hours of work, for an average of €1,300 a month.

And the client who pays 70% keeps deciding what her evenings are for.

That is the price of a plan with no page for tonight. The work is all there. The steering isn't.

## What one page per goal does not fix

Look at what you now hold. Each goal has a page. The hours add up. You stop saying yes by reflex, and the week has a shape.

That treats the symptom. The cause sits one level deeper. A plan is one stage of a longer loop. And the move from client work to product income runs on its own clock.

Two steps close that gap:

- **The full control cycle, eight stages.** The plan is stage four. Two later stages carry it: structures (a payment service, a subscriber list, automated emails) and weekly observation. Skip them, and a page stays a page. Chapter 7.
- **Steady mode and manoeuvre.** Elena's switch runs in three periods, at the pace of the process, not the calendar. Judge the book after its first month, and you call a working plan a failure. Chapter 10.

The page sits inside the whole loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. It is one link, not the chain.

## FAQ

### What should a freelance business plan include?

One page for each goal, in order of priority. Each page names the levers, the hours and money, what you will not do, a weekly leading indicator, and a deadline with a first checkpoint. Then one table adds up the hours of all the pages.

### How can a freelancer make income more stable?

Measure your average over several months, not one good month. Then grow income that does not depend on one client or on your hours, such as packages, repeat orders or a product. Elena's plan for a stable €3,000 a month came from four sources by month 14.

### How do I avoid freelance burnout while growing?

Treat hours as a budget. Write a weekly limit, give any extra hours an end date, and free hours by turning down small work before you add new work. Elena allowed herself 55 hours only for three months, with 35 as the target.
