"A good profit." "Happy customers." "More time for the family." Every owner I know has written goals like these, and every one of them has felt the same quiet disappointment a few months later, when nothing has changed and nobody can say whether anything should have. The problem is not ambition. It is that a wish cannot be steered.

Chapter 1 of *Steer, Don't Guess* opens with a coffee shop owner named Andrew writing "growth and joy" at the top of his list, then crossing it out. What replaces it is the most practical lesson on how to set measurable goals I have read: six fields, a limit of nine, and a check that takes one minute. The whole chapter is in the free sample, which tells you something about how confident the author is in it.

## What are measurable goals, really?

A measurable goal is one where, on any given Monday, two different people looking at the same data would agree whether you are on track. That is a higher bar than most goal-setting advice sets. "Increase revenue" passes the usual tests if you add a number and a date, yet it still leaves the most important questions open: increase from where, measured how, and at what point should someone start to worry?

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> a goal is real only when six fields are filled in: the parameter, its definition and data source, the target value and deadline, the current value, the permissible deviation, and the check rhythm with the person responsible. An ordered list of such goals, at most nine, is called the goal vector.</div>

## How to set measurable goals: the six fields

Here is the template, in the book's order. Each field closes a gap the previous one leaves open.

| Field | The question it answers | What goes wrong without it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Parameter | What exactly do we measure? | Everyone measures something different |
| 2. Definition | How is it calculated, and where do the data come from? | Two numbers for the same thing |
| 3. Target and deadline | Where, and by when? | A direction, not a destination |
| 4. Current value | Where are we now? | "Without it, the goal remains a wish" |
| 5. Permissible deviation | Where does normal end? | Every wobble becomes a crisis, or none does |
| 6. Rhythm and owner | Who looks, and how often? | The number exists; nobody sees it |

Fields 4, 5 and 6 are the ones most templates skip, and they are exactly where plans quietly die. A target without a current value cannot tell you how far you have to go. A target without a permissible deviation cannot tell you when to act. And a number nobody checks is not a goal; it is decoration.

## A measurable goals example, filled in completely

The book gives one finished example, and it is worth reading slowly, because every clause does a job:

> Margin, meaning operating profit as a percentage of revenue, taken from the accounting system into which the till data are exported; 15% by the sixth month; currently 9%; once the goal is reached, no lower than 14% is permissible; Andrew checks it every week.

Notice the "currently 9%". Andrew had believed his margin was twenty per cent until he counted waste properly, a story told in [busy but not profitable](/blog/busy-but-not-profitable/). The current value field is where that kind of comfortable error gets caught. And notice "no lower than 14%": the goal knows in advance what a bad week looks like, so a single dip does not trigger panic and a slow slide does not go unnoticed.

Here is Andrew's full list after that night, with time counted in months from today (M6 means six months from now):

| No. | Goal | Value | Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cash reserve | at least 2.0 months of expenses | M12 |
| 2 | Margin | 15% | M6 |
| 3 | Regular customers | 40% of receipts | M12 |
| 4 | Quality | no more than 2 complaints a week | M3 |
| 5 | Owner's time | no more than 40 hours a week | M12 |

Writing it all down took him fifteen minutes. The order of the rows is not decoration either: it is his refusal order, what he would defend last and what he would give up first. If that idea is new to you, start with [how to set priorities when everything is urgent](/blog/set-priorities-refusal-order/).

## The one-minute test for any goal

My favourite passage in the chapter is four sentences long. "A good profit" has no number. "15" has no unit. "15%" has no deadline. "15% by the sixth month" is a goal.

That is the whole test: number, unit, deadline. Run it over your current list and you will usually find that half of it fails, not because the goals are wrong but because they were never finished.

## Goals vs methods: the mistake that hides in every list

Andrew's first rough list had eight items, and three of them were not goals at all: "launch loyalty cards", "hire a second barista", "buy a new coffee machine". Those are methods. For each he asked one question, *what for?*, and found the real goal underneath: loyalty cards are there to bring customers back (the goal is the share of regular customers); the barista and the machine are both there for quality.

This matters more than it seems. A goal answers "what do I want to achieve?"; a method answers "how will I do it?" Write a method into your goals and you commit to one path before you have even seen the map. Eight items became five, and the five were better.

## Six areas, nine goals at most

To keep the list from leaning to one side, the book works through six areas: finance, clients, sales, processes and quality, people, and development and the owner. You take zero to three goals from each, and no more than nine in total. An area may stay empty, but on purpose, with the reason written down.

The sixth area is the one most often forgotten: the owner's own time, health and money. The book is blunt about it: if you are building a business at the cost of your own life, that too is a goal, and better chosen consciously. And the nine is a design limit for a person working on paper, not a law of nature. In the book's words, for a person, ten priorities are no longer priorities. They are a list.

## Measurable goals for a freelancer

The same six fields work far from a coffee bar. Elena, a freelance designer in the book, writes three goals: a three-month average income of at least €3,000 a month by M14; her largest client's share of income no more than 30% by M14; no more than 35 working hours a week, except during the three months she spends writing her first online book, when up to 55 are allowed.

That last clause is goal setting at its most grown-up. The overtime is not a lapse; it is a decision made in advance, with an end date. When the long weeks come, Elena does not have to argue with herself. She already did, calmly, on paper.

## A SMART goals alternative, or a SMART goals upgrade?

If you have used SMART goals (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound), the six fields will feel familiar for the first three rows. The difference is what comes after the target: a current value, a permissible deviation and a named person with a rhythm. SMART tells you whether a goal is well written. The six fields tell you, every week, whether it is being met, and the refusal order tells you what to protect when two goals collide. I would not call it a replacement so much as the half of the job SMART leaves undone.

## Where this does not reach

Six fields make a goal measurable; they do not make it right, and they do not make a set of goals compatible. "The lowest prices, the best coffee and a thirty per cent margin" can pass every field and still be impossible as a set. The book handles that with a separate test, covered in [competing priorities vs conflicting goals](/blog/competing-priorities-conflicting-goals/). Legal, safety and contractual requirements also sit outside the goal list: they are constraints, not goals you can trade away.

## FAQ

### How do you set measurable goals?

Write six fields for each goal: what you measure, how it is calculated and where the data come from, the target and deadline, the current value, the permissible deviation, and who checks it and how often. Keep the list to nine goals or fewer.

### What is an example of a measurable goal?

"Margin, operating profit as a share of revenue from the accounting system: 15% by month six; currently 9%; no lower than 14% once reached; checked weekly by the owner." It has a number, a unit, a deadline, a starting point and a warning level.

### What is a good alternative to SMART goals?

The six fields of the Jirgenson Model keep what SMART does well and add a current value, a permissible deviation and a check rhythm, plus a refusal order that decides which goal wins when two collide.
