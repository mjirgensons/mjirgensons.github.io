It's three in the morning. Your partner is asleep with their back to you. You're doing next month's rent in your head for the fourth time, and the answer keeps changing.

Nearly half of owners lie awake at night worrying about the business.

At seven you get up with the same worry and less sleep. At dinner the phone lies face up next to your plate. Your daughter tells you about her day, and you hear the bank app.

Here's the irony. You do hard things all day. And you still can't say whether this month is fine.

That's not weakness. You can work. You carry every number in your head, with no system to hold them for you.

The Jirgenson Model gives you the answer. It is a control system that moves the numbers out of your head and onto one sheet, with a colour for each. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## Why the worry comes back every night

A worry is a number you can't see. You know it matters. You don't know its value today, or where "too low" begins. So your head checks it again, because nothing else does.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> a number with no data, old data or two different answers sits in the undefined zone. It is not green and it is not red. The only move there is to measure first. That is where the 3 a.m. worry lives.</div>

The cure for an undefined number is not willpower. It's a row on paper, a source, and a fixed time to look.

## How to stop worrying: the Monday sheet in six steps

Andrew runs a coffee shop with a queue at the counter and a thin bank balance. Here is the sheet he built, step by step, with his entries.

**1. Turn each worry into a row with a number.** Not "money", but "cash reserve in months of expenses". Andrew wrote five rows: cash reserve, margin, regular customers, complaints per week and his own hours. Keep it to five to start.

**2. Give each row one source.** One place the number comes from, and only one. Andrew's till sends a weekly export into a shared spreadsheet. Margin comes from the accounts. If the till says one thing and the accounts another, you get an argument instead of a number. If you need help here, [start with one number per source](/blog/business-stress/).

**3. Write the goal and today's value.** The error is the gap: now minus goal.

| Parameter | Goal | Now | Error | Zone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash reserve, months | 2.0 | 0.5 | −1.5 | 🔴 |
| Margin, % | 15 | 9 | −6 | 🟡 |
| Regular customers, % | 40 | 25 | −15 | 🟡 |
| Complaints per week | ≤ 2 | 5 | +3 | 🟡 |
| Hours per week | ≤ 40 | 65 | +25 | 🟡 |

**4. Draw the zones on a calm evening.** Andrew set his thresholds the same evening, while his head was clear. Never move them in the middle of a bad week.

| Parameter | 🟢 Normal | 🟡 Acceptable | 🔴 Emergency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash reserve, months | ≥ 2.0 | 1.0 to under 2.0 | < 1.0 |
| Margin, % | ≥ 14 | 5 to under 14 | < 5 |
| Regular customers, % | ≥ 36 | 20 to under 36 | < 20 |
| Complaints per week | ≤ 2 | 3 to 8 | > 8 |
| Hours per week | ≤ 42 | over 42, up to 70 | > 70 |

The red line sits where damage gets hard to reverse. For the reserve, Andrew put it at one month. A month gives him time to ask for a deferral, find money and decide. Learn the full method for drawing these lines in [the post on KPI zones](/blog/kpi-zones/).

**5. Fix the slot.** Monday, same time, two minutes. One minute to read the colours. One minute to write the single action. Andrew and Sara, his senior barista, ask three questions. Which numbers changed during the week? Which zone are they in now? What needs to be done about it?

**6. Obey the colours.** Red: act, and everything else waits. Yellow: find the cause while it is still cheap. Green: do nothing.

That's the whole tool. The first version takes about 20 minutes.

## What changed at Andrew's table

Andrew's first thought was loyalty cards. Win the customers back, right away.

The sheet said no. The reserve was red, and loyalty cards cost money: printing, discounts, setting up the till. That money would come out of a reserve already under a month.

He put the cards aside. For the first time, he felt no guilt about it. Fatigue hadn't made the decision. His own order had, written down with a clear head.

A month later the Monday briefing took fifteen minutes instead of an hour. There was nothing left to argue about. The figures showed everything. If you run a team, [the weekly review in three questions](/blog/meeting-cadence/) shows how the same sheet runs a short meeting.

Usual way: you check the bank app ten times a day and still don't know if you're safe. Model's way: you read five colours once a week and know exactly which row needs you.

## Peace of mind is a green row you leave alone

Green is the hardest colour for a worrier. Nothing is wrong, so you look for something to fix.

Don't. In the green zone, nothing needs to be done. This is the main guard against a costly habit: fixing what works. Control includes the ability to leave things alone.

So when a row is green, close the sheet. Green means breathe.

The relief doesn't come from perfect numbers. It comes from knowing which ones don't need you this week.

## Anxiety at night: what to do with the 3 a.m. worry

When a worry wakes you, ask one question: which row is it?

If it has a row, the row has a colour, and the colour already tells you what to do. If it's green, it's green at 3 a.m. too. If it has no row, write one line on paper by the bed. Give it a row on Monday, and [sort it into a zone](/blog/small-business-owner-stress/) with three questions.

Andrew had a bad month later on. The supplier raised prices by 8%, the coffee machine broke down, and a barista left. He wrote six lines. Reserve 1.56 months. Margin 12%. Nothing red: the reserve and the margin were yellow. One lever: prices up 5%. Check receipts and complaints in three weeks. Six lines, and the night grew calmer.

An instrument reading, not a verdict.

One line on health. The sheet handles business numbers. It does not treat anxiety. Worry that won't switch off for weeks is more than a bad week. So is broken sleep, or dread that stays on good days too. See your doctor. If you feel in danger, call your local crisis line now; in the US and Canada, you can call or text 988.

## Worrying about money: what six more months cost

Leave the worry in your head, and the numbers keep moving without you.

Andrew's margin sat at 9% against a goal of 15%. On €30,000 of monthly revenue, that is €1,800 of profit a month below the goal. Six more months of guessing: €10,800 that never reaches the reserve.

The reserve tells the same story. If nothing changes, Andrew reaches two months of reserve at M16, sixteen months from now, against a deadline of twelve. With the plan working, he gets there at M10. By the end of the third month without action, the reserve stands at about 0.8 months: red.

And every breakdown costs more while the margin is thin. At 9%, one breakdown costs about a month of time. At 15%, it costs 0.57. [Months of cash in reserve](/blog/cash-reserve-months-of-expenses/) is the number that decides how scary a broken machine is.

Behind those figures sits one person, still awake at three, checking a number nobody wrote down.

## "I have no time for a sheet"

You have time to worry. You spend it every night.

The basic start takes 30 minutes: five rows, one source each, zones, one day of the week. After that, the read takes two minutes. That's less than one round of checking the bank app.

Your business isn't too different. The same rows work for a coffee shop, a freelancer or a family budget. If decisions pile up when you're tired, [decide once, while calm](/blog/decision-fatigue-small-business/).

## Where the Monday sheet stops

The sheet treats the symptom: the worry with no place to go. It works from the first Monday.

But the cause runs deeper. Your thresholds still need rules that hold under pressure, and your sheet only shows the past. Two steps fix that.

The first is the threshold rules and the red protocol, in Chapter 4. They tell you where each line goes so it doesn't flicker, and what to do first when two rows turn red on the same morning. Without them, two reds bring the panic back.

The second is the two forecasts, in Chapter 8: "if nothing changes" and "if the plan works", with numbers and dates. They turn the fog of next month into lines on a chart. Without them, you are still worried about the future, only with better data about the past.

The sheet is one part of the whole loop: goals in order, the real state, the error, levers, a plan and a forecast. [How to forecast cash flow](/blog/forecast-cash-flow/) shows what the second step looks like from the outside.

## FAQ

### How do I stop worrying about money all the time?

Give each money worry a row with a number: cash reserve in months, margin, the bill due next. Fill it from one source and draw three zones. Then look once a week, not all day. Often the colour says there is nothing to do yet.

### How do I stop worrying about the future?

Turn the future into two lines with dates: what happens if nothing changes, and what happens if the plan works. A fog you can't measure keeps you awake. A forecast with a date gives you a checkpoint. The full method for the two forecasts is in Chapter 8 of Steer, Don't Guess.

### Is constant worrying a sign of anxiety?

Only a doctor can tell you that. Worry that lasts for weeks, breaks your sleep or stays even when things go well is a good reason to ask one. The Monday sheet helps with business chaos. It does not treat anxiety, and it doesn't replace care.
