Your kid's birthday. Cake, candles, eight children shouting in the living room.

You're in the hallway with your phone, watching a red position. Your partner sees you from the doorway and says nothing. That silence is louder than the party.

Here is the irony. You set the stop on a trade to the cent. Nobody ever set a stop on how much of the family's month goes into the account.

You're not lazy. You can read a chart for hours. What you don't have is a system. Every decision about money, family and risk sits in your head, and a tired head decides by hope.

Hope is the villain here. Not the market, and not you.

So, is trading gambling? The Jirgenson Model gives the answer as a control system. Decide the limits, the floor and the order before the bell. Then the hard afternoon meets a decision that already exists. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## Is trading gambling? It depends on what you wrote before the bell

The screen doesn't settle the question. Your rules do. A bet with no limit, no order and no stop works like gambling, whatever the chart shows. The same trade inside written limits is a decision.

The difference is made before the first click. If you don't decide in advance what matters more, fatigue will decide for you.

Think of a captain who loves two ports. He swings between them all night and reaches neither. You choose your port before you put to sea, not in the storm.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> every problem has four layers. Constraints are never given up. Minimums mark where the result stops being viable. Goals are ranked by the refusal order. A rule shares what is left. The order is yours to choose. The layers are not optional.</div>

## The four-layer sheet: the most you may lose, filled in

This is the tool. One sheet, twenty minutes, four layers from top to bottom. Each layer has an entry from the book beside it, then yours as an illustration.

The [day trading success rate](/blog/day-trading-success-rate/) post runs the refusal test on the trading account itself. This sheet starts one step earlier, at the kitchen table.

### Layer 1. Constraints: what is never on the table

A constraint is a line you never trade away: the law, a contract, safety, the load a person can carry. It isn't ranked with the goals. It sets the edge of the board before any goal gets a turn.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, keeps two of them. The taxes go out on time. And the machines get their maintenance, because skipping it buys cash today and a breakdown next month.

Your entries, as an illustration:

- The rent and the family's monthly budget never enter the trading account.
- No borrowed money in the account.
- The most you may lose, written in money: a sum the family can lose without anything changing at home.

When a goal and a constraint collide, the constraint wins. You look for another route, or you stop and change the conditions.

### Layer 2. Minimums: the floor where you stop

A minimum is the level below which the result is no longer viable. Write it as a number, not a feeling.

Andrew's entry: once his margin reaches 15%, it may never fall below 14%. That is his floor.

Your entry, as an illustration: the account balance at which trading stops for the month and you review on Sunday. A stop on one position protects one trade. This floor is a stop loss rule for the whole month, and for the people at home.

### Layer 3. Goals in refusal order

Now the refusal test. Imagine life takes your goals away one by one. Which do you let go first, and which do you hold until the end?

The last one left is goal number one. The test itself is laid out in [the refusal order for setting priorities](/blog/set-priorities-refusal-order/).

Alex is thirteen. He wants a €300 bicycle by 1 September, football twice a week and as much console time as he likes. There isn't time or money for all three. When football camp leaves him two free evenings a week, the choice turns easy. Games go first, football second, and the bicycle stays to the end.

| No. | Alex's goal | Value |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bicycle fund | €300 by 1 September |
| 2 | Football | twice a week |
| 3 | Games | no more than 10 hours a week |

Look at row 3. He didn't delete the games. He capped them. An order you will keep is worth more than a pretty one.

Your list, as an illustration: the family savings, evenings at home, your sleep, the trading account. Where does the account sit? If it sits above the savings, write that down honestly and read it in daylight.

Every goal needs a number, a unit and a deadline. Keep nine at most. Past that, a list of priorities is only a list.

Watch for one defect. The book calls it a violated order: a lower goal rescued at the cost of a higher one. Andrew buys expensive beans for "quality" while the reserve sits below its safe minimum. Topping up the account from the savings to win back a loss is the same defect, in a different room.

### Layer 4. A rule for the next euro

What is left after the first three layers gets shared by a rule: urgency, risk and cost.

Emma and Paul ended every month €400 short. They wrote three scenarios and chose the third: cut spending and add a side job. But they started with one lever, the cuts, so they could see what it did. They added the side job a month later.

Your rule, as an illustration: new money reaches goal number one before the account sees it. And one change at a time, so you know which change worked.

That is the whole tool. Writing his order down took Andrew fifteen minutes, and for the first time in two years he felt solid ground under his feet.

## Trading addiction or a bad month? Read the sheet, not the mood

A limit you set at the worst hour isn't a limit. It's a mood.

Here is the contrast, as an illustration. One trader, two Thursdays.

The hope Thursday: "One more trade and I'm back to even. I'll tell her when it's fixed." No floor, no order, no rule.

The sheet Thursday: "The floor is hit. The month is over. I review on Sunday, and the savings stay where they are." One look, and the decision is already made.

Can't stop when your own written line says stop? Hiding the losses from your family? That is more than a bad week. Talk to a doctor or a gambling helpline. They are the right help. The sheet treats the chaos around the money, not an addiction.

## Losing everything starts with one unwritten line

Look at Emma and Paul's money first. Savings of €2,000, and €400 short every month. If nothing changes, the savings run out in five months. With one lever, cutting €180, the gap falls to €220, and the savings last about nine months. One decision bought four months.

Andrew knows the other road. With no order, a tired yes to every request would have drained his cash within a month. The money would have gone before the time did, and nobody would have chosen it.

The human cost fits in one sentence. Every month without the sheet, the biggest money decision in your house gets made in a hallway, during a birthday, by hope.

## FAQ

### Is trading gambling or investing?

That depends less on the market than on your rules. Without a written limit, a floor and an order, any trade works like a bet. With them, each trade is a decision inside lines you set on a calm evening. The book gives no trading signals.

### What is a stop loss rule?

On a single trade, it is an order that closes the position at a price you set in advance. For your life, add a second one: a floor for the month, and a sum the family can lose without anything changing at home. Write both before the bell.

### How do I know if I have a trading addiction?

A sheet can't tell you, and neither can this post. If you trade past your own written limits, or hide it from your family, talk to a doctor or a gambling helpline. They are the right help. The four layers help with the money decisions around it.
