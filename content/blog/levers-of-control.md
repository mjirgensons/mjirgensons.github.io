"We need profit. We have to do something."

Andrew, the coffee shop owner in *Steer, Don't Guess*, says this at a morning briefing, staring at a red number. His senior barista, Sara, asks the only reasonable question: "Do what, exactly?" He does not know. They talk for ten minutes and part with the feeling that they have been discussing the weather.

If you have ever sat through that meeting, possibly as the person running it, this page is for you. It is about levers of control: the short list of things you can actually move, and the much longer list of things you only wish you could.

## Levers of control: the wheel and the heading

A helmsman does not turn the ship with his bare hands. He turns the wheel, and the heading follows. That image opens the chapter, and the whole lesson is inside it: between the wish ("sail north") and the result (the ship moving north) sits a mechanism within reach. You do not push on the result. You move the mechanism.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> a lever is a parameter you can reach and change by your own decision. The set of your levers is called the control vector. Everything that affects the result but is not yours to move (weather, foot traffic, the market's mood) is an environmental factor: you take it into account, you do not work on it.</div>

(A note for readers arriving from management accounting: Robert Simons' "Levers of Control" is a different, well-known framework about control systems in large organisations. This post is about the practical meaning of the phrase for an owner: the levers you personally can pull.)

## Business levers vs results: the mistake behind most "we must do something" meetings

The chapter's simplest table is also its most useful. On the left, what you want; on the right, what you can actually do about it:

| Result (what I want) | Lever (what I do) |
|---|---|
| Margin | Prices, menu, purchasing, portion size |
| New customers | Promotion channels, wording of the offer |
| Customer payment period | Payment terms, prepayment, reminders |
| Order lead time | Process steps, priority rules, capacity |

"We need profit!" and "We need sales!" are pushes on the left column. Nothing moves, because nothing on the left obeys you. You see the result; you move the lever. As long as the lever goes unnamed, you get ten minutes of talk about the weather.

## How to focus on what you can control: Andrew's line

The most satisfying scene in the chapter is a clean sheet of paper. Andrew writes down everything that comes to mind, then starts crossing out. The weather? An environmental factor. Rent and taxes? Given conditions. Foot traffic in the street? Not his lever. What remains is four lines:

- the prices of lattes and cappuccinos;
- the menu: drop the items that sell poorly and cost a lot;
- purchasing: talk to the milk supplier;
- portion size.

He rereads the list and feels noticeably calmer. Until then he had spent his nerves on everything at once: the rent, the weather, the customers' mood. Now he has four things he can tackle tomorrow morning. That, I think, is the most honest description of how to focus on what you can control that I have read in a business book. Not a mindset. A crossed-out list.

The same move works anywhere. Elena, a freelance designer, first wrote "more social media, increase sales, be more visible". Every item sounded businesslike; not one could be done tomorrow. Her second list: write three chapters, prepare the book's page, collect the first hundred addresses. Those are business levers. Alex, thirteen and saving for a bicycle, crossed out "get money for my birthday" when his mother pointed out it depended on his uncle.

## Three questions before you pull any lever

Not every lever is equally good. Before pulling one, ask three questions: Can I move it myself, today? How long before the effect shows? Can I undo it if it does not work? Here are Andrew's four levers through that filter:

| Lever | Myself, today? | When the effect comes | Rollback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prices | Yes | In weeks; customers take time to adjust | Hard: customers remember the jump |
| Menu | Yes | Quickly | Easy: an item can be brought back |
| Purchasing | Through negotiation | In a month, after a new contract | Hard: the contract is signed |
| Portion size | Yes | Immediately | Easy, but past a limit quality suffers |

The table tells you where to start: fast and reversible first, slow or irreversible later. It also explains something owners often get wrong: why the first honest check of a plan comes in the second month. Some levers simply cannot work sooner.

## Testing a lever in euros: will a 5% price rise pay off?

Here is where the chapter turns from good advice into a tool. Andrew's numbers: revenue €30,000, ingredients €9,000 (they move with customer numbers), other costs €18,300, profit €2,700, a margin of 9%. He raises prices by 5% and assumes 2% of customers leave.

| | Before | After +5% price, −2% customers |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | €30,000 | €30,870 |
| Ingredients | €9,000 | €8,820 |
| Other costs | €18,300 | €18,300 |
| Profit | €2,700 | €3,750 |
| Margin | 9% | about 12% |

One lever delivers about three of the six percentage points he needs. Then comes the better question: where is the limit? The rise pays off as long as fewer than 6.7% of customers leave. "Raise the margin" has become something you can test: how many customers will leave? And the test is cheap, because a leading indicator answers it early: the number of receipts in the first two weeks after the change.

## When there is no lever

Sometimes you write down a goal and cannot find a single lever. The book treats that as a signal with two possible meanings. Either the goal is worded so it cannot be controlled directly ("readers' trust" is not a lever; publications, examples and reviews are, and trust follows), or the lever exists and you have not spotted it. The question that finds it: what has to be done for this to happen, and who does it? If the honest answer is "nobody", no effort will solve the task, and you need to revisit the goal or who is responsible for it.

## Where this does not reach

A lever list tells you what you can move, not how hard to push or in what combination. Strength varies, effects take time, and causes other than your lever keep acting, which is why it pays to start with one lever at a time so you can see its effect. And when an environmental factor is stronger than all your levers combined, no list will save you; that is a different problem, covered in [why business plans fail](/blog/why-business-plans-fail/).

### Key takeaways

- Levers of control are actions you can take yourself; margin, sales and trust are results, never levers.
- Write everything down, then cross out weather, market, other people and luck. What is left is your control vector.
- Before pulling a lever ask: today, myself? When will it show? Can I undo it?
- Test a lever in numbers: a 5% price rise pays off as long as fewer than 6.7% of customers leave.

## The ten-minute exercise that ends the weather talk

Here is the exercise for anyone who feels stuck: pick one goal in trouble, write four or five actions you can take yourself in the next few days, then cross out everything that depends on others, the weather, the market or luck. It takes about ten minutes. What remains is short, a little humbling and enormously calming.

## FAQ

### What are levers of control in a small business?

They are the parameters you can change yourself, by your own decision: prices, menu, purchasing terms, payment terms, promotion channels, process steps. Results such as margin or sales are moved through them, never directly.

### How do you focus on what you can control?

Write down everything that affects your goal, then cross out what depends on other people, the weather, the market or luck. Work only on what remains, starting with the levers that are fast and easy to undo.

### How do I know if a lever is worth pulling?

Ask three questions: can I move it myself today, how soon will the effect show, and can I undo it? Then test it in numbers, such as how many customers you can lose before a price rise stops paying off.
