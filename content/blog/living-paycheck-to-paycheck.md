It's the 27th. The card gets declined at the grocery till, and your kid sees your face before you can fix it.

At home your partner checks the bank app in the bathroom, with the door locked. Nobody says the word "rent". Everybody hears it.

If you run a business, you know the other version. Half of all owners have gone months without paying themselves.

The month that never ends isn't a sign that you're bad with money. You can work. You earn, you pay, you juggle. You carry the whole month in your head with no system, and the head runs out before the money does.

The Jirgenson Model gives you the answer: a control system for the household, with goals in order, real numbers and one lever at a time. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What living paycheck to paycheck measures

Living paycheck to paycheck means each pay covers the bills until the next one, and nothing is left over. One broken car, and the month breaks.

So the real problem isn't the size of the pay. It's the size of the cushion. And you can measure the cushion tonight.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> the cushion is the reserve, measured in months of expenses: savings divided by monthly spending. A second number sits next to it: the months until the money runs out, savings divided by the monthly shortfall. They are different things, and you watch both.</div>

## The reserve in months, step by step

Take a family from the book. Emma and Paul bring home €4,000 a month. Half a year back, spending matched it. Then, in one quarter, spending climbed 10% to €4,400. In the account: €2,000. Each month leaves a €400 hole.

The first reaction was a quarrel. The second was six steps. Do them with your own numbers, next to theirs.

**1. Take the numbers from one source.** Use three months of bank statements, not memory. Emma and Paul found the trajectory there: expenses rising, income flat. One month would have hidden that.

**2. Count the reserve in months.** Savings divided by monthly expenses. Theirs: 2,000 / 4,400, about 0.45 months. They could live less than two weeks with no income at all. Their goal: at least 3 months. That puts the row in the red zone. [How many months of cash you need](/blog/cash-reserve-months-of-expenses/) explains where to set your own goal.

**3. Count the months until the money runs out.** Savings divided by the monthly shortfall. Theirs: 2,000 / 400 = 5 months. That's the clock. If expenses keep rising, it runs faster.

**4. Write three scenarios with numbers.**

| Scenario | Monthly result | What happens to the €2,000 |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Change nothing | −€400 | Gone in 5 months, sooner if spending rises |
| 2. Cut subscriptions and eating out by €180 | −€220 | Lasts about 9 months |
| 3. The same cut, plus a side job at €300 a month | +€80 | Grows every month |

One cut of €180 buys four more months. That is what a scenario table shows that a feeling can't.

**5. Decide the order before the month starts.** This is the refusal test, and step 6 depends on it. More on it in the next section.

**6. Pull one lever first.** Emma and Paul chose scenario 3. But they started with a single lever, the cut, to watch what it did. The side job came a month after that. Before you take one, [measure what a side hustle leaves you](/blog/side-hustle-income/). Two changes at once, and you never learn which one worked.

That's the tool. Six steps, one evening, one sheet. [A household budget in deficit](/blog/household-budget-deficit/) puts the same numbers into a full template if you want every row.

## How to pay bills in the right order

When the money won't cover everything, something decides what gets paid. Usually it's whoever calls the loudest.

The refusal test takes that power back. Imagine you're forced to give up your goals one at a time. Which would you give up first? Which second? Which would be the last one left? The last one standing is number one.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, ran this test on his own goals. His reserve got number one. Without it, the shop would close, and then nothing else on the list would matter. [The refusal test](/blog/set-priorities-refusal-order/) has the full method for a business.

Before the ranking, separate four layers:

1. **Constraints.** The law, contracts, safety. Taxes paid on time. These are not goals you rank. They decide what is allowed at all.
2. **Minimum levels.** Below them, life stops working.
3. **Goals.** Ranked by the refusal test.
4. **A rule for the rest.** Where leftover money goes, by urgency, risk and cost.

Here is an illustration for a family like Emma and Paul's. The rows are our example, not the book's:

| Layer | Entry |
|---|---|
| Constraints | Rent, loan payments, taxes |
| Minimums | Food, transport to work |
| Goal 1 | Reserve: at least 3 months |
| Goal 2 | Holiday fund |
| Rest | Eating out and subscriptions, only after goal 1's monthly step |

How to pay rent when money is tight: rent sits in the constraints, before the goals. If even the constraints don't fit, that's a red row. Stop, call the landlord or the lender early, and change the conditions. A [debt payoff plan](/blog/debt-payoff-plan/) keeps old debt from growing while you do.

## Running out of money: what six more months cost

Do nothing, and the €400 gap does the deciding.

Six more months at −€400 is €2,400. Emma and Paul have €2,000. So the savings are gone after five months, and month six starts below zero.

The cut alone changes that clock from 5 months to about 9. The cut and the side job stop the leak.

The human cost doesn't show on the statement. It's the same fight on the 27th, every month, in a quieter voice.

## Stress about money at night

Money worry is loudest at 3 a.m., when you have no numbers and no lever in reach. The reserve row and the clock give the worry a place. "0.45 months, 5 months, one lever this week" is easier to sleep on than "we're broke".

One line on health. The sheet handles money. It does not treat anxiety. If the stress about money breaks your sleep for weeks, or low mood stays on good days too, see your doctor. In a crisis, call your local crisis line; in the US and Canada, you can call or text 988.

## Where the reserve line stops

The tool stops the panic. You know your reserve, your clock and your first lever. That's a lot for one evening.

But it treats the symptom: this month. The cause is that nothing yet connects today to the goal. At +€80 a month, three months of reserve sits a long way off. Three months at €4,400 of expenses is €13,200. From €2,000, at €80 a month, that takes more than ten years.

Two steps close that gap. The first is the backward move, in Chapter 15. You start from the goal and the date, then walk back to this month's checkpoint. It shows how many levers the goal needs. The second is the two forecasts, in Chapter 8: "if nothing changes" and "if the plan works", with dates. They warn you before the next shock, not after. Without them, you're steering this month and guessing at the year.

The reserve row is one part of the whole loop: goals in order, the real state, the error, levers, a plan and a forecast. A [sinking fund](/blog/sinking-fund/) shows one more piece of it: saving for a bill you know is coming.

## FAQ

### How do I stop living paycheck to paycheck?

Measure your reserve in months and the months until it runs out. Write three scenarios, rank your goals with the refusal test, and pull one lever at a time. The first goal is a reserve, even a small one, because it turns the next shock from a crisis into a bill.

### How do I pay bills when I'm running out of money?

Pay the constraints first: rent, loan payments, taxes. Then the minimums, like food and transport to work. Then your goals in the order you chose on a calm day. If the constraints alone don't fit, call the lender or landlord early, before the date.

### How many months of expenses should I save?

It's your choice, made on a calm day. Emma and Paul set at least 3 months. Andrew's coffee shop set 2, with the red line at 1 month. Put the red line where damage gets hard to reverse. Steer, Don't Guess shows how to set all three zones.
