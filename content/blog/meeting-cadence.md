Andrew's Monday meeting ran an hour. Nobody walked out knowing what to do.

He talked about the slow week. Sara, his senior barista, talked about the Friday rush. The till had the answer the whole time, and nobody opened it.

Picture your own Monday. Coffee, a long table, everyone's view of last week. An hour later, the one decision on the table is to meet again next Monday.

Here is the irony. The meeting about the numbers is the one place where nobody looks at the numbers. The loudest voice sets the agenda, and opinion fills the hour.

The answer to that comes from the Jirgenson Model: four meetings, each with one question and one sheet to read. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What a meeting cadence is

A meeting cadence, or operating rhythm, is the fixed schedule of recurring meetings in a business. It says which meeting happens how often, who comes and what it decides.

The idea is not new. EOS, the Entrepreneurial Operating System that Gino Wickman describes in *Traction*, is known for its meeting rhythm for small businesses. The Jirgenson Model keeps the rhythm and adds the reason behind each meeting: which part of the control loop it watches.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> meetings are stage six of the full control cycle: observe and coordinate. Each meeting looks at one layer of the system, at that layer's own speed, and answers one question. A meeting with no question is a conversation.</div>

Skip that stage, and someone else performs it for you. Stop observing your numbers, and your debts start observing you.

## The meeting calendar: four meetings, four questions

Here is the whole cadence on one page. Copy it, then fill in the last column with your own names and days.

| Meeting | How often | What it reads | The one question | Andrew's version |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lever check | Every week | Leading indicators | Are they moving the right way? | Monday, with Sara, at the shared sheet |
| Gap review | Weekly for fast numbers, monthly for slow ones | The traffic-light dashboard | Which numbers are yellow or red? | Complaints and receipts on Monday; reserve and margin once a month |
| Plan review | Every quarter | The one-page plans and the forecast | Is the plan working? Did the forecast match the facts? | Once a quarter, and whenever the forecast breaks |
| Goal review | Every six months or once a year | The goal list and its order | Are the goals and their order right? | At the half year and at year end |

Read the rows from fast to slow. The weekly meetings steer. The quarterly one corrects the plan. The yearly one checks the port you are steering toward.

## The weekly business review in three questions

The Monday meeting is where the hour goes, so fix it first. Andrew's first Monday at the spreadsheet came down to three questions.

| Question | Where the answer comes from | Andrew's first Monday |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Which numbers changed this week? | The weekly till export in the shared sheet | Five rows: reserve 0.5 months, margin 9%, regular customers 25%, complaints 5 a week, his hours 65 a week |
| 2. Which zone is each one in now? | The colours | Reserve red; the other four yellow |
| 3. What do we do about it? | A two-line list: action and owner | An illustration from his plan: drop the two most loss-making items (Andrew); loyalty cards wait while the reserve is red (agreed) |

That is the weekly review template. Three questions, three sources, one short list.

The till answered the first question, the colours the second, the list the third. Everything they used to argue over now sat on the screen.

A month in, the Monday briefing took fifteen minutes. It used to take an hour.

Three rules keep it that short:

1. **Open the sheet before anyone speaks.** The first voice in the room belongs to the till.
2. **Talk about red and yellow rows only.** Green rows get no airtime.
3. **End with names.** Every action on the list has an owner and a day.

## The monthly business review template

Slow numbers move too little in a week to read. The cash reserve in months and the margin get their look once a month.

The monthly review puts two lines next to the real figure. One line shows where the plan says you should be. The other shows where you would be if nothing changed.

| Month | Plan line, reserve in months | "Change nothing" line |
|---|---|---|
| M1 | 0.60 | 0.60 |
| M2 | 0.72 | 0.70 |
| M3 | 0.86 | 0.80 |

At month 3 the two lines sit 0.06 apart. Small, and easy to miss. The monthly review asks the one question: which line is the shop on?

The margin works the same way. Andrew's margin plan had a revision line: below 10% at month 2, rewrite the plan. At month 2 the margin read 10.5%. Above the line, so the plan stayed.

## The quarterly business review meeting

The quarterly meeting asks whether the plan itself still holds. It compares the forecast with the facts and changes the plan when they part ways.

A plan review also runs when the forecast breaks. Andrew's hardest one came after month 8. A supplier raised prices by 8%, a repair ate part of the reserve and a barista left. The margin fell from 15% to 12%. On the table were three versions of the next four months:

| Option | Margin from M9 | Reserve at M12 |
|---|---|---|
| Change nothing | 12% | 2.11 months |
| Prices +5% | 15% | 2.27 months |
| Prices +5% and a part-time barista | 13.5% | 2.18 months |

All three reach the two-month goal. The review is where you see the difference in room for error, and choose with numbers instead of mood.

## The goal review, and when the cadence changes

Once or twice a year, the question turns to the goals themselves. Are they still right? Is the order still right?

Andrew's reserve passed 2.0 months at month 11 and stayed there at month 12: 2.09, then 2.27. Once it holds for three months in a row, that goal moves into maintenance mode. Its meeting slows down: a monthly check instead of a weekly one. The hours it used to take go to the next goal.

The rhythm speeds up too. In a cash crisis, the bank balance gets a daily look. A cadence is a starting frame, not a law. The speed of the number sets it.

## One on one meetings: the same sheet, two people

In a small business, the weekly review often is a one on one meeting. Andrew and Sara were two people at one spreadsheet.

The sheet changes what a one on one is for. It stops being a mood check and turns into a place where disagreement becomes a rule.

- Sara read Andrew's plans and asked: "You'll raise prices and launch discount cards. Don't those two contradict each other?" They did. Andrew added a rule: card discounts start only once the margin reaches 14%.
- Sara asked why loyalty cards had to wait. Andrew pointed at the red row. Regular customers drift away slowly, from the yellow zone. A red reserve can end everything fast.

Neither talk took long. Both ended with a decision written down.

## What a missing cadence costs

Start with money. Andrew's plan puts his reserve at 2.41 months of expenses by month 12. If nothing changes, it sits at 1.69, still in the yellow. Without a monthly look, nothing tells you which line you are on until the year is gone.

Then the hours. An hour every Monday against fifteen minutes is 45 minutes a week. Over a year that is 39 hours: close to a full week of the 40-hour week Andrew was fighting for. He was working 65.

Then the human cost. The owner without a cadence learns about the problem from the bank.

"We're too small for meetings." Andrew's meeting was two people and a shared sheet. The till sent a weekly export. No software, no consultants.

## Where the cadence stops

The cadence treats one symptom: meetings that end without decisions. With four questions and one sheet, every meeting ends with an owner and a day. That works from next Monday.

The cause sits one layer deeper. A meeting reads colours, and colours are only as good as the lines between them. Guess where yellow ends, and the meeting debates a guess. And every meeting in this calendar looks back at a week that is already over. It reacts after the deviation.

Two steps close that gap:

- **Zone thresholds and the red protocol.** Where each number turns yellow and red. And the order of action when several rows go red in one week. Chapter 4.
- **Control by forecast.** The three ways to steer, by plan, by deviation and by forecast, and how the Monday meeting acts before the damage. Chapter 9.

Without them, the cadence tells you when to meet. It does not yet tell you what each colour means, or what is coming next month.

The calendar is the heartbeat of the whole loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast, each reviewed at its own speed. How often each single number needs a look is its own small calculation, set out in [how often to review KPIs](/blog/how-often-to-review-kpis/).

## FAQ

### What is a good meeting cadence for a small business?

Four meetings. A weekly lever check. A gap review, weekly for cash and monthly for margin and reserve. A quarterly plan review. A goal review every six to twelve months. Each answers one question from one shared sheet.

### What should a weekly business review cover?

Three questions. Which numbers changed this week? Which zone is each one in? What do we do about it, and who owns it? In a small coffee shop, that took fifteen minutes. *Steer, Don't Guess* sets out the full meeting calendar with the tools behind it.

### What should a one on one meeting cover?

In a small business, the same sheet as the weekly review. Look at the red and yellow rows, settle any clash between plans, and write the decision down as a rule with an owner.
