It's your son's birthday. The cake is on the table, the candles are lit, and you're smiling for the photo.

In your head, you're answering the supplier. You're moving Friday's shift. You're counting whether the card payment clears before rent.

Later your partner says it quietly: "You weren't there tonight."

Four out of ten owners have hidden business stress from their own family.

You didn't hide it to lie. You hid it because you had nowhere to put it. It lives in one place only: your head.

That's the turn. You can work. Hard work isn't the problem. The problem is that you carry it all, alone, with no system to carry it with you.

The Jirgenson Model gives you the answer. It is a control system that moves the load from your head onto paper, with a name next to each task. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What is mental load?

Mental load is the thinking work behind every task. Noticing what's needed. Weighing the options. Deciding. Checking that it got done. The term started with households, where one partner can end up remembering everything for both. In your business, that person is you.

The hands-on work gets shared. The thinking doesn't. So the owner who delegates the till still carries the till in their head.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> every part of control becomes an object you can pick up: a table, a source, a dashboard, a list. Until that object exists, the part does not work. A task held only in your head is not delegated. It is postponed.</div>

That's the mental load meaning in one line: work that has no object yet.

## Mental load definition, in owner terms: levers with no names

You can't hand over a worry. You can hand over a lever.

A lever is something you can change by your own decision: a price, a menu, a supplier contract. A result is what follows: profit, sales, calm. It's tempting to push on the result ("we need profit!") and carry the levers in your head, unnamed.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, tried this at a morning briefing. "We need profit. We have to do something," he told Sara, his senior barista.

"Do what, exactly?" she asked.

He didn't know. They talked for ten minutes and parted with the feeling they'd discussed the weather.

## How to put the mental load on paper in six steps

Here is the tool: a list of levers with the people responsible. It takes about 20 minutes. Andrew's entries sit next to each step.

**1. Empty your head onto one sheet.** Write everything you carry. Don't sort yet. Andrew took a clean sheet and wrote down everything that came to mind.

**2. Cross out what you can't move.** Weather, the market and other people's moods are not levers. Andrew crossed out the weather, rent and taxes, and foot traffic in the street. Four levers remained: the prices of lattes and cappuccinos, the menu, purchasing (talk to the milk supplier) and portion size.

He reread the short list and felt noticeably calmer. Until then he'd spent his nerves on everything at once. Now he had four things he could tackle the next morning. [Find the levers that move your numbers](/blog/business-levers/) if your list stays long.

**3. Ask three questions of each lever.** Can I move it myself, today? When does the effect show? Can I undo it if it fails?

| Lever | Myself, today? | When the effect comes | Rollback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prices | Yes | In weeks | Hard: customers remember the jump |
| Menu | Yes | Quickly | Easy: an item can come back |
| Purchasing | Through negotiation | In a month, after a new contract | Hard: the contract is signed |
| Portion size | Yes | Immediately | Easy, up to the point where quality suffers |

Start with what is fast and reversible. Leave the slow, hard ones for when you can watch them.

**4. Write one name next to each lever.** One person moves it and reports on it. Ask the plain question: "What has to be done, and who does it?" If the answer is "nobody", no effort will fix it. Change the goal or the level of responsibility. If you aren't sure who should take a lever, [pick the person who sees furthest ahead](/blog/delegating-to-the-right-person/).

Here is an illustration of Andrew's sheet with names. Who owns which lever is our example:

| Lever | Person responsible | Next check |
|---|---|---|
| Prices | Andrew | Margin, monthly |
| Menu | Andrew | Weekly sales by item |
| Purchasing | Andrew | When the new contract starts |
| Portion size | Sara | Complaints, weekly |

**5. Give every number one source and one owner of the calculation.** Two sources mean two answers, and an argument. One number, one source, one person who calculates it.

**6. Put the list on a sheet two people can see.** Andrew set up a shared spreadsheet that he and Sara could both open. The till sent a weekly export into it. No expensive programs. A month later the Monday briefing took fifteen minutes instead of an hour. There was nothing left to argue about.

That's the whole tool. You still lead. You no longer carry it all.

## Always working? Put your own hours on the list

There is one row the owner forgets: the owner.

Andrew's goal list had five rows. The last one read: owner's time, no more than 40 hours a week. He was working 65. When he ran the refusal test, he put his own time in last place. He had known for ages that he was tired. He had never said out loud that he'd give up rest for the business. Now it was on paper.

That honesty matters. Working too much by default is a slide. Working too much by a written decision, with an end date, is a plan. [How to prioritize your life without putting yourself last](/blog/how-to-prioritize-your-life/) shows the same test for the whole of your life.

And among Andrew's first wishes was this one: at least one Sunday without a phone in his hand. That is a goal too. It deserves a row.

## Stress and family: the same list at home

The load at home works the same way. Emma and Paul earn €4,000 a month. Their expenses rose to €4,400, and their savings stand at €2,000. They end up €400 short every month.

At first they quarrelled. Then they put the numbers on paper, from three months of bank statements. They found two levers: cut subscriptions and eating out by €180, and add a side job worth €300 a month. They pulled one lever first, to see its effect, and added the second a month later.

The fight became a sheet both could read. [A couples budget on one shared page](/blog/couples-budget/) walks through that page line by line.

## What carrying it alone costs in six months

Keep the load in your head for six more months, and the numbers keep moving.

Andrew's reserve stood at 0.5 months against a goal of 2.0. In money, that gap is €40,950. His margin was 9% against 15%: €1,800 of profit a month below the goal. Six months of that is €10,800.

If nothing changes, the reserve reaches its goal at M16, sixteen months from now. With the plan working, it gets there at M10. And Andrew keeps working 25 hours a week over his limit, every week of that wait.

Without the sheet, all the clarity he had gained would have stayed in his head and on napkins. In the first tense month, fatigue would have been making the decisions again.

The Sunday without the phone never comes. That's the human price.

One line on health. Burnout from work is real, and the signs of burnout are a matter for a doctor, not a spreadsheet. If exhaustion, low mood or broken sleep last more than a bad week, see your doctor. The model treats the chaos in the business, not an illness. ## "I'm a lonely entrepreneur. There's nobody to hand it to."

Then the list matters even more. Paper is the first person you hand it to.

A solo owner's levers still get names: "me, Monday" or "me, after the contract". The task lives on the sheet, with a date, not in your head at three in the morning. [Running a one person business](/blog/one-person-business/) starts with the same honest list.

## Where the list of levers stops

The list takes the weight off your head. It works from tonight.

But it treats a symptom. The cause is deeper: the people on your list still don't know when to act without asking you, or what they must never do. So the load comes back as questions.

Two steps fix that. The first is the zones and the red protocol, in Chapter 4. They tell everyone, in advance, when a number needs action and what comes first when two numbers go red. The second is the one-page plan for each goal, in Chapter 6, with its constraints: what we will not do, even under pressure. Without them, every lever owner still phones you.

The list is one part of the whole loop: goals in order, the real state, the error, levers, a plan and a forecast. [A management system in a week](/blog/management-system-small-business/) shows how the parts fit together.

## FAQ

### What is mental load at work?

It's the planning, remembering and checking behind every task, carried by one person. At work it usually sits with the owner or manager. You lower it by putting each lever on paper with a person responsible and a check date.

### How do I stop carrying the weight of everything alone?

Write down everything you carry, cross out what you can't change, and give each remaining lever one name. Then share the sheet and look at it once a week. The full set of tools, from the goal list to the forecasts, is in Steer, Don't Guess.

### Is working too much a sign of burnout?

Long hours alone are not a diagnosis. Exhaustion that lasts, low mood or broken sleep are reasons to talk to a doctor. On the business side, put your hours on your goal list with a limit and an end date for any overload.
