<strong>One podcast in four stops at exactly one episode.</strong>

You recorded the pilot on a Saturday. You edited it on Sunday, published it on Monday and sent the link to everyone you know. Episode two is still a folder with "draft" in its name.

Nobody decided to stop. The show stopped by itself, one quiet week at a time.

Here is the irony. You chose the microphone with care. You never wrote down what would make you stop the show.

Shows don't die from one bad episode, they fade because nobody wrote rules for a slow week. The Jirgenson Model answers that as a control system: a real goal, a plan, a corridor. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## Podcast statistics and episode one: name the launch a manoeuvre

Call your new show what it is: a manoeuvre. Then measure it in the show's own time, not in your patience.

A ship holding a straight course is in steady mode. The goal stays the same, the waves rock it, and the helmsman makes small turns of the wheel. When the captain heads for another port, the ship turns. That turn is a manoeuvre.

A new show is a turn. You move from "no show" to "a show with listeners", and the goal travels with you.

A manoeuvre runs on its own clock. If a new shop takes eight months to pay for itself, it makes no sense to demand a result within a month. The same holds for episode one.

How to avoid the one-episode show: before you record, write how long the turn takes by the laws of the process, not by your wishes.

## The manoeuvre card for a new show, filled in

Here is the whole tool. Five lines on one page, each with a worked entry from Elena, Andrew or Alex.

**1. Three periods.** Exit from the old mode, movement at constant speed, entry into the new mode.
Elena, a freelance designer, used this for her first online book. Months 1 to 3: she exits the old mode and writes the book, about 120 hours. Months 4 to 9: the book launches, visits grow to 1,400 a month, sales reach 21. Months 10 to 14: she enters the new mode, with income from three sources and her week back to 35 hours.

**2. Launch conditions.** The measurable event that starts the turn. Not a date.
Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, wanted a second location. His condition: the cash reserve holds at two months for three months in a row. Nothing in the red zone before a growth manoeuvre starts.

**3. The corridor.** How far you may stray from the planned line at each checkpoint, in value and in time.
Elena's corridor allowed up to 55 hours a week while writing. Andrew's corridor: his cash reserve never below 1.0 month, along the whole route.

**4. The review rhythm.** Who looks at what, and how often.

| What | How often | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Levers | Every week | Are the leading indicators moving the right way? |
| Error | Weekly for fast numbers, monthly for slow ones | Which numbers are in the acceptable or emergency zone? |
| Plan | Every quarter | Is the plan working? Did the forecast match the facts? |
| Goal | Every six months or once a year | Are the goals and their order right? |

**5. Stop, sell or revise conditions.** Written in advance, while you're calm.
The data may show a goal is out of reach, or that its price beats the benefit. Write what reading would tell you so, before the first episode.

That is the card. It fits on one page.

## Past episode three: hold the corridor, not the mood

<strong>Almost half of all podcasts never make it past episode three.</strong>

Draw the planned line for your first episodes, then a corridor around it. Check the show against the corridor, not against your mood on Tuesday night.

Alex shows how this works. He is saving €300 for a bicycle, and his planned line says €170 by 1 July. On 1 July he has €140.

He doesn't change the goal to "a cheaper bicycle". An error of €30 is a reason to change the plan, not the goal. He puts his scooter up for sale at once, without waiting for August. By 1 August he has €250, €15 above the line.

Do the same with your show. Episodes behind the line mean a new lever: a shorter format, a fixed recording slot, a guest list booked ahead. The goal stays.

As long as you stay inside the corridor, you control the manoeuvre. Drop out of it and you need either a controlled return or a halt. You decide which, on paper.

How to avoid the fade: put the planned line and the corridor on the card before episode one. Then episode three is a check, not a verdict.

## Podfade after episode one: write the stop rules first

<strong>The biggest drop-off isn't at episode seven. It's right after episode one.</strong>

Write the conditions for stopping before you record. Then the morning after episode one is a reading, not a mood.

Andrew learned why the condition comes first. He came back from viewing premises with a gleam in his eye and six points sketched on a sheet. He checked the panel instead. Nothing was red, but the reserve stood at 1.35 months, short of two.

So he wrote the condition and no date. The system itself would set the date: three monthly readings in a row at 2.0 or more.

Sara asked, "So we wait?" "No," Andrew replied. "We do whatever brings the condition closer."

A show needs both kinds of rule: when it starts, and when it stops. A written stop rule protects the show from a bad week. A missing one lets a bad week stop the show.

If your stop rule does fire, the next question is whether to quit the idea at all. That test is in [when to give up on a business idea](/blog/when-to-give-up-on-a-business-idea/).

How to avoid the silent stop: decide tonight which reading would make you stop. If no reading would, keep recording.

## How often to blog: set the rhythm before the content

<strong>Two out of three blogs go silent within two months.</strong>

Fix the review rhythm first. Content consistency follows the rhythm, not the other way round.

Use the table on the card. Look at your levers every week: did you record, write, publish? Look at the error weekly for fast numbers and monthly for slow ones. Look at the plan every quarter, and at the goal every six months.

The rhythm depends on how fast things change and how soon damage sets in. A blog that posts weekly needs a weekly look at the levers. A podcast with monthly episodes needs a monthly look at the error.

A missed week then shows up on Monday, while it costs one post. Without the rhythm, it shows up in month three, when the blog has already gone quiet.

How to avoid the two-month silence: book the weekly review before you book the next post.

## What a stop after one month costs

Run Elena's numbers. By month 14 her book sells 21 copies a month at €29: €609 before commissions. It keeps working without her daily involvement.

Had she judged the launch after its first month, she would have concluded the book didn't work. She would have given up about €609 a month, or €3,654 over six months. And 120 hours of writing would have gone in the drawer.

Starting too early has its own price. Andrew's second location needs about €45,000, 1.8 months of expenses. With a reserve of 1.35 months, there would not have been enough money even for the opening.

A show pays the same way, in hours and in listeners who never hear episode two.

And the folder of unpublished episodes sits on your desktop for years.

## Where the manoeuvre card does not reach

The card you wrote is complete. It names the periods, the start, the corridor, the rhythm and the stop rules. It treats the symptom: quitting on a mood.

The cause sits one level deeper. The card doesn't tell you which milestone must stand at which month for the goal to arrive on time. And when the plan is right, the launch was timed well and still nothing comes, the card can't tell you where the hidden error sits.

Steady mode and manoeuvre are one link in the loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. Two more steps close the gap.

- **The backward move.** From the goal back to this week's first action, which also sets when the manoeuvre starts. Chapter 15.
- **The three offsets.** Three hidden errors that break even a good plan: in the goal, the starting point or the environment. Chapter 11.

Without them, the card keeps the show alive. It doesn't yet tell you if the show is heading for the right port.

## FAQ

### Why do podcasts fail so early?

Look for the missing card before you doubt your voice or your topic. Without written periods, a corridor and stop rules, one bad week can end a show. Write those five lines before episode one.

### How many podcast episodes should I plan before launch?

Plan the first period of the manoeuvre, not a number pulled from the air. Write how long the exit from the old mode takes, the planned line of episodes and the corridor around it. Then check the line every week.

### What are the best podcast tips for consistency?

Book the recording slot and the weekly review before the topic. Write a stop rule while you're calm. The book's chapter on steady mode and manoeuvre shows the full card, with worked numbers.
