You know the bill is coming. You even know the month. And it still lands like a surprise.

The insurance renewal. The new van. The deposit on a lease. You saw each one months away. Then it arrives, and the money [went into last month's groceries](/blog/where-does-money-go/).

Here is the irony. You plan a two-week holiday down to the airport transfer. The one bill that could sink the year gets a sticky note and a hope.

A known bill is not a risk. It's a missed plan.

The answer to this pain comes from the Jirgenson Model: [work backwards](/blog/backward-planning/) from the bill to a number you can check today. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What is a sinking fund?

A sinking fund is money you put aside in regular amounts for one known future cost. When the bill comes, the cash is already there. Nothing is borrowed, and [the emergency savings](/blog/cash-reserve-months-of-expenses/) stay untouched.

One note on the sinking fund meaning. In corporate finance, the same name means money a company sets aside over time to repay a bond or a loan. This post is about the household and small business sense: a pot for a bill you can see coming.

The usual advice stops at one division. Take the amount, divide by the months, set up a transfer. That is a wish with a direct debit.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> a sinking fund is a chain of conditions worked back from the bill. Each checkpoint says what must be in the fund by then for the remaining flows to still reach the target. Miss a checkpoint and you know today, not on the due date.</div>

## The backward method, step by step

Here is the tool. Five steps, each filled in with a real case: Andrew, the coffee shop owner in [Steer, Don't Guess](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0HPCJCWKF), saving for a second location.

The numbers below are the rough, simplified estimate he made at month 12 (M12). Revenue €30,000 a month. Margin 15%.

### 1. Write the end state, precisely

Not "save for the new place". An amount, a date and what the money pays for.

Andrew's end state: €45,000 for the second location, saved from the profit of M13 to M24.

### 2. Write today's state, precisely

Count what is in the pot today, from one source. A wrong starting point sends a perfect plan to the wrong place.

Andrew starts the fund at zero at M13. His reserve must stay at two months of expenses or above, so the fund is a separate pot.

### 3. Write the rule of each month

What comes in, what goes out, and when that changes. This is the step the simple division skips.

Andrew's rule: a 15% margin on €30,000 brings in €4,500 a month. From M21 he hires a manager for the first location at €2,000 a month. So M13 to M20 add €4,500 each. M21 to M24 add only €2,500 each.

### 4. Walk backwards to checkpoints

Start at the bill. At each checkpoint ask one question: what must be in the fund now for the months still ahead to close the gap?

| Checkpoint | Minimum in the fund | How it is worked out |
|---|---|---|
| M24 | €45,000 | The target |
| M20 | €35,000 | €45,000 minus 4 months × €2,500 |
| M18 | €26,000 | €35,000 minus 2 months × €4,500 |
| M15 | €12,500 | €26,000 minus 3 months × €4,500 |
| M12 | Below zero | €12,500 minus 3 months × €4,500 = minus €1,000 |

The last row is the good news. Starting from zero, Andrew has €1,000 of room. The plan line runs €13,500 at M15, €27,000 at M18, €36,000 at M20 and €46,000 at M24. That is €1,000 above the minimum at every checkpoint.

One thousand euros on a €45,000 goal is thin. One repair could eat it. Now Andrew knows that at M12, not at M24.

### 5. Check the fund against the line

At every checkpoint, compare the real balance with the minimum. Above it, carry on. Below it, the chain no longer closes. Add a lever, cut a cost or move the date, and decide now.

That is the whole tool. Amount and date, start, monthly rule, backward checkpoints, a monthly check.

Too much maths for [a family pot](/blog/couples-budget/)? It is subtraction. A sheet of paper and twenty minutes. A car repair, a school trip or a tax bill fits the same five steps.

## Why the simple division fails

Run Andrew's case the usual way, as an illustration.

| The usual way | The backward way |
|---|---|
| €45,000 / 12 = €3,750 a month | A minimum balance for each checkpoint |
| Assumes every month looks the same | Shows that M21 to M24 bring in €2,000 less each |
| Assumes the money will be there | Ties the inflow to the margin that produces it |
| Finds out on the due date | Finds out at the first missed checkpoint |

€3,750 a month looks safe against €4,500 of profit. But the transfer depends on the margin. At a 9% margin, the shop makes €2,700 a month. The neat monthly figure fails from the first month, and the spreadsheet still shows it as planned.

The habit is the villain here. We plan forwards, from today, with an average. The options branch like an avalanche, and the average hides the one month that breaks the chain. [Walk backwards](/blog/backward-planning-template/), and each step asks the same question. What must be true right before this?

## What a checkpoint tells you that a balance doesn't

The same chain works on any pot, not only cash for a project. Andrew ran it on his reserve. His goal was a reserve of 2.0 months of expenses at M12. Walking back from it, he needed at least 1.29 months at M8.

At M8 he had a bad month: the supplier raised prices by 8%, a repair hit, a barista left. The reserve stood at 1.56. That is 0.27 above the line. Bad month, still on course.

Now the same check with the margin stuck at 12%, [the "change nothing" case](/blog/forecast-cash-flow/). The requirement at M8 rises to 1.45. The room shrinks to 0.11. One more breakdown would eat almost all of it.

A balance tells you how much you have. A checkpoint tells you how much room is left. Only the second one lets you sleep.

## The cost of six more months of hoping

Here is what an unchecked fund costs, with Andrew's own numbers.

If nothing changes and the margin stays at 9%, the shop makes €2,700 a month. Twelve months from M13 to M24 bring in €32,400. Take away the manager's €8,000 and the fund holds €24,400. The second location needs €45,000. He would be €20,600 short, with a little over half the money.

And the reserve itself would not reach two months until M16, instead of M10 under the plan. Six extra months below the line he chose.

The human cost is quieter. It is the owner who signs a lease on hope, then lies awake the week the fit-out invoice lands.

## What the chain does not fix

Be clear about what you built. In [the full loop](/blog/control-cycle-management/) of goal, state, gap, levers, plan and forecast, the chain is the plan. It tells you whether the money will be there. It treats the symptom: the surprise bill.

The cause is still in place. You have a date, but no rule for when you may start spending the fund. And your checkpoints rest on one forecast, one number, with no range and no condition. A real plan needs both.

Two steps close that gap:

- **Launch conditions.** The measurable event that must happen before you touch the fund, and the conditions to stop or revise. Andrew tied his to the reserve holding at two months, three readings in a row. Chapter 10.
- **Two forecasts, as a range.** "If nothing changes" against "if the plan works", each with a range and a condition, so the checkpoints have honest edges. Chapter 8.

The backward move also has limits. It shows what must be true. It does not promise the goal is reachable. You still check the resources at every step. The model itself is open on [GitHub](https://github.com/mjirgensons/jirgenson-model).

## FAQ

### What is a sinking fund?

Money you set aside in regular amounts for one known future cost, such as a renewal, a repair or a deposit. When the bill comes, the cash is there, and your emergency reserve stays untouched.

### What is the difference between a sinking fund and an emergency fund?

The first pays for a bill you can see coming, with an amount and a date. An emergency fund covers what you cannot see, measured in months of expenses. Keep them apart, so one does not quietly empty the other.

### How much should I put in a sinking fund each month?

Start from the bill and walk backwards. Write the monthly rule, including any month when costs change, then set a minimum for each checkpoint. The right amount is whatever keeps you above every checkpoint, not the total divided by the months.
