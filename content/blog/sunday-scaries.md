The kids are laughing at the table, and you haven't heard a word of the story.

Your phone is face down next to your plate. You turn it over anyway. Three unread messages. One supplier. One customer. One you don't want to open until tomorrow.

It's Sunday, half past six, and the week has already started inside your head.

Four out of ten owners have hidden business stress from their own family.

So you nod at the story. You laugh a beat too late. Your partner notices, and says nothing.

Here's the strange part. You can work. Monday itself rarely scares you. What scares you is the open week: every task, every risk and every date carried on your back, with no system to set them down.

The Jirgenson Model gives you the answer: close the week before it opens, with a short Monday review that ends with names. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## Sunday scaries meaning: dread of an open week

Sunday scaries is the everyday name for the dread that builds on Sunday evening before the work week. People also call it Sunday dread or Sunday night anxiety.

For an owner, the dread has a shape. Nobody knows which numbers moved last week. Nobody knows which ones are in trouble. Nobody owns what happens next, except you, by default, for everything.

That's not a weekend problem. It's a missing rhythm.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, put one modest wish on his list of goals: at least one Sunday without a phone in his hand. It looked like the softest goal on the page. It turned out to need the hardest tool: a Monday that already has an answer.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> rhythm is a tool, not a habit. It is a calendar of short meetings, weekly, monthly and quarterly. Each one looks at the same sheet and asks the same questions. Until the calendar exists on paper, the review lives in your head, and so does the week.</div>

## The Monday review: six steps, fifteen minutes

Andrew feared his first Monday review would drag on for an hour. It came down to three questions. A month later, the Monday briefing took fifteen minutes instead of an hour. There was nothing left to argue about, because the figures showed everything.

Here are the six steps, each with his entry next to it.

### 1. Fix the slot

Pick one time every Monday and write it in the calendar. Same time, same sheet, same people.

Andrew and Sara, his senior barista, meet at the shared spreadsheet every Monday. Sunday night now has a fact to lean on: the questions have an hour.

### 2. Open the five rows

One screen, five rows, five columns: parameter, goal, now, error, zone. It reads in a minute, and you see at once where the fire is.

Andrew's sheet in his first month:

| Parameter | Goal | Now | Error | Zone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash reserve, months | 2.0 | 0.5 | −1.5 | 🔴 |
| Margin, % | 15 | 9 | −6 | 🟡 |
| Regular customers, % | 40 | 25 | −15 | 🟡 |
| Complaints per week | ≤ 2 | 5 | +3 | 🟡 |
| Hours per week | ≤ 40 | 65 | +25 | 🟡 |

The numbers come from one source each. The till feeds the spreadsheet once a week. If you don't have a sheet yet, [small business KPIs](/blog/small-business-kpis/) shows how to build one you can read in a minute.

### 3. Ask: which numbers changed this week?

The till answers this one, not memory. Write the new values. Nothing else yet.

### 4. Ask: which zone are they in now?

Look at nothing but the colours. Green means breathe. Yellow means watch. Red goes first. Andrew's reserve row is the only red one, so the reserve leads the meeting. For drawing the colour lines, see the [traffic light dashboard](/blog/traffic-light-dashboard/).

### 5. Ask: what needs to be done about it?

Answer with a list of two lines. Not ten. Each line has three parts: the action, the name, the date.

Andrew's two lines could read like this. It's an illustration built from his plans.

| Action | Name | By |
|---|---|---|
| Talk to the milk supplier about the price | Andrew | Wednesday |
| Run the Friday shifts | Sara | This Friday |

Handing the Friday shifts to Sara was part of his plan for the owner's hours. She could predict Fridays better than he could. The person at the wheel of any area should be the one who sees furthest ahead. That rule is unpacked in [delegating to the right person](/blog/delegating-to-the-right-person/).

### 6. Close with names

Read the two lines out loud. A line without a name isn't an action. It's a worry, and it will come home with you on Sunday.

Then close the sheet. The week is open on paper and closed in your head.

### Working alone? The same review fits one person

Elena, a freelance designer who also sells a book, runs the review by herself. Every Monday she takes last week's visitors to her book page, multiplies by the conversion rate and compares with her goal: 21 sales a month.

Say 280 people came in a week. At 1.5%, that's 4.2 sales a week, about 17 over four weeks. Four short, and the month isn't over. Her two lines: rewrite the page headline, email her subscribers. Both carry one name, hers, and a date.

## Sunday night anxiety feeds on an open list

An open list has no end. Your head keeps scanning it because nothing on it is settled. A closed list has owners and dates. There is nothing left to scan.

The usual way, and the model's way, run on the same week. This is an illustration.

| The usual week | The week with a Monday review |
|---|---|
| Sunday night: reread every message | Sunday night: the sheet is waiting for 9:00 |
| Monday: an hour of "we have to do something" | Monday: three questions, fifteen minutes |
| Every task lands on the owner | Every line ends with a name |
| Problems found when a customer complains | Problems found in the yellow zone |

The common enemy isn't Monday. It's the habit of running the business from memory, by mood, through the last angry email. You spend Sunday rehearsing a meeting nobody scheduled.

A note on care. A Monday review helps with the chaos in the business. It doesn't treat an illness. If the dread before work lasts for weeks, affects your sleep or keeps pulling your mood down, see a doctor. If you ever feel unsafe, call a crisis line, such as 988 in the US and Canada.

## What a week without review costs

Andrew's own story shows the price. In his second month, his menu plan had an error. One item he meant to drop was the thing that brought his regulars in.

The weekly review caught it in the second month. Without it, the item would have vanished. A month later he would have seen fewer regulars and had no idea why. He would have gone back to the loyalty cards he had shelved, and paid for them out of the red zone.

That red zone held half a month of expenses: €13,650 against a goal of €54,600. Money spent there on the wrong fix is money the shop doesn't have. Weekly observation caught the error in the second month rather than the sixth. That's four months of regulars kept.

The Monday hour itself had a price too. Andrew's briefing shrank from an hour to fifteen minutes. That's 45 minutes back every week, about 39 hours a year, from one meeting.

And the human cost sits at the Sunday table. Every week without a review is another Sunday with the phone in your hand.

## "My week is too chaotic for a fixed meeting"

That's the reason to have one. A chaotic week is exactly the week that needs a fixed point. The review is short because it looks only at colours and names. It doesn't solve everything. It decides who solves what, and by when.

## Where the Monday review stops

The Monday review is complete. You can run it tomorrow. But it treats a symptom. It shows the colours and hands out names. It can't make the colours honest, or the actions effective.

Two missing pieces cause that.

- **Zone thresholds and the red protocol (Chapter 4).** The colour lines, set on a calm day. And what to do when two rows turn red at once. Without them, "which zone?" turns into an argument. See [KPI zones](/blog/kpi-zones/).
- **The list of levers (Chapter 5).** What you can change, and what you can't. Without it, a name gets attached to an action that won't move the number. See [business levers](/blog/business-levers/).

The review is the rhythm part of a single loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. A loop without a rhythm runs only when you panic.

## FAQ

### What is the Sunday scaries?

It's the everyday name for the dread that builds on Sunday evening before the work week. For an owner, it often comes from an open week: nobody knows which numbers moved, and every next step sits with you. A fixed Monday review gives those questions an hour.

### How do I stop Sunday night anxiety?

Give Sunday a fact to lean on. Fix a short Monday review with three questions: which numbers changed, which zone they are in, and what needs doing. End every action with a name and a date. If the anxiety lasts for weeks or affects your sleep, talk to a doctor.

### Why do I get anxiety before work on Monday?

Many causes are possible, and a doctor is the right person to ask when it lasts. On the business side, the dread grows when the week has no owner but you. The book shows the full set of tools, from the five-row sheet to the meeting calendar, in one system.
