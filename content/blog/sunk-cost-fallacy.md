"How much more?"

Your partner asks it at the kitchen table, quietly, with the bank app open between you. You don't answer. You've already moved the savings once. You're about to do it again.

**When a young business hits trouble, two out of three owners pour in their own money.**

You tell yourself it's loyalty. Three years, the second mortgage, every weekend. You can't stop now, you've put too much in. But listen to that sentence. It isn't about the business. It's about the past.

You can work. Nobody doubts that. The trouble is that you carry the whole bet on your back, alone, with no rule for when to stop. So the project stops being yours. You become its servant.

The Jirgenson Model gives the answer here: a stop line, written on a calm day. Then numbers you set in advance decide whether you go on or stop. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What the sunk cost fallacy is

A sunk cost is money, time or effort you've spent and can't get back. The fallacy is letting it vote on what you do next. The cash already in the shop is gone whether you stay or leave. Only the future costs and the future results belong in the decision.

Everyone knows this in theory. Then it's your shop, your name on the sign, and theory goes quiet. That's the sunk cost fallacy at work: the past gets a vote it never earned.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> the cure for the sunk cost fallacy is a stop line. It holds the conditions for stopping, selling or revising, written before the hard month. An error is a reason to change the plan. The goal changes only when conditions change, when the data show it can't be reached, or when its price exceeds the benefit.</div>

The irony is sharp. You'd never let a supplier bill you twice for the same delivery. But you let last year's spending bill you again every month.

## Steady mode or manoeuvre: name what you're in

Before you can draw a stop line, name the kind of road you're on. The model has two.

- **Steady mode.** The goal stays fixed. You hold the course with small turns of the wheel, like a helmsman in a swell. Andrew raising his margin from 9% to 15% is steady mode.
- **Manoeuvre.** The goal itself moves. You're passing into a new state: a second location, a new product, a way out of a crisis.

Sunk cost thinking hides in manoeuvres. You've turned the ship, the old port is behind you, and turning back feels like failure. That's why a manoeuvre needs its stop line before it starts.

There's a second trap on the same road: stopping too early. Measure a manoeuvre in the system's own time, not your calendar. Elena, a freelance designer, moved from client work to her own book. Writing took three months. The book needed six more months of launch before her income settled. Had she judged it after one month, she'd have decided the book didn't work.

So a stop line protects you twice. It stops you pouring money into a dead road. And it stops you abandoning a live one in a bad week.

## When to walk away: write the stop line on a calm day

Here is the tool. One card per project. Fill it in on a calm day, with a clear head. It takes 15 minutes. Each step sits next to Andrew's entry.

### 1. Write what's already spent, then draw a line under it

Put the sunk cost at the top of the card: the money, the months, the hours. Then draw a thick line under it. Nothing above the line takes part in the decision. You write it down so you can see it, and then stop arguing with it.

### 2. Set the floor: the red line nothing may cross

Name the one number that must never go red while the project runs. For Andrew it's the cash reserve, and his red starts under one month of expenses. One month gives him time to negotiate a deferral, find money and decide. Below that, there's no time left for any of it.

This is your stop loss rule for the business. It's set in money and months, not in hope.

### 3. Name the first checkpoint and the result that means revise

A stop line needs a date to look. Andrew's margin plan had one:

| Field | Andrew's entry |
|---|---|
| Goal | Margin from 9% to 15% in six months |
| First check | Month 2 |
| Revise if | Margin below 10% |
| Backup lever | Track and cut write-offs |
| Final result | Month 6 |

At month 2 the margin stood at 10.5%. Above the line. Not proof yet, but a reason to carry on. Had it been 9.5%, the card would already say what happens next: revise, pull the backup lever. No midnight debate.

### 4. Write the stop, sell or revise conditions for the goal

The checkpoint tests the plan. This step tests the goal itself. Write when you'd stop, sell or rethink the whole thing. Only three reasons count:

- the conditions changed;
- the data show the goal can't be reached;
- the goal costs more than it brings.

"I've put too much in" is not on the list. That's the point of the list.

### 5. Check the whole route, not only the start

Before a manoeuvre, every stretch of the road must still have levers and a reserve. Andrew wanted a second location after his margin reached 15%. His reserve stood at 1.35 months: yellow, not green. Rent, equipment and staff would have dragged the reserve under the floor. He wrote a launch condition instead of a date: start only when the reserve holds at 2.0 months for three months in a row. How that condition works in detail is in [when to pivot and when to hold the course](/blog/when-to-pivot-small-business/).

### 6. Sign it and set the review rhythm

Date the card. Then fix when you look at it. Levers every week, the reserve every month. The plan every quarter, the goal once or twice a year. A stop line nobody reads is a wish.

## The common objection: "Stopping means I failed"

No. A red line crossed is an instrument reading, not a verdict. The card doesn't say you were wrong to start. It says the conditions you set on a calm day have now spoken.

Closing a branch, selling a product line or pausing a launch can be the move that saves the main goal. A captain who turns back in a storm hasn't failed at sailing. Before you close your business for good, read [when to give up on a business idea](/blog/when-to-give-up-on-a-business-idea/). It covers the test for an idea that no plan can save.

The real failure mode is the one with no line at all: the zombie company that never dies and never lives. It eats the savings one month at a time. If you've signed for the debt yourself, read [personal guarantee business loan](/blog/personal-guarantee-business-loan/) before the next signature.

## What pouring in without a line costs

Look at Emma and Paul, a family budget the book follows. Income €4,000 a month, spending €4,400, savings €2,000. If nothing changes, the savings run out in 5 months. Cut subscriptions and eating out by €180, and they last about 9 months. Add a side job of €300, and they run a surplus of €80 a month.

Same family, same savings. One choice empties the account by spring. The other grows it. The difference isn't effort. It's whether someone drew a line before the money moved.

For Andrew the price of no line is as plain. Sign the lease at a reserve of 1.35 months, and within a month the rent pushes him back into the red. He'd lose what he spent six months building.

And the human cost: the evening you stop telling your partner what the bank app says.

If the regret and the worry stop feeling like a bad week, talk to a doctor. In a crisis, call a crisis line (988 in the US and Canada). The stop line treats chaos in the business, not an illness.

## What the stop line does not fix

Be clear about what you now hold. The card tells you when to stop and when to carry on. It treats the symptom: decisions made by guilt about the past.

The cause sits further ahead. The card reacts when a number arrives. It can't show you, before you start, where the route will take the reserve in month four. And it can't tell you which week to begin so the launch condition is met on time.

Two steps close that gap:

- **Two forecasts.** "If nothing changes" against "if the plan works", in numbers and dates. You see the red month before it comes. Chapter 8. A first look is in [how to forecast cash flow](/blog/forecast-cash-flow/).
- **The backward move.** Start from the goal and walk back to this week. Arithmetic sets the start date of a manoeuvre, not eagerness. Chapter 15.

Without them, the stop line fires on time but too late to be cheap. The card is one part of a loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast.

## FAQ

### When should you give up on a business?

When one of three things is true. The conditions changed. The data show the goal can't be reached. Or the goal costs more than it brings. Write these conditions down before the hard month. Money already spent is not a reason either way. Set the red line first, as in [KPI zones](/blog/kpi-zones/).

### Should I close my business or keep going?

Check the floor you set: is the key number, such as the cash reserve, in the red? If it is, act on the red first. Then test the goal against the three reasons above. [Months of runway](/blog/months-of-runway/) shows how long your cash lasts at today's pace.

### What is a stop loss rule for a small business?

It's a number and a date you fix in advance: the floor no figure may cross, and the checkpoint where a weak result means revise. Andrew's floor is a reserve of one month of expenses. His first check came at month 2. Short videos on stop lines are on [the Steer, Don't Guess YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@SteerDontGuess).
