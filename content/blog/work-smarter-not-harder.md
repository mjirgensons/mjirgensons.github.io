Your daughter's birthday party. Cake on the table, phone face down by your plate. It buzzes. You check it "for one second", and you're gone for twenty minutes.

Later, in the car, nobody talks about the cake. They talk about the phone.

Half of all founders burned out last year. Three out of four live with anxiety.

You took one Saturday off. Everything stopped. The spreadsheet sat untouched, the plan hung on the wall, and the only thing that moved in the whole business was you.

That's the turn. You can work. Nobody doubts it, least of all you. But you carry the whole business on your back, with no system to hand it to. So every free day costs you twice: once at the party, and again on Monday.

Then someone tells you to work smarter, not harder. Fine. How? The slogan never says.

The Jirgenson Model gives the "how", and it is concrete: a closed loop of eight stages that runs without you holding every piece. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

## What "work smarter not harder" leaves out

The slogan treats "smart" as a trait. Some people have it, some don't. That makes the advice useless on a Tuesday morning, when the till is short and two staff are off sick.

Working harder has a precise meaning. It means you perform the missing parts of the loop yourself, by hand, every day. Working smarter means the loop exists, so the parts run on a rhythm.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> control is a closed cycle of eight stages, from noticing the pressure to finishing or maintaining the result. A skipped stage does not disappear. Either nobody performs it, and you pay for it, or somebody else performs it, and hardly in your favour.</div>

If you don't set the goal, the market, the bank or a competitor sets it for you. If you don't watch the numbers, your debts start watching you.

Working harder is the price of a missing stage, paid in hours. Andrew's revenue looked good and customers kept coming, the trap described in [busy but not profitable](/blog/busy-but-not-profitable/).

## How to work smarter: the eight-stage check

Take the project that's hardest right now. Write one sentence for each stage. Below, each stage sits next to what Andrew wrote for his coffee shop, where cash was melting and he was working 65 hours a week.

1. **Notice the pressure.** What is forcing you to act? Andrew: the cash reserve is melting away, and the owner is burning out.
2. **Recognise the situation.** Name it, with numbers. Andrew: not "a bad season". No cash cushion, and a margin of 9% instead of the twenty he expected.
3. **Set the goal vector.** An ordered list of measurable goals. Andrew: five goals in a fixed order, the cash reserve first and his own time last. The [refusal test](/blog/set-priorities-refusal-order/) sets that order.
4. **Build the concept.** A one-page plan for each goal. Andrew: a plan for the margin. The other plans wait while the reserve sits in the red zone.
5. **Build structures.** Systems, owners, reporting. Andrew and Sara, who works with him, set up a shared spreadsheet with five indicators. The till feeds it once a week. Each indicator has an owner.
6. **Observe and coordinate.** A fixed meeting on the gap between goal and state. Andrew: every Monday, he and Sara meet at the spreadsheet.
7. **Improve the concept.** Learn from the gap between forecast and fact. Andrew, in the second month: a menu item he planned to drop had to stay. It brings the regulars in.
8. **Finish or maintain.** Andrew: when the reserve reaches 2.0 months and holds for three months in a row, that goal moves to maintenance.

It takes 15 minutes. The empty line is where your extra hours go. Most often it is stage 3, wishes with no numbers, or stage 6, no regular look. A deeper walk through the stages, with a PDCA comparison, is in [the control cycle](/blog/control-cycle-management/).

## Working too much is a skipped stage

Andrew's stage 5 used to be Andrew. He was the structure. Every number lived in his head, so every decision waited for him.

Look at what his first plans moved off his back. Sara took over the Friday shifts, because she could predict them better than he could. Purchasing moved to a single morning a week. Neither is a productivity trick. Each one fills a stage with a person and a slot. For more on choosing who takes what, read [delegating to the right person](/blog/delegating-to-the-right-person/).

Here is the same goal both ways, as an illustration.

| | Working harder | Working smarter |
|---|---|---|
| Who notices the drop in cash | Andrew, at 2 a.m. | The weekly sheet, in the red row |
| Who decides what comes first | Whoever shouts loudest | The goal order, written on a calm day |
| Who checks if the plan works | Nobody, until the bank calls | Andrew and Sara, every Monday |
| What a day off costs | Everything stops | One meeting moves |

Always working is not dedication. It is a loop with you as the missing part.

## The first Monday: three questions instead of an hour

Andrew feared the first Monday meeting would drag on for an hour. It came down to three questions:

1. Which numbers changed this week?
2. Which zone are they in now?
3. What needs to be done about it?

The till answered the first. The colours answered the second. A two-line list answered the third. Everything they used to argue about was now on the screen. That's what smarter looks like from the outside: less talk, because the sheet does the talking. Building the colours is in [traffic light dashboard](/blog/traffic-light-dashboard/).

## Don't judge a lever before the loop turns once

Here is a trap for tired owners. You change something on Monday, nothing moves by Friday, and you change it again. Chaos at work often starts there.

Every loop has its own delay. Andrew needs up to 7 days to see a change and 1 day to decide at the Monday meeting. Changing the menu and prices takes 7 days. The guests take about 30 days to respond. That's about 45 days in total. So the first check of his margin plan falls in the second month, not the first. Before that, he would be measuring noise, not the lever.

Smarter means waiting for one full turn of the loop before you judge. Reports that arrive after the damage are covered in [phase lag](/blog/phase-lag-management/).

## What six more months of the hard way cost

Money first. Andrew's reserve stood at 0.5 months of expenses against a goal of 2.0. With monthly expenses of €27,300, the shortfall was €40,950. If nothing changes, the reserve reaches its goal at M16 instead of M10. By the end of the third month it stands at about 0.8: still in the red zone.

Then the hours. His goal was 40 a week; he worked 65. That's 25 extra hours a week. Over six months, about 650 hours: more than sixteen full working weeks, spent being the missing stage. His own red line for hours sat above 70, five hours away.

And the people at the party get the back of your head, bent over the phone.

This is a business system, not a health treatment, and nothing here diagnoses exhaustion from work. If the anxiety, the sleepless nights or the low mood last longer than a bad week, see a doctor. If it feels urgent, call a crisis line. The model treats the chaos in the business, not an illness. For stress sorted by zone, see [small business owner stress](/blog/small-business-owner-stress/).

## Where the eight stages stop

"I'm a one-person business; this is for companies." Elena is a freelance designer working fifty hours a week. Her income swung between €800 and €2,200, and one client brought 70% of it. She ran the same eight stages on her book launch. Her stage 6 is a weekly look at the page visits. Her stage 7 is a rule: if fewer than 1.2% of visitors buy two weeks running, she rewrites the headline.

The eight-stage check finds the missing stage. That treats the symptom: you, doing it by hand. The cause underneath is that you don't know when a number deserves action, or which lever will move it.

Two steps of the system fix that, and the check fails without them. The zones and the red protocol, in Chapter 4, say when a reading demands action and what comes first when several turn red. The list of levers, in Chapter 5, says what you can move yourself, how fast it acts, and whether you can undo it. Without zones, the Monday meeting argues about every number. Without levers, it pulls the wrong one.

The eight stages are the frame for the whole loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. The model's rules are public in the [Jirgenson canon on GitHub](https://github.com/mjirgensons/jirgenson-canon), and short walk-throughs are on the [Steer, Don't Guess YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@SteerDontGuess).

## FAQ

### What does work smarter not harder really mean?

It means the control loop runs without you holding every piece. Goals are in order, numbers have owners, and a fixed meeting compares the state with the goal. Working harder means you do those missing stages yourself, by hand, every day.

### How do I stop working too much as a business owner?

Put your own hours on the panel as a goal with a number, and give it zones. Then find the stage you perform by hand and hand it to a person and a slot. Andrew moved the Friday shifts to Sara and purchasing to one morning a week. The full method is in Steer, Don't Guess.

### What to do when work feels out of control?

Write one sentence for each of the eight stages of your hardest project. The empty one is usually the goal list or the weekly look. Fill it first. If you have lost control of your sleep or mood as well, and it lasts longer than a bad week, see a doctor.
