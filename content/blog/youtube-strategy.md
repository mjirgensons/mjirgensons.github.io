It's 11 p.m. on Sunday. The video went live an hour ago. Twenty hours of scripting, filming and editing.

Fourteen views. You refresh. Fourteen.

At dinner your partner asked, "How's the channel going?" You said "good" and checked the phone under the table. The kids noticed. You told yourself next week's video will be the one.

**Nine out of ten YouTube channels never hit 1,000 subscribers.**

Here is the irony. You plan every cut down to the frame. The plan for the channel fits in three words: "post more, hope."

You can work. You proved it with twenty hours on one video. What wears you down is carrying the whole channel in your head with no system, pushing on the one thing you can't touch: the views. So every upload feels like a verdict on you.

The Jirgenson Model gives you the answer: stop pushing on the result and name the levers. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works ([doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934)).

One plain line on youtube burnout. If the low mood or the bad sleep lasts more than a bad week, see a doctor or call a crisis line. This is a tool for the chaos in the channel, not a treatment.

## A YouTube strategy is a list of levers, not a wish

A helmsman doesn't turn the ship with his hands. He turns the wheel, and the heading follows. Between the wish to sail north and the ship's movement sits a mechanism within his reach.

Your channel works the same way. Views, subscribers and watch time are the heading. You can't grab them. You can only turn the wheel.

<div class="def"><strong>In the Jirgenson Model,</strong> levers are the parameters you can change yourself, by your own decision. Together they form the control vector. Everything else around your goal is an environmental factor: you take it into account, but you don't work on it.</div>

Here is the split for a channel, as an illustration.

| Result (what you want) | Lever (what you do) |
|---|---|
| Views | Title, thumbnail, topic choice |
| Watch time | The first minute, pacing, length |
| Subscribers | The reason to come back: a series, a promise kept |
| Hours you can sustain | Upload rhythm, batch filming, what you stop doing |

The usual strategy pushes on the left column: "I need views!" The model pulls the right column and watches the left one follow. As long as the lever stays unnamed, you get another week of talk about the weather.

## Build your lever list in ten minutes

Here is the full tool. It takes one sheet and about ten minutes.

### 1. Write every action that comes to mind

Don't filter yet. Elena, a freelance designer launching her first online book, wrote: "more social media, increase sales, be more visible". Not one item could be done tomorrow.

### 2. Ask of each item: what do I do with my own hands?

Elena asked it, and the answers changed: write three chapters, prepare the book's page, collect a list of the first hundred addresses. Those were levers. Each one had a verb she could do on Monday.

### 3. Cross out what isn't yours

Cross out everything that depends on other people, the platform, the market or luck. Andrew did this for his coffee shop. The weather went. Rent and taxes went. Foot traffic in the street went. Four levers stayed: prices, menu, purchasing, portion size.

He reread the short list and felt calmer. Until then he had spent his nerves on everything at once. Now he had four things for the next morning.

### 4. Ask three questions of every lever that stays

Can I move it myself, today? How long before the effect shows? Can I undo it if it fails? Andrew's answers:

| Lever | Myself, today? | When the effect comes | Rollback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prices | Yes | In weeks | Hard: customers remember the jump |
| Menu | Yes | Quickly | Easy: bring the item back |
| Purchasing | Through negotiation | In a month | Hard: the contract is signed |
| Portion size | Yes | Immediately | Easy, up to a quality limit |

The rule from the table: start with what is fast and reversible. A new thumbnail on an old video is fast and reversible. Deleting twenty videos is neither.

### 5. Add one number that moves first

Views for the month are a lagging indicator. They tell you what already happened. Pick one leading indicator you can read every week, one that moves before the subscriber count does. Elena's was page visitors a week and the share who bought. For the difference between the two kinds, see [leading and lagging indicators](/blog/lagging-vs-leading-indicators/).

That is the whole list, on half a page. For a plain primer, read [business levers](/blog/business-levers/).

## No views on YouTube? Change one lever per video

A video flops. So the next one gets a new topic, thumbnail style, length and upload day. Better or worse, you can't tell why.

Andrew faced the same trap with prices. Suppose he had raised them fifteen per cent in one go, "to be sure". Some customers would leave, others would get confused. He couldn't tell how much of the drop came from the price and how much from something else. So he capped every step at five per cent. The limit made the experiment readable.

He also turned the price rise into a testable question. The rise pays off as long as fewer than 6.7% of customers leave. A vague goal, "raise the margin", became a number he could check in two weeks.

Do the same with the channel. One lever per video, or per small batch. Write down which lever you moved and what you expect. Then read the leading indicator. Elena did exactly this with her book page: she changed the headline and watched how the buying rate responded.

## YouTube algorithm changed? It was never your lever

A car enters a bend on ice and starts to skid. The ice is an environmental factor. You can't melt it or scold it. A driver who gets angry at the road crew is wasting time. A driver who eases off and steers into the skid gets control back.

The algorithm is the ice. When it changes, you note it, the same way Andrew notes the weather. Then you go back to the wheel: title, thumbnail, first minute, rhythm.

The same holds for "my audience's mood" and "luck". Neither is a lever. If you write a goal and can't find one lever for it, the goal is worded in a way you can't control. Trust follows from the videos you make; it is not something you can pull. The full idea is in [external factors in business](/blog/external-factors-business/).

## Is YouTube worth it? Ask the levers first

The question "should I quit YouTube?" usually arrives at 11 p.m., right after the refresh. That is the worst moment to answer it.

Ask a different one first. Have I pulled named levers, one at a time, and read a number that moves first? If the answer is no, the channel hasn't failed. It has never been tested. An unsteered dream is not a verdict.

If the answer is yes, and the leading number stays flat for weeks, then you have a real decision to make, with data in hand. For that decision, the post on [when to give up on an idea](/blog/when-to-give-up-on-a-business-idea/) sets the line in advance.

## What six more months of posting into silence cost

Start with money and hours. Elena's plan for her book needed about 120 hours over three months and €200 for tools. Spend hours like that with no named lever, and you have spent them on the weather.

Then time. Elena's book page reached 1,400 visits a month only at month 9. The launch was at month 4. Had she judged it after one month, she would have concluded the book didn't work. Without a lever list and a number that moves first, you judge every week, and every week says "no".

Andrew's case shows the price of drifting: if nothing changes, his reserve reaches its goal at month 16 instead of month 10.

And the human cost: six more months of the phone under the dinner table.

## "But the algorithm decides anyway"

It decides the current. You decide the steering. A yacht can't stop the drift, but the skipper who knows his levers still reaches port. The skipper who blames the sea drifts further every hour. ## Where the lever list stops

Be honest about what you built. The lever list treats a symptom: pushing on views you can't touch. It points your hours at things that obey you.

The cause of the next lost month is still there. A lever pulled on a Monday is not yet a plan. You don't know how many hours each lever gets, what you will never do for views, or when to check if it works. And you don't know when a bad week is noise and when it is red.

Two steps close that gap:

- **A one-page plan per goal**, with resources, limits and a check date that says "revise". Chapter 6. A worked example for channels is in [the 1000 subscribers plan](/blog/1000-subscribers/).
- **Zones for each number**, set in advance, so one flat week doesn't trigger panic. Chapter 4. Then the step after both: steering by forecast, shown in [YouTube growth](/blog/youtube-growth/).

The levers are one step of the whole loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. They answer the one question the other steps can't: what can I change?

## FAQ

### What is a good YouTube strategy for a small channel?

A short list of levers you control: title, thumbnail, the first minute, topic choice and upload rhythm. Change one per video, read one weekly number that moves before subscribers, and give the plan a check date. Views follow the levers; you can't pull them directly.

### Why do I get no views on YouTube?

Views are a result, not a lever, so look at what you changed. If every video changes five things, you can't tell which one hurts. Move one lever at a time and watch a leading number, and the cause shows up within a few uploads.

### Should I quit YouTube if my channel is not growing?

Decide on data, not at 11 p.m. If you have never pulled named levers one at a time, the channel hasn't been tested yet. If you have, and the leading number stays flat for weeks, decide then. A one-page plan sets that check date and stop line in advance.
