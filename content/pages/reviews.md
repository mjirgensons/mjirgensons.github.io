## What the early reviews agree on

**Mechanics, not advice.** "Most management books give advice. This one gives mechanics." The book does not hand out tips like "delegate" or "think strategically"; it shows how to break a problem with five goals and ten constraints into parts you can handle.

**The refusal test is the idea quoted most.** Rank your goals by which one you would give up first and which you would defend to the end. If you do not decide that in advance, the decision is made for you by fatigue, haste and whoever shouts loudest.

**Levers versus conditions.** Reviewers call this the most liberating distinction in the book: separate what you can turn yourself (prices, the menu, terms, hours) from what you can only take into account (the weather, a competitor, your own lack of resources), and stop spending energy on the second kind.

**Three reading levels in every chapter.** A simple everyday example (a teenager saving for a bike), a practical case (a coffee shop, a freelance designer) and a formal level with definitions and formulas. Reviewers note that this makes the book work for a teenager planning pocket money, a small-business owner and an analyst alike.

**The author promises nothing.** Every reviewer mentions it: the model is presented as a tool for thinking, not a recipe for success; forecasts can be wrong, data can be incomplete, the model does not replace industry knowledge or a financial calculation. One reviewer: "This caution makes the book's approach more credible."

## What they criticise

The terminology is dense (goal vectors, state vectors, zones, control modes, three offsets). Repeated examples slow the reading in places. Readers who already know goal-setting methods and PDCA will find part of the content familiar. The novelty, as one review puts it, is in how the ideas are arranged and applied, not in a brand-new principle.

The author's answer: the terms are kept because the canon for AI assistants depends on them being exact, and the glossary (Appendix B) and the one-page cheat sheet (Appendix A) exist for exactly this reason.
