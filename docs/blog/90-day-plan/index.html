90 Day Plan: Start at Day 90 and Walk Back to This Week

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

Build a 90 day plan backwards. Write day 90 as one number with a date. Work out how far that number can move in a month. Then step back: the minimum it must reach by day 60, by day 30 and today. Put today's measured figure next to that minimum. The headroom shows what one bad month can cost, and the first row tells you what to do this week.

A 90 day goal gets two looks. One on the Monday you set it. One on the Friday you miss it.

Picture the start of a quarter. You write "build up the cash reserve" on the whiteboard. Twelve weeks later the number is short, and you blame a slow season.

Nothing went wrong in week twelve. It went wrong in week one, when nobody knew where the number had to be by week four.

That is the quiet irony of quarterly goals. You plan the quarter forward from hope, and you judge it backward from the bank statement.

The fix is to plan in the other direction. The answer comes from the Jirgenson Model: start at day 90, ask what must be true right before it, and walk back to this week. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

What a 90 day plan is, and why direction matters

A 90 day plan is a plan for the next three months, split into checkpoints at day 30, day 60 and day 90. You meet it in new jobs, product launches and quarterly business goals. For the full year, see annual planning.

The usual version runs forward. What first? What next? Every step adds options, and by week three the plan is a wish list.

A navigation app works the other way. It knows where you are and where you are going. Miss a turn, and it plots a fresh route from the spot where you stand. It never asks you to explain the past.

In the Jirgenson Model, this is the backward move. You define the end result first. Then, at every step, you ask one question: what must be true immediately before this? The answers form a chain of conditions, and the first link tells you what to do now.

Forward planning asks what you can do. Backward planning asks what must already be true. The second question has fewer answers, and each one has a date.

The 90 day plan template, filled in

Here is Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, at the end of month 9. His goal number one is a cash reserve of at least 2.0 months of expenses by the end of month 12. That deadline sits exactly 90 days away. How to count that figure is in cash reserve months of expenses.

Month 8 was bad. A supplier raised prices by 8%, a repair ate part of the reserve, and a barista left. The margin fell from 15% to 12%. In month 9 Andrew raised his prices by 5%, and the margin came back to 15%.

Now he plans the last quarter, in five steps.

1. Write day 90 as a number with a date

Not "a healthy reserve". A number, a unit and a deadline.

Andrew writes: reserve of at least 2.0 months of expenses at the end of month 12.

2. Find the monthly step

How far can the number move in one month if the plan works? Use your own figures, not a target you like.

At a 15% margin, Andrew's shop earns €4,500 a month against €25,500 of expenses. Each month adds about 0.18 months to the reserve.

3. Walk back one month at a time

Start at day 90 and subtract one step for every month you go back.

Checkpoint When Minimum reserve, months
Day 90 End of M12 2.00
Day 60 End of M11 1.82
Day 30 End of M10 1.65
Day 0 End of M9 (today) 1.47

The last row is the one that matters tonight. If today's reserve sits below 1.47, the goal is already out of reach at this pace. You find out on day 0, not on day 90.

4. Put today's measured figure next to day 0

Measure it. Do not remember it. Andrew counts his reserve from the bank and the accounts: 1.74 months.

Subtract the minimum: 1.74 minus 1.47 leaves 0.27. That is his headroom.

5. Read the headroom, then write this week's action

Headroom is the size of the mistake the plan can survive. The repair in month 8 cost about 0.10 months of reserve. So Andrew's plan can take two more repairs like it and still close on time.

The first row answers "what do I do this week?" For Andrew: keep the new prices, and watch the average margin per receipt every week. If any checkpoint lands below its minimum, he adds a lever before the next one. That weekly margin is his leading indicator.

Here is the whole sheet after the quarter:

Checkpoint Minimum Actual Headroom If below
Day 0 (M9) 1.47 1.74 +0.27 Add a lever this week
Day 30 (M10) 1.65 1.91 +0.26 Add a lever this week
Day 60 (M11) 1.82 2.09 +0.27 Add a lever this week
Day 90 (M12) 2.00 2.27 +0.27 Goal met

One sheet, four rows, five columns. It takes about twenty minutes to draw. The full model behind the sheet is open on GitHub.

A 90 day plan example: the same quarter at two margins

Now take the same 90 days without the price rise. The margin stays at 12% after the bad month. Same goal, same deadline. Both columns come from the same simple model of the shop.

Change nothing (12%) Prices +5% (15%)
Monthly step 0.14 months 0.18 months
Minimum at day 0 1.59 1.47
Actual at day 0 1.70 1.74
Headroom 0.11 0.27
One more repair (0.10) Headroom nearly gone Room for two more
Reserve at day 90 2.11 2.27

Both versions reach 2.0 on time. A forward plan would call them equal.

The backward chain shows the truth. At 12%, the plan survives only if nothing breaks for three months. One machine, one supplier letter, one sick barista, and the goal slides past day 90. At 15%, the same breakdown is an annoyance.

A forward plan shows you the gap on day 90. A backward plan shows it on day 0.

A 90 day plan for a project, not a number

Some quarters have no single number. A launch, a move, an opening. The chain still works. Each checkpoint becomes a condition you can answer with yes or no.

Andrew's second coffee shop shows it. He walked back from the opening at month 30. Here is his last quarter.

When What must be true
M27 (day 0) Renovation and fit-out complete
M29 (day 60) Test week
M30 (day 90) Opening

If the fit-out is unfinished at month 27, the opening is already late. You know it on day 0, with three months left to act.

The same walk works for a new role or a launch: day 90 first, then day 60, day 30 and this week.

The chain also exposes goals that look too big. Andrew once pictured ten coffee shops in five years. Walked back, that goal landed on one task for this week: hire a senior barista and start writing procedures. If he cannot free his hands today, the tenth shop is a daydream. The mistake shows up now, not in year five.

What a quarter of forward planning costs

Run the numbers on hope.

If Andrew changes nothing, his reserve reaches two months at month 16, not month 10. That is six extra months below the safe line, one breakdown away from trouble.

In the last quarter, the 12% plan carries 0.11 months of headroom. One repair costs about 0.10. A forward plan would not show that. It would show "on track" right up to the day it was not.

And the second shop waits on that reserve. Every month the reserve slips, the whole chain to month 30 slips with it.

The human cost is plainer. Three more months of doing the sum in your head before every supplier payment.

"I have no time for this." The sheet takes twenty minutes. A missed quarter takes three months, and you only find out at the end.

Where the 90 day plan stops

The chain treats one symptom: the late surprise. You see the slip on day 0 or day 30, while there is time to add a lever. That part works today.

The cause sits one level deeper. The chain trusts two things it cannot check. It trusts that your day 0 figure is right. And it trusts that day 90 is possible at all. Start from a wrong figure, and a flawless chain walks you to the wrong place. Never mistake an open window on a high floor for an exit.

Two steps close that gap:

  • The three offsets. A day 90 that fights another goal. A day 0 measured wrong. A market fading under the plan. How to test all three before you commit: Chapter 11.
  • The tolerance corridor and launch conditions. How far you may stray from the line, in value and in time. And the measurable event that lets a big move begin. Chapter 10.

Without them, the chain tells you where you must be. It does not yet tell you whether the route is real. Both chapters are in the paperback as well as on Kindle.

The chain is one link in a loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. It turns the goal and the forecast into dates you can check. Each checkpoint still needs a plan behind it: which levers, what is off limits, who owns it. That page is the one page business plan.

Key takeaways

  • Write day 90 as a number, a unit and a date.
  • Find the monthly step from your own figures, then subtract it month by month back to today.
  • Put today's measured figure next to the day 0 minimum. The difference is your headroom.
  • Small headroom is a warning on day 0, even when the plan still reaches its goal.
  • A project quarter works the same way, with yes or no conditions in place of numbers.

Day 0 or day 90

You have two choices this quarter. Write a forward list, look at it on day 89, and blame the season. Or write day 90 tonight and walk back to this week. A filled-in backward planning template helps on the first try.

The chain gives you the line. The three offsets and the corridor tell you if the line is real, and how far you may drift from it before you act.

Questions people ask

What is a 90 day plan?

A plan for the next three months with checkpoints at day 30, day 60 and day 90. It works best backwards: define day 90 first, then work out what must be true at each earlier checkpoint, down to this week.

What should a 90 day plan template include?

Five columns: the checkpoint, its minimum, today's measured figure, the headroom, and the action if a checkpoint lands below its line. Add one row for this week's first action.

What is a good 90 day plan example?

A coffee shop needs a cash reserve of 2.0 months of expenses in 90 days. Walking back gives minimums of 1.82, 1.65 and 1.47. Today's 1.74 leaves 0.27 of headroom. *Steer, Don't Guess* by [Martin Jirgenson](https://www.goodreads.com/author/show/73234443.Martin_Jirgenson) works through the full chain, including the scenario with less room.
