Action Plan Template: Five Rows That Turn a Goal Into Monday

Martin Jirgenson31 Oct 2026 · 8 min read
Short answer

A useful action plan template has five rows for one goal: the levers you will pull; the resources in hours, people and money; the constraints, meaning what you will not do; a leading indicator you check every week; and an owner with a first checkpoint that triggers a revision. Then add up the levers' effects and check they cover the gap.

A to-do list can be finished in full and still leave the goal where it was.

Imagine it is Sunday night. Your action plan is a list in your notes app: call the supplier, fix the website, post more, chase invoices. Every line is sensible. You tick them all by Friday. On Monday you still cannot say whether the goal moved.

The list grows every week. The goal stays put. And nowhere on the list is there a line for what you refuse to do.

That is not a plan. It is a pile of bricks without a blueprint.

Why a task list is not an action plan

Levers are bricks: prices, offers, channels, hours. A plan is the blueprint that says which bricks, in what order, with what resources, and inside which boundaries. Without the blueprint, a busy week looks like progress.

In the Jirgenson Model, the plan for one goal is the control concept. It answers five questions: levers, resources, constraints, a leading indicator, and a deadline with an owner. If any answer is missing, you hold a wish, not a plan. The plans for all your goals together form the general concept.

A task list answers one question: what shall I do? The template below answers five. The four missing answers are where goals stall.

The action plan template, filled in

Here is the whole template on one page. The example is Elena, a freelance designer. Her income swings between €800 and €2,200 a month and averages €1,300. One client pays 70% of it. She works fifty hours a week. Her goal: a three-month average income of at least €3,000 by month 14, with no client above 30%.

One lever for that goal is her first online book. This is her launch plan.

Row The question Elena's entry
1. Levers What will I change, myself? Write the manuscript; a price of €29; a sales page for the book; a mailing list
2. Resources How many hours, people and euros? About 120 hours over three months; €200 for the payment service and the mailing tool
3. Constraints What will I not do, even if it is faster? No emails to people who have not subscribed; no undercutting on price
4. Leading indicator Which number moves before the result? 350 visitors to the sales page a week, and the share who buy
5. Deadline and owner Who, by when, and when is the first check? Elena. Launch at M4, first check at M5, target income by M14

Copy the first two columns. Write one goal at the top. Fill in the third column.

How to fill each row

Row 1: levers you can pull yourself

A lever is something you change with your own hands this week. "Sell more books" is a result. "A €29 price and a sales page" are levers. Write only what passes that test.

Row 2: resources, with their source

Hours do not appear from nowhere, so name where each one comes from. Elena's 120 hours over three months come to about ten a week. Five sit on top of her usual fifty, inside a cap of 55 she set in advance for the book months. The other five come from turning down small orders.

That last line is the honest one. A plan that adds hours without saying what gets dropped is a plan to burn out.

Row 3: constraints, written while you are calm

A constraint answers: what will I not do, even if it would save the goal? Write it before the pressure arrives. Under pressure, every shortcut looks reasonable.

Elena's two constraints protect her name and her price. Andrew, the coffee shop owner, wrote "do not delay wages". Alex, a thirteen-year-old saving for a bicycle, wrote "do not borrow money, do not skip school".

Row 4: a leading indicator you check weekly

The result arrives late. Income at month 14 tells you nothing in month 5. So pick a number that moves first.

Elena tested a trial stream of visitors to her page: 1.5 buyers per 100 visitors. At 1,400 visitors a month, that is 21 sales and about €609 before fees. So 350 visitors a week is the number to watch. If it holds, the income follows.

Row 5: an owner, a first check, a revision line

One name. A launch date. A first checkpoint early enough to change course. Then write the result at that checkpoint that forces a revision. Andrew's margin plan said it plainly: below 10% at month 2, the plan gets rewritten.

Check the plan covers the gap

A well-written plan can still be too small. So add up what the levers bring and compare it with the gap.

Alex wants €300 for a bicycle by 1 September. On 1 May he has €40. His levers:

Lever Expected
Pocket money, €20 a month for 4 months €80
Jobs for the neighbours, €30 a month for 4 months €120
Selling his scooter, once €60
Already in the piggy bank €40
Total €300

The plan covers the gap with nothing to spare. So he drew a line to check against every Sunday: €105 on 1 June, €170 on 1 July, €235 on 1 August. On 1 June he had €90. Behind the line. His rule was simple: when he falls behind, he adds a lever.

A thirteen-year-old with a piggy bank runs a tighter plan than a business with a forty-item task list.

What the missing page costs

Here are the coffee shop's numbers. At a 9% margin, the shop earns about €2,700 a month. The margin plan aims at 15%, which means €4,500. Every month at the old margin is €1,800 short.

Over time it adds up. If nothing changes, Andrew's cash reserve reaches its two-month target at month 16. With the plan carried out, at month 10. Six extra months with less than two months of cash between him and a bad week.

Alex's version is smaller and as plain. With no plan beyond pocket money, he reaches autumn with €120. He is €180 short, and the bicycle stays in the shop.

The human cost is the same in both: you keep working hard on the wrong things and wondering why nothing moves.

Where one page stops

The page gives you a plan for one goal. It treats the symptom: busy weeks with no direction.

The deeper cause is still in place. A plan is a hypothesis, and reality will push against it. When the first check comes back short, you need to know what kind of miss it is. And you need to see it coming before the checkpoint, not on the day.

Two steps of the system handle that.

  • Control by forecast (Chapter 9). Two forecasts, "if nothing changes" and "if the plan works", so you act before the miss shows up.
  • Steady mode and manoeuvre (Chapter 10). When a miss means change the plan, and when it means change the goal.

Without them, the page tells you what to do. It does not tell you what to do when the page turns out wrong.

"I have no time to plan"

Use the action plan template for one goal at a time: one page, about fifteen minutes. If the page will not fit, the goal is too big: split it in two. Start with the goal in the worst shape, not with all of them.

Key takeaways

  • A task list says what to do. An action plan also says with what, within which limits, and how you will know.
  • Five rows: levers, resources, constraints, a leading indicator, an owner with a first check.
  • Name where every hour comes from, and what you drop to free it.
  • Add up the levers and compare the total with the gap before you start.
  • A plan is a hypothesis. Write the number that forces a revision.

Tonight's choice

You have two choices. Add three more tasks to the list and hope Friday feels different. Or take your worst goal and write its five rows tonight.

The page gets you moving with a direction. The forecasts and the plan-or-goal rule keep you moving when the first check comes back short.

Picture next Monday: one page on the desk, one number to look at, and one thing you already know you will not do.

Questions people ask

What is a one page business plan, and is it an action plan?

Here it means one page per goal: the levers, resources, constraints, a leading indicator, and an owner with a first checkpoint. It is an action plan for one goal, not a pitch for a bank or an investor.

What should a simple action plan include?

Five rows. What you will change, and what it costs in time and money. What you will not do. The number you check weekly. Who owns it, with a first checkpoint.

How do I know if my action plan is working before the deadline?

Watch the leading indicator every week and compare it with a planned line. *Steer, Don't Guess* shows how to build that line as a forecast, so a shortfall shows up months before the deadline.
