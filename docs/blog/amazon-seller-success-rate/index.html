Amazon Seller Success Rate: A One-Page Plan Before Launch

Martin Jirgenson6 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

The Amazon seller success rate is low, and no plan guarantees yours. A plan makes your launch readable. Write one page per goal with five answers: which levers in what order, what resources, what you will not do, which weekly number shows progress, and who checks it on which date. If a question has no answer, you have a wish, not a plan.

Seven out of ten new Amazon sellers never make a single sale.

It didn't have to be seven. Some of those sellers might have found their first buyer with a plan on one page, not in their head. With a control system, that count could look different.

Picture the order form for your first batch of stock. Quantity, price, ad budget. You fill each box by feel, look at the total for a long minute, and click Pay.

Here is the irony. The supplier sends you every term in writing. Your own plan for the money lives in your head, and it changes every night.

Knowing your levers is not enough. A coffee shop owner can raise the price of coffee on Monday and find the room empty by Wednesday. Pulling the right levers without a plan is roulette with your own business.

Amazon seller success rate starts with a page, not a launch

Write the plan before you spend the money. One goal, one page.

Levers are bricks. You can't build a house from bricks without a blueprint: the order of work, the resources and the boundaries. The plan is the blueprint. It answers one question: what am I doing, in what order, with what, and what will I not do?

The last part matters most. Write your limits while you are calm. In a crisis, any move looks acceptable. Whoever hasn't decided what they will never do ends up doing anything at all.

The five questions, answered on one page

This is the tool. About 15 minutes for one goal. Here it is with the plan of Andrew, who runs a coffee shop called "The Corner". His goal: raise the margin from 9% to 15% in six months.

Question What it asks Andrew's answer
1. Levers Which ones, in what order? Remove the 2 most loss-making items; rework the recipes of 3 low-margin items; a new milk contract; prices up 5% in month 2
2. Resources How much time, how many people, how much money? 4 hours of his time a week; Sara's help
3. Constraints What will I not do, even if it would be faster? No cheaper coffee beans; no price rise above 5% in one step
4. Leading indicator Which number shows the plan working before the deadline? Average margin per receipt, every week
5. Deadline and owner Who checks, when, and what result means revise? Andrew. First check at month 2: below 10% means revise. Final result at month 6
Backup lever What if the first check falls short? Track and cut write-offs

Fill it in for your own goal, row by row.

1. Put the levers in order. Fast and reversible first, slow ones after. Andrew's price rise waits until month 2. The other three levers start first.

2. Give each lever its resources. Hours and money, written as numbers.

3. Write what you won't do. This row protects the business from you.

4. Pick one weekly number. It must move before the monthly result does.

5. Name the owner, the check date and the revision line. Write the line before the check, never after.

If a row stays empty, you don't have a plan yet. You have a wish, and filling that row is your next task.

Now test the page against the gap. The margin needs 6 points. Andrew's estimates: removing two items, plus 1.5 points. New recipes, plus 1.0. The milk contract, plus 0.5. The 5% price rise, if 2% of customers leave, plus about 3.1. The total is 6.1 points against a gap of 6.0.

The plan passes, with almost no buffer. That is exactly why the month 2 check is mandatory, and why the backup lever sits on the page in advance.

In the Jirgenson Model, the plan for one goal is the control concept: levers, resources, constraints, a leading indicator, and a deadline with an owner. The plans for all your goals together form the general concept. They must not contradict each other.

The Jirgenson Model is new. It is scientifically proven to work (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934). Because it is new, it could not help the sellers behind these numbers. Now it can help you.

Amazon FBA failure rate a year in: the limits keep you selling

A year later, fewer than one in ten new Amazon sellers is still selling.

A year is long in a game you can't read. Some of those sellers might still be selling if they had written their limits before the first bad month. The odds could have looked different.

Ask the hard question now: what will I not do, even if it would save the goal?

Andrew wrote two answers. No cheaper beans. No price jump above 5% at a time. Cheap beans would have lifted the margin within a month. They would have cost him customers within six months.

The price limit did a second job. Suppose that on the first Monday he had raised prices by fifteen per cent at once, to be sure. Some customers leave, others get confused. And he can no longer tell how much of the drop came from the price and how much from something else.

Here is the same move both ways. Illustration from his story.

Without a limit With a written limit
The move Prices up 15% in one go Prices up 5%, in month 2
What he learns Nothing readable Whether this one lever worked
If it goes wrong Customers lost, cause unknown One step to undo

Others write different limits. Elena, a freelance designer launching her first online book: don't email people who haven't subscribed, don't undercut on price. Alex, a boy saving for a bicycle: don't borrow money, don't skip school to earn it.

A written "no" stops a midnight decision faster than any argument.

Amazon first sale and the number you read every week

The monthly result tells you what happened. The leading indicator tells you what is coming.

Andrew reads one figure every week: the average margin per receipt. That is the receipt's revenue minus its direct costs. He sees the trend weeks before the monthly accounts close.

Elena picked hers for her launch: 350 page visitors a week and the share of them who buy.

At Andrew's first check, in month 2, the margin stood at 10.5%. Above the revision line. Not yet proof. He still had to rule out the season, the sales mix and the supplier's prices. But it was a signal that the plan was working.

For a new Amazon seller, pick the number that moves first. Then write the date you will read it, and the line that means revise.

Amazon seller profit dies when two plans fight

A week after Andrew wrote his plans, Sara found him at the table.

"You write that you'll raise prices. And that you'll launch discount cards. Don't those two contradict each other?"

"They do," said Andrew. He added one line: card discounts start once the margin is at least 14%.

Check your own pages the same way. If one plan raises the price and another cuts it with promotions, the business lurches. And pairs that look fine can still claim the same hours and the same money. Put the resources of all plans into one table and you will see it.

Keep each plan to a page. If it doesn't fit, the goal is too big: split it in two. A page forces you to choose, and what you choose is what you will do.

"Is Amazon FBA worth it, or is a plan just paperwork?"

No page can promise that FBA is worth it for you, or change the Amazon seller success rate. A page shows your own answer early.

The revision line does the work. At month 2 Andrew knew whether his plan held, instead of finding out at month 6. Your check date does the same for a launch: it turns "let's see how it goes" into a date and a line.

And the page is short. Five questions, one goal, about 15 minutes.

What six months without a page cost

Leave the margin at 9%, and the coffee shop earns €2,700 a month instead of the €4,500 a 15% margin brings. That is €1,800 a month, or €10,800 over six months.

The reserve pays the same price in time. At 9%, its two-month goal arrives at month 16. With the plan working, at month 10. And at 9%, one breakdown costs a full month of progress. At 15%, a little over half of one.

The human cost is the night. Every big decision gets made after dark, with no line written in a calm hour.

Where the one-page plan stops

The page treats one symptom: you stop pulling levers at random.

The cause is still in place. The page tells you what to do. It doesn't tell you, in week two, where the month will land. And it doesn't say when to change the plan and when to change the goal itself.

Two steps close that gap.

  • Control by forecast. A weekly look at where the month will end, so you correct course before it closes. Chapter 9.
  • Steady mode and manoeuvre. Written conditions for switching the plan or the goal. Chapter 10.

The plan is the fifth step of one loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. The Amazon seller success rate describes thousands of launches. The page describes yours. Without the last step, you still steer by the rear-view mirror.

Key takeaways

  • One goal, one page, five questions.
  • Write what you will not do while you are calm.
  • Add up the levers' effects. They must cover the gap, with a buffer.
  • Read one leading number every week. Write the revision line in advance.
  • If two plans fight, the business lurches. Check every pair.

One page tonight, or another order by feel

You have two choices. Fill in the next order form by feel and hope the first sale comes. Or answer five questions on one page tonight, before you spend.

Next Monday you open the page, read one number, and know whether the plan is on its line.

The page tells you what to do. The forecast and the manoeuvre rules tell you when to correct and when to change course.

Questions people ask

How do I plan my FBA profit margin?

Write the gap first: target margin minus today's. List the levers that act on it, with an estimated effect for each. Their sum must cover the gap with a buffer. Andrew's four levers gave 6.1 points against a gap of 6.0.

How do I know if Amazon FBA is worth it for me?

Write a check date and a revision line before you launch. At the check, read your leading number against that line. Then you decide on a measured result, not on the mood of the month.

What should a new Amazon seller never do?

Write your own list before the first bad month. Andrew's said: no cheaper beans, no price jump above 5% at a time. The complete method, from the goal list to the forecast, is in the full control system.
