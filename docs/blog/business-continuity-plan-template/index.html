Business Continuity Plan Template: Name What Pushes You Off

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

A business continuity plan template for a small business is one sheet of factors: everything that can push the business off course. For each factor, write its direction, its strength in money, the goal it hits and your response: predict it, adjust to it, use it or hold a reserve for it. Then test whether your lever is stronger than the factor. If it is not, no internal fix will save you.

Three people at the tables. Yesterday at the same hour, there was a queue.

The owner opened his spreadsheet and felt his stomach drop. What had he done wrong?

Nothing. It had started to rain.

That one wet Wednesday nearly cost him a plan that was working. He was a step away from changing his prices to fix a problem that the weather had caused.

A continuity plan copied from a template covers the fire, the flood and the server. Then the real threat walks in the front door. A new price list from the supplier. A quiet holiday month. A barista who quits on a Friday.

The answer to the threats nobody wrote down comes from the Jirgenson Model. Name every factor that acts on your path, size it, and pair it with a lever. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

What is a business continuity plan?

A business continuity plan keeps the essential work of a business going through a disruption. It also sets out how to return to normal after it. It answers one question in advance: what keeps us running when something hits?

The weak spot is in the word "something". A list of disasters is not a list of what pushes a small business off course week after week. The model widens the list and adds a direction to every line.

In the Jirgenson Model, an environmental factor is anything that acts on your path to the goal. It carries you, holds you back or pushes you aside. A factor is a role, not a thing. Money is part of your state, but a lack of money is a factor.

The rules of the model are open to read in its GitHub repository.

So a lack of knowledge, of time or of cash goes on the list too. It does not matter whether a factor comes from the market or from you. What matters is that it acts on the path.

Business risks are factors with a direction

Picture a yacht that must sail due north. A crosswind pushes it ten degrees east. Hold a heading of zero, and it lands off to one side. The longer it sails, the further it strays.

The experienced helmsman notices the drift and steers 350 degrees. The wind and the correction cancel out. The yacht sails north. He doesn't curse the wind. He picks the rudder and sets it against the push.

Business risks behave the same way. Each one has a direction (with you, against you or sideways) and a strength. Together they add up to one resultant push, and that push decides where you end up.

Some factors you can weaken or turn to use. Others you cannot change at all: the weather, the season, a general rise in prices. Those you only take into account. Either way, you have to see a factor before you can correct for it.

The business continuity plan template: six steps

Here is the template, filled in with the book's coffee shop, run by Andrew. His figures are a worked illustration: revenue of €30,000 a month, ingredients of €9,000 a month.

1. Pick the goal the plan protects

One goal per sheet. In a coffee shop, the margin and the cash reserve sit at the top, because a hit to the margin drains the reserve.

2. List five factors, including your own shortages

Write what acts on the path. Then add what you lack: knowledge, time, money. Count each item once. Training can be a lever; a lack of knowledge is a factor. Never put the same thing in two columns.

3. Mark the effect and the response

Andrew's sheet:

Factor What it does Response
Rain Sales fall Predict it from the weather forecast
Rising supplier prices The margin falls Do not fight it; adjust the price
The university building nearby More customers will come Use it: build it into the plan for a second location
A coffee machine breakdown Could happen any day Keep a reserve
The coffee shop across the street Competition Keep an eye on it; do not assume it will sit still

Look at the response column. There are only five kinds of answer: predict, adjust, use, reserve, watch. Every line on your own sheet will land in one of them.

4. Size each factor in the units of the goal it hits

A factor without a number is a worry. With a number, it is a line in the budget.

The supplier raises prices by 8%. On €9,000 of ingredients that is €720 more in expenses every month. On €30,000 of revenue, it takes 2.4 points off the margin.

5. Run the compensation test

Now ask the one question that decides continuity: is my lever stronger than this factor?

To win back €720 of profit through price, Andrew needs prices up by 2.4% (2.6% to restore the margin exactly). His plan allows no more than 5% in one step. The lever is stronger than the factor. He can absorb the hit.

Now change one number. Say the supplier raised prices by 30%. The factor becomes €2,700 a month and nine points of margin. Winning that back needs about 9% on prices, about 10% to restore the margin, and some customers will leave. The lever is weaker than the factor.

Supplier rise Cost a month Margin lost Price rise needed Within the 5% limit?
8% €720 2.4 pp 2.4% Yes: compensable
30% €2,700 9 pp about 9% No: the factor wins

When the factor wins, no process inside the business will help. You are rowing a heavy raft against the current. You go with the current or you change rivers. The model calls this offset (c), and the check for it is the Titanic test.

6. Write one lever for the strongest push each way

Find the factor that carries you hardest and the one that pushes you off course hardest. Write one lever next to each. That is fifteen minutes of work.

A business continuity plan example: the bad month

The sheet earned its keep in month 8. Three hits landed at once: the supplier's prices rose by about 8%, the coffee machine needed a repair, and a barista left.

The margin fell from 15% to 12%. The repair ate about 0.1 months of reserve. After the month, the reserve stood at 1.56 months of expenses.

That is yellow, not red. The shop kept trading, and the reserve stayed above its red line of one month.

Two of the three hits were no surprise. The supplier and the breakdown were both on the sheet from the first evening. Each had its response written: adjust the price, keep a reserve. Continuity came from the list, not from luck.

The same method fits a freelancer. Elena, a designer publishing her first book, listed four factors. Clients order less in the holiday weeks. Platforms change their rules without warning. Other authors answer her price. And she knows too little about selling online. Each got a direction, a strength and a lever.

What a missing list costs

Go back to the rainy Wednesday. Without the sheet, Andrew would have read the empty room as proof that the plan had failed. He would have changed his prices. The cause was the weather, so he would have thrown away the part that worked.

Now put money on that mistake. At a 9% margin, the shop's reserve reaches its two-month goal at month 16. With the margin plan working, at month 10. Abandon the plan in the first wet week and you buy back those six months of exposure.

And every surprise costs more on a thin margin. A breakdown worth 0.1 months of expenses sets the goal back by about a month at 9%. At 15%, by 0.57 of a month.

The human cost is the Wednesday itself: an owner who reads every quiet afternoon as a verdict on his own work.

"There are too many risks to list" is the fair objection. It is true: there are infinitely many factors. So don't list them all. List five, starting with your own shortages and the two that push hardest. A sheet of five beats a binder nobody opens.

Where the template stops

Be clear about what the sheet gives you. You see what pushes you, how hard, and whether you can push back. That treats the symptom: being blindsided by a factor you could have named.

The cause of the damage is still in place. The sheet doesn't tell you the point at which a factor's push becomes an emergency. And it shows factors once they strike; it doesn't spot them coming. Triggers set before the bad day are the subject of the contingency plan template.

Two steps close that gap.

  • Zone thresholds. The exact reading at which a push turns from a nuisance into an emergency, set in advance for every goal. Chapter 4.
  • Recognizing a factor before it strikes. Four techniques, from leading indicators to a second observer. They put the supplier's rise on your sheet before the invoice does. Chapter 8.

The sheet is one part of a loop: goals in order, the real state, the gap, levers, plan, forecast. Factors explain the gap; they do not close it. Short videos on that loop run on the Steer, Don't Guess YouTube channel.

The reserve that absorbs a breakdown is easiest to read in months, not euros: see cash reserve months of expenses.

Key takeaways

  • A continuity plan lists factors, not only disasters. Your own lack of time, money or knowledge counts.
  • Give every factor a direction, a strength in money and a response: predict, adjust, use, reserve or watch.
  • Run the compensation test: is your lever stronger than the push?
  • When the factor wins, stop rowing. Go with the current or change rivers.
  • Don't curse the wind. Set the rudder against it.

Questions people ask

What is a business continuity plan?

A written plan that keeps the essential work of a business going through a disruption and back to normal after it. For a small business, the working version is a sheet of the factors that can push it off course, each with a response.

What should a business continuity plan template include?

Five or more factors, each with its direction, its strength in money, the goal it hits and your response. Add a compensation test for the strongest one: can your lever offset it within your own limits?

What are the main business risks for a small business?

Anything that acts on the path to your goals. Weather and season, supplier prices, competitors, a breakdown. And your own shortages of knowledge, time or money. Each one carries you, holds you back or pushes you aside.
