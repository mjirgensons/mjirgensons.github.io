When Your Business Is Struggling Quietly: Set the Warning Lines

Martin Jirgenson6 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

When a business is struggling, the signs are rarely loud. They are gaps: a reserve below its goal, a margin under target, regulars thinning out. Make them visible. For each goal, subtract the goal from the measured value, then set three zones in advance: normal, acceptable, emergency. A gap that crosses the red line is your signal to act.

Most businesses don't crash and burn. They just quietly lock the doors and disappear.

No bailiff. No headline. Only a few weeks that each looked like "a bad week".

Picture yours. The coffee machine broke again. Two regulars stopped coming. The reserve slid a little, then a little more. None of it felt like an emergency. All of it was the same story.

Here is the irony. A business can fade with a queue at the door every morning. Andrew's coffee shop had exactly that, and by the end of each month the money had vanished.

The quiet signs don't ring. You set the alarm yourself, in advance.

Business is struggling? Set the warning lines in one evening

A quiet slide becomes visible the moment every goal has a line. Do this tonight. Andrew set his for five goals in one evening, while his head was still clear. Here is the whole tool, with his entries beside each step.

1. Put the goal and the measured value side by side. No feelings in the second column, only numbers with a source. Andrew: reserve goal 2.0 months of expenses, now 0.5. Margin goal 15%, now 9%.

2. Calculate the error for each row: actual minus goal. For "no more than two complaints a week", count only the complaints above two.

Goal Target Now Error
Cash reserve 2.0 months 0.5 minus 1.5 months
Margin 15% 9% minus 6 points
Regular customers 40% 25% minus 15 points
Complaints per week 2 or fewer 5 plus 3
Owner's hours per week 40 or fewer 65 plus 25

3. Draw three zones for each row. Normal, acceptable, emergency. Put the red line where damage turns hard to reverse. Andrew put it at one month of reserve. A month gives him time to negotiate a deferral, find money and decide. Below a month, that time is gone.

Parameter Normal Acceptable Emergency
Cash reserve, months 2.0 or more 1.0 to under 2.0 under 1.0
Margin, % 14 or more 5 to under 14 under 5
Regular customers, % 36 or more 20 to under 36 under 20
Complaints per week 2 or fewer 3 to 8 over 8
Owner's hours per week 42 or fewer over 42, up to 70 over 70

4. Check each line against your measuring error. A zone can't be narrower than the error of your records. If margin accounting is off by one point, a green band half a point wide means nothing.

5. Sort the errors into zones. Andrew's reserve landed in emergency. The other four rows landed in acceptable. A row with no data, or stale data, gets its own mark: undefined. Measure it first. It is not normal.

6. Write the lines down and leave them alone. Thresholds get set in a calm moment and revised rarely. Moving them in a crisis bends the yardstick to fit the result.

That is the tool. It takes about fifteen minutes once the numbers exist.

How to avoid the quiet slide: read the colours every week. A row that changes colour is the sign. A feeling is not.

In the Jirgenson Model, the error is the gap between the goal and the measured state, row by row. Three zones, set in advance, turn that gap into a decision. Normal: leave it alone. Acceptable: find the cause. Emergency: everything else waits.

A quiet closure starts long before any courtroom

For every business that dies in a courtroom, thirty to fifty just turn off the lights.

The courtroom is loud. The red line is quiet, and it comes first. Act there.

When a row crosses into emergency, follow the red protocol. Check for direct threats to people, safety, mandatory payments and the law. Freeze secondary work. Put time, money and attention on the red row and its levers. If several rows are in the red, save first the one closest to damage you can't undo.

Andrew wanted loyalty cards at once, to win customers back. The table said no. The cards cost money, and that money would come out of a reserve already under a month.

"But you wanted loyalty cards," Sara said. "I did," Andrew replied. "But the reserve is below a month. Until it's back up, I spend on nothing else."

He put the cards aside and felt no guilt. The order decided, not the fatigue.

How to avoid the dark windows: treat red as the engine light, not the scrapyard. It is an order to stop and act now, while the engine is still in one piece.

"We were doing fine": the early warning signs sit in the yellow zone

A third of owners who shut down thought the business was doing fine when they closed it.

"Fine" is a feeling. Andrew felt his margin was about twenty per cent. The table said 9%.

The yellow zone is the most treacherous. Nothing in it is on fire, so nothing seems urgent. Yet the work there is to find the cause while that is still cheap.

Ask three questions of every yellow row:

  1. What has changed since last time?
  2. Since when?
  3. Which lever affects it?

Andrew's answer for the margin: waste and unprofitable menu items. For regular customers he didn't know yet. So he wrote "find out" in his task list. That counts as a result too.

How to avoid false comfort: leave green rows alone. Fixing what works costs money and buys nothing. Spend the attention on yellow.

Is my business in trouble? Count your stability margin

Every year, one UK company in 190 goes under. Count the shops on your street.

Some of those shops had a queue too. What they lacked was room to absorb a shock.

Every business has a stability margin: how hard a hit it can take without crossing a line. A broken coffee machine is a nuisance for a coffee shop with two months of reserve. For one with two weeks, it is an emergency.

So build the margin before you need it. Put the cash reserve in your goal list. Andrew ranked it number one: he would defend it to the end.

How to avoid being the next empty window on the street: measure the reserve in months of expenses, not in currency. Months tell you how long you can last.

Problems in order of arrival, or in order of priority

Here is the same week two ways. Illustration.

The queue of problems The zones
Monday The milk supplier raises prices. You argue for an hour. Which goal? Margin. Zone? Acceptable. Note the cause.
Wednesday Sara asks for a second barista on Fridays. You agree. You're tired. New cost, red reserve. It waits.
Friday Loyalty cards go to print. Frozen until the reserve is back above a month.
Month end The money ran out before the time did. The reserve moves towards the yellow zone.

The queue of incoming problems runs on chance. The queue of decisions runs on priority.

What the quiet slide costs in six months

Put the gaps into money. A margin of 9% instead of 15% on revenue of €30,000 means €1,800 less profit every month. The goal yields €4,500. The real figure is €2,700.

The reserve gap is bigger: 1.5 months of expenses, or €40,950.

If nothing changes, the reserve reaches its goal at month 16, not month 12. By the end of month 3 it sits at about 0.8, still in the red. With the margin plan working, the goal arrives at month 10.

Six extra months with no cushion. One breakdown in that stretch pushes the goal back about a month.

And the owner pays in hours. Andrew could not remember the last time he had eaten lunch at home.

"I have no time for dashboards"

Spotting the signs your business is failing takes no software. You need five lines on the sheet you already have. Setting the zones takes about fifteen minutes.

Compare that with one month of the margin gap. Or with one loyalty campaign paid out of a reserve under a month.

Where the warning lines stop

The zones are the third link of one loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. They treat the symptom. You no longer miss the quiet signs.

The cause is still there. A red row tells you how serious things are. It doesn't tell you what to change. You can't order money to appear. And the lines show today, not where the trend goes next.

Two steps close that gap.

  • The list of levers. What you can change yourself, today, and what only looks like a lever. Chapter 5.
  • Two forecasts and control by forecast. "If nothing changes" against "if the plan works", so you act before a row turns red. Chapters 8 and 9.

Without them, the zones ring the alarm. They don't yet put out the fire.

Key takeaways

  • The signs your business is failing are gaps, not noises. Measure them.
  • Error is actual minus goal. Three zones per row turn it into a decision.
  • Draw the red line where damage turns hard to reverse, and draw it in a calm moment.
  • In yellow, find the cause while it's cheap. In green, leave it alone.
  • Red is an engine light, not a verdict.

Two choices before the next bad week

You have two choices this week. Keep reading each slow month as bad luck, and hope the next one turns. Or put three lines on every goal tonight, so the slide shows up as a colour.

Next Monday you look at colours first, and the decision is already made.

The zones tell you where the danger is. The levers and the two forecasts tell you what to pull and when. If a row is already deep in the red, start with how to save a failing business.

Questions people ask

What are the warning signs of a failing business?

Gaps that grow while each week feels normal: a reserve below its goal, a margin under target, fewer regulars, more complaints, longer owner hours. They become signs only when each has a line drawn in advance.

How do I know if my business is in trouble?

Subtract each goal from its measured value. Then check the zone. Any row past its red line means trouble now. A row with no data is a warning in itself: measure it first.

What should I do first when my business is failing?

Check for direct threats to people, safety, payments and the law. Then freeze secondary work and put everything on the red row. The complete method, with levers and forecasts, is in the full control system.
