Business Transformation: Find the Stage Nobody Is Running

Martin Jirgenson6 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

A business transformation passes through eight stages: notice the pressure, name the situation, set ordered goals, build the plan, build structures, observe, improve, then finish or maintain. A skipped stage does not vanish. Someone else performs it, and rarely for you. Write one sentence per stage for your change, find the empty one, and fill it first.

Only one leader in four calls their transformation a real success.

Picture the kickoff. A new logo, a new system, a slide with a rocket on it. A year later the Monday meeting has the same argument, in the same room, about the same numbers.

The irony is hard to miss. The new software has a budget line of its own. Nobody can say who looks at the gap between goal and fact, or when.

The villain is not the people. It is the stage of the change that nobody runs.

Business transformation in eight stages: find the empty one

Whatever you are changing, a shop, a service or a whole company, the change passes through the same eight stages. Stages 1 and 8 are always there. The stages between can merge or split, but none can be dropped.

In the Jirgenson Model, a full control cycle means eight linked stages, from pressure to maintenance. The first four set the destination and the rule for acting. The next three do the work and adjust it. The last one closes the loop. Drop a stage, and its job is left undone or done by other hands.

Here is the audit. Take your hardest change and write one sentence for each stage. Next to each one, see how Elena filled it in. She is a freelance designer, and her change was large: from selling hours to selling a product.

No. Stage Ask this Elena's line
1 Notice the pressure What forces me to act? Is it real? Income swings between €800 and €2,200; one client brings 70%
2 Name the situation Would a second observer see the same thing? She needs a product that does not depend on her hours
3 Set the goals Is every goal measurable, in order? €3,000 a month by month 14
4 Build the plan Is there a plan per goal? Can I foresee the result? A manuscript in 120 hours, a set price, a page for the book
5 Build structures Does every area have an owner with authority and resources? A payment service, a subscriber list, automated emails
6 Observe Who looks at the gap, and when? Every week: visits to the page (goal 350) and the share who buy
7 Improve What did the gap between forecast and fact teach? Below 1.2% buying for two weeks running: rewrite the headline
8 Finish or maintain Who safeguards the result? 21 sales a month, then the book sells without her daily work

Now read your own column. One line will be empty or vague. The usual gaps are stage 3, where goals are still wishes, and stage 6, where nobody watches on a fixed day. Pick one first action to fill the gap. The whole audit takes fifteen minutes.

Why does the empty line matter so much? Because the function is needed anyway. Leave stage 3 blank, and a bank, a rival or the market will write your goal. Leave stage 6 blank, and your debts do the watching. Leave stages 1 and 2 blank, and a crisis names the situation, at the worst possible hour.

How to avoid it: before the kickoff, show the eight lines to one person outside the project. An empty line is cheap on paper and costly in month six.

Why change fails at the stage everyone thinks is done

Stage 2 looks easy, so it gets skipped. It decides which levers come into play.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop called "The Corner", could have called his trouble "a bad season". That name calls for patience. The measured name was different: a 9% margin where he believed twenty, and no cash cushion. That name sends him to prices, the menu and purchasing.

Name it wrong, and the right levers go to the wrong problem. So run the check: would another observer, using the same numbers, name it the same way?

How to avoid it: write the name of the situation with numbers in it. "A bad season" has none.

Merger integration: why mergers fail before day one

Half of big mergers destroy value. Fewer than one in five create any.

A merger joins two sets of goals and two versions of the truth. Start the integration with one page, not one org chart.

First, write one goal list for the combined business, in refusal order. Ask: if things went badly, which goal would we give up first, and which last? Where there are employees, customers and partners, that order has to fit their rights and obligations too.

Second, give every number one source. When the two halves count revenue two ways, the argument over the right figure eats more energy than the business.

Third, audit the starting state before any plan. A medium-sized company books €100,000 of stock in its warehouse. A physical stocktake finds half of it out of date or spoiled. The real state is €50,000. Any plan built on the larger number starts from a floor that does not exist.

How to avoid it: no integration step starts until both halves read the same goal list and the same numbers.

Digital transformation failure: the budget with no red line

Canada's government pay system cost sixteen times its budget.

A budget row needs a red line written before the first invoice. Give the budget three zones. Green: within tolerance. Yellow: worse than normal, find the cause while it is cheap. Red: past the limit, everything else waits.

Then put stage 6 on the calendar. Name who looks at the gap between budget and spend, and how often. Weekly for fast numbers, monthly for slow ones.

When a number crosses into the red, follow the red protocol. Check for any direct threat to people, payments or the law. Freeze secondary work. Put time, money and attention on the red number.

Respect the loop's own speed. Andrew's loop takes about 45 days: noticing, deciding, acting, and the guests responding. A checkpoint set earlier measures noise, not the lever.

How to avoid it: set the zones in a calm hour, before the start. A line moved in the middle of a crisis bends the yardstick to fit the result.

Change management failure: what six more months of an empty stage cost

Here is what one skipped stage costs, in the coffee shop's own numbers.

Andrew planned to drop an item from the menu. Weekly observation, stage 6, showed that item brought the regulars in. Without it, the item would have gone. A month later he would have seen fewer regulars and no reason. He would have gone back to loyalty cards and spent money from a reserve already in the red. Observation caught the error in month two, not month six.

The reserve shows the scale. At a 9% margin it grows about 0.1 months a month. If nothing changes, it reaches the two-month goal at month 16, against a deadline of 12. At 15%, it arrives at month 10. A breakdown worth 0.1 months of reserve costs about a month of progress at 9%, and about half a month at 15%.

And the human cost: an owner who cannot take a day off, because without him the plan on the wall does not move.

"Transformation is for corporations"

It is not. The same eight stages ran Elena's one-person business and Andrew's coffee shop with five numbers. A small business does not need a team per stage. It needs a name next to each one.

The one-page version is enough to start: eight lines, one owner per line, one Monday meeting.

Where the eight-stage audit stops

Be honest about what the audit fixes. It treats the symptom: no stage of your change runs unowned.

The deeper cause is still in place. A transformation is not a steady course. It moves the goal itself, from one state to another. You do not yet know how far you may stray at each checkpoint, or the condition for starting and stopping. And your loop still reacts to the past rather than acting on the next three months.

Two steps close that gap.

  • Steady mode and manoeuvre. The tolerance corridor along the route and the written launch conditions. Chapter 10.
  • Control by forecast. Acting on "if nothing changes" against "if the plan works", before the damage shows. Chapter 9.

The eight stages are the frame of one loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. Without the next two steps, they tell you who does what. They do not yet keep the change on course. For the croissant test that shows why reacting runs late, read the control cycle.

Key takeaways

  • Every change passes through eight stages. None can be dropped.
  • A stage you skip still runs, by other hands and on their terms.
  • Write one sentence per stage and fill the empty line first.
  • In a merger: one goal list, one source per number, one audited starting state.
  • A red line goes on the budget before the first invoice.

Two choices before the next kickoff

You have two choices. Launch the change the usual way, with a slide and a hope, and learn in month six which stage nobody ran. Or write eight lines tonight and find the empty one.

The audit gives every stage an owner. The corridor, the launch conditions and the forecast keep the change on course once it moves.

Questions people ask

Why do transformations fail?

When one stage of the change has no owner, someone else runs it. The goals stay wishes, or nobody watches the gap between goal and fact. Write one sentence per stage, find the empty line, and give it a name and a date.

Why do mergers fail, and how does merger integration work?

Two companies bring two goal orders and two versions of each number. Start integration with one goal list in refusal order, one source per number, and an audit of the real starting state.

How do you stop a digital transformation from running over budget?

Give the budget three zones before the start, with a red line set in a calm hour. Name who checks it, and how often. The full method adds launch and stopping conditions for the whole route.
