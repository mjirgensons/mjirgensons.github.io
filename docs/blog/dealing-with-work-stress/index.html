Dealing With Work Stress: Let the Sheet Carry the Alarm

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

Dealing with work stress starts with moving the alarm out of your body. Write the numbers that keep you awake as rows. On a calm day, give each one three zones: green, leave it alone; yellow, find the cause; red, everything else waits. Then the sheet rings instead of your nerves, and a bad day gets a rule instead of a mood.

Your stomach knew the cash was low before the bank did.

It told you at your kid's birthday, between the cake and the candles. It told you again at dinner, when your hand went to the phone for the fourth time. And it told you at 3 a.m., while your partner slept with their back to you and you did the sums on the ceiling.

Four out of ten owners have hidden business stress from their own family.

Here is the odd part. You have a smoke alarm in the kitchen and a warning light for the engine oil. Your business has neither. So your body took the job.

You can work. Twelve hours, six days, no problem. What breaks you is carrying every number on your back with no system. Nobody drew the line where "fine" ends and "fire" begins, so every number burns a little, all day.

The Jirgenson Model gives you the answer: draw the lines in advance, on paper, and let the paper ring. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

One plain line first. This is a tool for the chaos in the work, not a treatment. If the bad nights last more than a bad week, see a doctor or call a crisis line. That help comes first.

Dealing with work stress: your nerves are doing a gauge's job

Look at an engine temperature gauge. Needle in green: drive on. Yellow: pay closer attention. Red: stop. You don't strip the engine to read it. You look at the sector.

Your work has numbers like that engine: cash, margin, hours, complaints, unpaid invoices. But nobody drew the sectors. So every reading comes in raw, and your nerves judge it on the spot, tired, at night.

That is stress from work in one sentence: a raw number with no line forces a judgment, and a tired head calls everything urgent.

In the Jirgenson Model, the gap between a goal and today's value is the error. Every goal gets three zones set in advance: normal, acceptable and emergency. The zones turn a raw gap into a decision. They are drawn on a calm day, so the worst day doesn't have to draw them.

Draw the lines while the fire is out. Moving them mid-crisis bends the yardstick to fit the result.

Set the alarm on paper in four steps

You need one sheet and fifteen minutes. Here is the full tool, filled in with Andrew's coffee shop. He drew his lines the evening he wrote his goals, while his head was still clear.

1. Turn each worry into a row with a number

"Money is tight" can't ring. "Cash reserve: 0.5 months of expenses" can. For each thing that wakes you, write the goal and today's value.

Andrew had five rows: cash reserve, margin, regular customers, complaints per week, his own hours per week. Today's values were 0.5 months, 9%, 25%, five complaints and 65 hours.

2. Draw the red line where damage gets hard to undo

Ask of each row: at what point would the harm become hard to reverse? Put the red line there.

Andrew put the reserve's red line at one month. A month gives him time to ask for a deferral, find money and decide. Below that, there is no time left.

3. Draw the green line where you leave it alone

Green means no action. Make the green band at least as wide as your measuring error. If your margin figure can be off by a point, a green band half a point wide will flicker all week.

4. Write what each colour makes you do

  • Green: do nothing. Fixing what works is a classic way to waste a week.
  • Yellow: find the cause while it is cheap. Ask three questions. What changed since last time? Since when? Which lever touches it?
  • Red: everything else waits until this row is back in yellow.

Andrew's finished sheet:

Row 🟢 Normal 🟡 Acceptable 🔴 Emergency Today Zone
Cash reserve, months 2.0 or more 1.0 to under 2.0 under 1.0 0.5 🔴
Margin, % 14 or more 5 to under 14 under 5 9 🟡
Regular customers, % 36 or more 20 to under 36 under 20 25 🟡
Complaints a week 2 or fewer 3 to 8 over 8 5 🟡
Owner's hours a week 42 or fewer over 42 to 70 over 70 65 🟡

One red, four yellow. That is not a verdict on Andrew. It is a reading. For the longer method behind each line, see how to set KPI zones.

How to handle stress when the sheet says red

Andrew's first thought that evening was loyalty cards, to win the customers back. But the cards cost money: printing, discounts, a change to the till. That money would come out of a reserve already under a month.

The sheet said no. Red first.

He told Sara, his senior barista. "But you wanted loyalty cards," she said. "I did," Andrew replied. "But the reserve is in the red zone." Regulars drift away slowly. A red reserve can end everything fast.

He put the cards aside and, for the first time, felt no guilt. An order chosen with a clear head made the decision, not fatigue.

That is the difference, side by side (an illustration):

Alarm in your body Alarm on the sheet
The trigger A feeling at 3 a.m. A row crosses a line drawn in daylight
The question "Is this bad?" "Which colour is it?"
The answer Whatever the loudest worry wants Red first, yellow next, green waits
The cost Every number burns a little, all day One row burns; the rest stay quiet

If two rows turn red, save first the goal you would give up last. That order is the refusal test, and it takes ten minutes.

Manager stress: the permission to leave green alone

Managers carry a special version of this. Every number on the desk seems to ask for a reply, and a reply looks like work. So the green rows get "improved" too. Fifty tracked KPIs, and nobody can say if Friday's payroll is safe.

The zones give you a written permission: green means hands off. Control includes the ability to leave things alone. A calm green row is not neglect. It is the system doing its job. For the panel itself in one picture, read the traffic light dashboard. For the night when every worry shouts at once, sort them into zones first.

Stress and family: what the sheet says at dinner

The partner with their back to you doesn't need the spreadsheet. They need one sentence they can trust. Without zones, you say nothing, or you say "it's bad". With the sheet, you can say this. "One row is red: the cash reserve. Everything else waits until it is above a month. The rest is yellow, and I know why." A fact and a plan, in fifteen seconds.

Andrew said almost that to Sara. She pushed back, and he had an answer, because it lived on paper, not in his mood. To see what cash cover means in months, start with the reserve in months of expenses.

What six more months of alarm by nerves cost

Andrew's red row, the reserve, was 1.5 months short. At monthly expenses of €27,300, that is a gap of €40,950. His yellow margin, 6 points short on €30,000 of revenue, cost him €1,800 of profit every month. Six more months of hoping: €10,800 he never sees.

Now time. If nothing changes, his reserve reaches two months at month 16. With the margin plan working, at month 10. And at a 9% margin, a single breakdown costs a full month of progress. At 15%, about half of one.

And the human cost: six more months of the phone at dinner and the ceiling at 3 a.m.

"My work is not a spreadsheet"

It doesn't have to be. Alex is thirteen and saving for a bicycle. On 1 July he is €30 behind his schedule. His lines: up to €10 behind is normal, up to €40 acceptable, more is an emergency. So he is yellow, he knows the cause, and he doesn't panic. If a teenager can draw three lines, you can draw them for your hours and your cash. No software, no maths.

Burnout from work: where the zones stop

Be honest about what you built. The zones treat a symptom: the alarm living in your body. The cause of the next red row is still in place. You know a row is red, but not what to pull to bring it back. And you see red only when it arrives, not three months before.

Two steps close that gap:

  • The list of levers. What you can change yourself, today, and how fast each one works. Without it, a red row produces ten minutes of "we must do something". Chapter 5. The idea in brief: business levers.
  • Two forecasts. "If nothing changes" against "if the plan works", in numbers and dates. Without them, the alarm rings late. Chapter 8.

Burnout from work grows in the gap between "I see the red" and "I know what to do". If your own hours sit at the top of yellow, read business owner burnout next. If the exhaustion lasts weeks, talk to a doctor.

The zones are one step of the whole loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. They are the step that tells you when to act.

Key takeaways

  • Stress from work grows when every number arrives raw, with no line to judge it.
  • Draw three lines per row on a calm day: red where damage gets hard to undo, green where you leave it alone.
  • Green means hands off. Yellow means find the cause. Red means everything else waits.
  • Let the sheet ring so your stomach doesn't have to.

Two choices before bed

You have two choices before bed. Keep the alarm in your body, check the phone at dinner again, and do the sums on the ceiling at 3 a.m. Or take one sheet and draw three lines per row while your head is still clear.

The sheet tells you when to act. The levers and the two forecasts tell you what to do, in time.

Questions people ask

How do you reduce work stress without quitting?

Move the alarm out of your head. Write the numbers that worry you as rows and draw green, yellow and red lines for each on a calm day. Act on red, look for causes in yellow, leave green alone.

How do I handle stress from work at home with my family?

Give your family one honest sentence instead of silence: which row is red, what you are doing about it, and that the rest is yellow. A fact and a plan calm a home faster than "it's fine".

When is stress from work more than a bad week?

When sleep, mood or dread stay bad for weeks, see a doctor or call a crisis line. The zones help with the chaos in the work. They are not a treatment.
