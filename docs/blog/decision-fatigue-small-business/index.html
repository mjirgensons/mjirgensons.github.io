Decision Fatigue in a Small Business: Decide Once, While Calm

Martin Jirgenson31 Oct 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer

Decision fatigue is the slide in the quality of your choices when you are tired and have decided too much already. In a small business the cure is not more willpower. Make three decisions in a calm hour: the order of your goals, the red line for each number, and what you will never do. Then the page decides when you cannot.

The decision that sinks a small business is rarely made in a meeting.

It is made at eleven at night, by the most tired person in the building, in under a minute.

Andrew runs a coffee shop. Picture his week if he had never ranked his goals. The supplier raises the price of milk. Then Sara asks for another barista on Fridays. Andrew says yes. He is tired, and he does not want to argue.

A month later he runs out of money before he runs out of time.

Nobody would have chosen that. Fatigue would.

What decision fatigue is

In plain words, decision fatigue is what happens to your judgement after a long day of choices. You stop weighing. You take the easiest yes. You put off the hard call.

You can price a latte to the cent. Then the choice that decides the year gets made by whoever asked last.

The usual advice is to rest more, decide fewer things, or try harder. All fine. None of it helps on the night the phone rings at the worst moment.

The fix is structural. You cannot stop being tired. You can stop being the one who decides when you are tired.

In the Jirgenson Model, you make the hard decisions in advance, in a calm moment. The order of your goals. The zones for each number. The limits you will not cross. On a bad day you do not decide from scratch. You read what you decided with a clear head.

Decide once, while calm: the calm-hour sheet

Take one evening this week. Write three decisions and one rule on a single sheet. Here is each one, with Andrew's entry next to it.

1. The order: what you defend to the last

Ask one question of your goals: if things got bad, which would I give up first, and which last?

Andrew's answer: cash reserve first, his own hours last. Without a reserve the shop closes, and then nothing else matters.

This is the step people skip, because it feels obvious. It is not. Until the order exists on paper, every crisis sets it for you.

2. The red line for each number

For each number, write where trouble starts. Not a feeling. A figure.

Andrew set his red line for the cash reserve at one month of expenses. His reason: a month gives him time to negotiate a deferral, find money and decide. Below a month, there is no time left for any of that.

He set the lines for all five numbers that same evening, while his head was clear.

3. What you will never do

Write the moves you refuse, even if they would rescue the goal.

Andrew's list: never cheaper coffee beans. Never delay wages. Never raise prices by more than 5% in one step.

A tired mind offers quick, foolish fixes. A written "never" stops them faster than any pep talk.

4. One rule for the inbox

Receive problems in the order they arrive. Resolve them in order of priority.

When a new request lands, ask it three questions. Which goal does it belong to? Which zone is that number in now? Is it more important than what I am doing right now? Only then does it earn a place in the queue.

That is the whole sheet. It takes about an evening. Writing his five goals in order took Andrew fifteen minutes.

The queue against the order

Here is one ordinary Monday, run two ways. An illustration, built from the coffee shop's numbers.

What arrives The tired way: in order of arrival The calm-hour way: in order of priority
The supplier calls about a price rise Agree on the phone Margin row: yellow. Note it, check the levers this week
A staff member asks to approve a purchase Approve, to end the conversation Reserve row: red. Nothing that does not bring it back
A complaint comes in Spend the morning on it Complaints row: yellow. Find the cause, no panic
The cash reserve slides towards the line Nobody calls about it, so nobody acts Red. This comes first, today

The left column is not lazy. It is tired. The loudest call wins because the most important problem never phones you. It does not raise its voice.

The ten-second test

Try this now. Can you name, in ten seconds, the goal you would defend to the last?

And the number at which your business is in real trouble?

If both answers come at once, the calm-hour work is done. If you hesitate, someone else is answering for you. The late supplier. The client who has not paid. Your own exhaustion.

The cost of letting fatigue decide

Tiredness does not only feel bad. It costs money.

In his first months Andrew worked sixty-five hours a week and made decisions late at night. Those were the hours of his worst mistakes. He mixed up figures, put off calling the supplier and gave wrong deadlines. Fatigue corrupts a forecast like a faulty instrument.

Now the money. His reserve stood at 0.5 months against a goal of 2.0. With monthly expenses of €27,300, that gap is €40,950. His margin was 9% against a goal of 15%. On revenue of €30,000 a month, that is €1,800 of profit lost every month: €2,700 earned instead of €4,500.

Leave it for six more months, and the margin gap alone costs €10,800. All while the owner keeps deciding at midnight.

The goal for the owner's own hours is not a luxury. It protects the quality of every other decision. The person at the wheel must be able to look ahead.

What changes when the order exists

That same evening Andrew's first thought was loyalty cards, to win customers back. A sensible idea. The table said no: the reserve was red, and the cards would cost money it did not have.

He put the cards aside. For the first time, he felt no guilt.

The decision had been made by the order he chose with a clear head, not by fatigue. And the question "What do I do?" had an answer: bring the reserve back above one month.

That is the real gift of the calm hour. Not more discipline. Less guilt, and fewer decisions per day.

What the calm hour does not fix

Be clear about what the sheet gives you. It treats the symptom: tired choices made from scratch. It does not yet treat the cause.

The cause is a business with no instrument panel. You have a red line for each number, but three gaps remain.

  • How to set every threshold so it holds. Three rules for a line that does not flicker or lie. Then the red protocol, for the week when two or three numbers turn red at once. Chapter 4.
  • The Red Card. Eight steps for the day your hands shake and the sheet feels far away. It runs from the danger check to the one person you tell out loud. It sits on the last pages, built for the worst night.

Name them now. Learn them before the bad day, not during it.

Key takeaways

  • Decision fatigue is not a character flaw. It is a missing structure.
  • Make three decisions in a calm hour: the order of your goals, a red line for each number, and what you will never do.
  • Receive problems as they arrive; resolve them by priority.
  • The most dangerous problem never phones you. Put it on the page.
  • Fatigue makes decisions. It never signs them.

Two choices for tonight

You have two choices. Keep deciding at midnight, one tired yes at a time, and let the loudest request steer the shop. Or spend one calm evening and write the sheet.

The sheet stops the tired yes. The thresholds, the red protocol and the Red Card keep you steady when everything breaks in the same week.

Questions people ask

What is decision fatigue?

The drop in the quality of your choices after a long run of decisions. Tired people take the easiest option, avoid hard calls and say yes to whoever asks last.

How do business owners avoid decision fatigue?

Move the hard decisions to a calm hour. Write down the order of your goals, a red line for each key number, and the actions you will never take. On a bad day, read the sheet instead of deciding from scratch.

What is a red line in a small business?

The value at which a number turns from a worry into an emergency. For a cash reserve it might be one month of expenses: below it, there is no time left to negotiate, find money or decide calmly.
