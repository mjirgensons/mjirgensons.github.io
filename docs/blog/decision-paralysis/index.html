Decision Paralysis: Pull One Lever You Can Undo, Starting Today

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

Decision paralysis is being stuck between options because every choice feels final. Break it with levers: list what you can change yourself, today. Ask three questions of each one: can I move it myself today, when will the effect show, and can I undo it? Start with the fast lever you can reverse, and pull only one at a time.

The decision you're afraid to get wrong is already being made. By default.

Sunday evening. Your partner asks the question for the third week running. "So are you raising prices or not?" You say you need to think. You've been thinking since September.

Meanwhile the margin keeps leaking. The decision you won't make is making itself.

You lie awake running the same two films. In one, you raise prices and the customers walk out. In the other, you don't, and the money runs out. Both endings feel final. So you choose neither.

Here is the turn. You can work. You can carry a week no one else could carry. What freezes you isn't laziness or a lack of confidence. Every option sits in your head as one giant, final bet. You have no system to cut it into small steps you can take back.

The Jirgenson Model gives the answer as a control system: you stop choosing between futures and start moving levers. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

What decision paralysis is, and why it locks you

In plain words, decision paralysis is being stuck between options. The fear of choosing wrong outweighs the cost of waiting.

The irony is that waiting is also a choice. It's the one choice nobody ever writes down, so nobody ever checks what it cost.

The lock usually has one cause. You're trying to decide on the result. "Fix the margin." "Get more customers." "Save the business." A result can't be pulled. So every option looks huge, vague and final.

In the Jirgenson Model, a lever is a parameter you can reach and change by your own decision. The set of your levers is the control vector. A result, such as margin or sales, is never a lever: you move the lever, and the result follows.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, met this lock at his own morning briefing. "We need profit," he told Sara, his senior barista. "We have to do something."

"Do what, exactly?" Sara asked.

He didn't know. They talked for ten minutes and parted feeling they'd discussed the weather.

How to break decision paralysis: three questions for any lever

This is the tool. It takes about ten minutes and works on any goal.

Step 1. Write five actions, then cross out

Pick one goal that worries you. Write four or five actions you could take in the next few days to move it.

Now cross out everything that depends on other people, the weather, the market or luck. What's left is your control vector for this goal.

Andrew took a clean sheet and wrote down everything that came to mind. Then he crossed things out. The weather? Not his lever. Rent and taxes? Given conditions. Foot traffic in the street? Not his. Four levers remained: the prices of lattes and cappuccinos, the menu, purchasing from the milk supplier, and portion size.

He reread the short list and felt calmer. Now he had four things he could tackle the next morning.

Step 2. Ask three questions of every lever

Before you pull anything, ask:

  1. Can I move it myself, today?
  2. How long before the effect shows?
  3. Can I undo it if it doesn't work?

Here are Andrew's answers, straight from his sheet:

Lever Myself, today? When the effect comes Can I undo it?
Prices Yes In weeks; customers take time to adjust Hard: customers remember the jump
Menu Yes Quickly Easy: an item can come back
Purchasing Through negotiation In a month, after a new contract Hard: the contract is signed
Portion size Yes Immediately Easy, up to the point where quality suffers

Look at what the table does to fear. The paralysing question was "raise prices or not?", one giant bet. The table shows two levers that are fast and easy to undo. A wrong choice there can be taken back next week.

Step 3. Start fast and reversible, and pull one at a time

The order follows from the table. First what is fast and reversible: the menu and portion size. Then what is slow or hard to undo: prices and the supplier contract.

Then the rule that ends the freeze for good: one lever at a time. When Andrew later faced a bad month, he chose a 5% price rise and started with that single lever, so he could see its effect. Two levers on the same day give you one result and no idea which lever earned it.

He also capped every price step at 5%. A small step is a test, not a bet. A limit like that made his experiment readable.

Step 4. Give the lever a leading indicator

Margin for the month is a lagging number. It tells you what already happened. So every money lever gets one leading indicator: for the price lever, the number of receipts in the first two weeks after the change.

Now the fear has a number. Andrew's sum showed that a 5% rise pays off as long as fewer than 6.7% of customers leave. "What if they leave?" stopped being a nightmare. It became a count at the till. More on choosing that early number is in lagging vs leading indicators.

Too many decisions? It's the same test

The test works far from a coffee shop. Elena, a freelance designer, wrote her first list for her book launch: "more social media, increase sales, be more visible". Every item sounded businesslike. Not one could be done tomorrow.

She asked herself: what exactly do I do with my own hands? The new list read: write three chapters, prepare the book's page, collect the first hundred addresses. Those were levers. The paralysis ended the day the list changed. Her whole launch plan is in book launch plan.

Alex, who is thirteen and saving €300 for a bicycle, started with "get money for my birthday". His mother pointed out that this depended on his uncle. Alex crossed it out. What remained: €20 a month from pocket money, about €30 a month from jobs for the neighbours, and about €60, once, for his old scooter. Every action was his own.

Clarity didn't come from thinking harder. It came from crossing out what wasn't a lever. When the day hands you too many decisions, the same sort cuts the pile: see decision fatigue for why the tired evening decides worst.

What waiting costs in money

Price the default choice with Andrew's numbers.

At M0 his margin was 9%. On revenue of €30,000 a month, profit was €2,700. As an illustration, with prices up 5% and 2% of customers gone, revenue becomes €30,870, expenses €27,120 and profit €3,750. That is a margin of about 12%, three points of the six he needed, from one lever.

The gap between pulling it and waiting is €1,050 a month. Six more months of "I need to think" costs €6,300 that never reaches the reserve. And that's one lever. The menu and the portions were waiting in the same queue.

How to read your own margin before you decide is in how to calculate profit margin.

The human cost, in one sentence: six more Sunday evenings answering the same question with "I need to think".

"What if I pull the wrong lever?"

Then you undo it. That's why you asked the third question.

A wrong lever that is fast and reversible costs you a week. A right decision you never make costs you every week. The table turns "I might be wrong" into "I'll know by Friday".

If the fear doesn't lift even when the numbers are clear, or worry keeps you awake for weeks, that is worth a doctor's time. The lever list treats the chaos in the business, not an illness.

Next Monday, you stop asking what to do with your life's work. You ask which of four levers moves first. The sheet already says: the fast one you can undo.

Where the lever list stops

Be honest about what you built. The list ends the freeze: you know what you can move, in what order, one at a time. It treats the symptom.

The cause of the next freeze is still there. A single lever pulled on a Monday is not yet a plan. You haven't written what resources it gets, by when it should work, or what you will never do even to save the goal. And you don't yet know the exact number at which the result says "revise".

Two steps of the system close that gap:

  • The control concept, one page per goal. Levers, resources, constraints, a leading indicator, a deadline and an owner. Chapter 6.
  • Zone thresholds set in advance. The lines where a result is normal, acceptable or an emergency, so you know when to stop or push. Chapter 4.

Without them, the list tells you what you can do. It doesn't yet tell you how far to go, or when the plan has failed.

The list is one part of a loop: goals in order, the real state, the gap, levers, a plan, a forecast. Each part replaces one giant decision with a small, checkable step. Key takeaways

  • Decision paralysis comes from deciding on a result. Decide on a lever instead.
  • Cross out everything that depends on others, the weather or luck.
  • Three questions: myself today? when does it show? can I undo it?
  • Fast and reversible first. One lever at a time.
  • A wrong lever you can undo costs a week. A decision never made costs every week.

Two choices for this Sunday

You have two choices this Sunday. Keep weighing two giant futures in your head and let the default decide for you, one quiet week at a time. Or write five actions on a sheet tonight, cross out what isn't yours, and pull the one you can undo.

The sheet ends the freeze. The one-page plan and the zone thresholds make sure the lever you pull leads somewhere, and tell you when to stop.

Questions people ask

What is decision paralysis?

Decision paralysis is being stuck between options because the fear of a wrong choice outweighs the cost of waiting. In a business it often means trying to decide on a result, like "fix the margin", instead of a lever you can move today.

How do I overcome decision paralysis when I lack confidence?

Shrink the decision. List the actions you can take yourself, ask which ones are fast and easy to undo, and pull one of those first. Confidence follows evidence: a small step you can reverse shows you something real within a week or two.

How do I make decisions when there are too many to make?

Cross out every option that depends on other people, the weather or luck. Ask three questions of what remains: can I do it today, when will it show, can I undo it? Then do one at a time. The book's Chapter 5 has the full lever method.
