Decisions Under Uncertainty: A Decision Log That Looks Ahead

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

Make decisions under uncertainty by forecast, not by habit or by yesterday's result. Before you act, write the forecast as a range, the assumptions behind it, the deviation you will ignore and the date you will check. Then compare the forecast with the facts and correct it. A decision log holds all of this on one line per decision.

Nearly half of owners lie awake at night worrying about the business.

Lie awake once and you'll notice something. The decision is already made. What keeps you up is not knowing if it was right, and finding out too late.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, ordered a hundred croissants every morning. Habit set the number. On Tuesday nearly a third ended up in the bin. On Saturday the case stood empty before ten. He blamed luck.

It wasn't luck. It was a control system, and a primitive one.

Bad luck is the name we give to a pattern nobody wrote down. The answer to this pain comes from the Jirgenson Model: decide by a written forecast, keep the feedback, and let the facts correct the forecast. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

What decisions under uncertainty are

A decision under uncertainty is one you must make before you can know the outcome. You order stock before the customers arrive. You hire before the work is certain. You set a price before you see how buyers react.

The usual advice says "trust your gut" or "gather more data". Neither tells you what to write down on Monday.

The model starts from one plain rule. "Random" is a description, not a denial of causes. Milk prices jump about for someone who looks at them once a month. For a supplier who knows the milking schedule, they are predictable. So the first question is always: can I learn more about the mechanism?

Some uncertainty always remains. You still decide, with that uncertainty in view. You give a range instead of one false number.

In the Jirgenson Model, a decision under uncertainty is a forecast you act on. You write the forecast, its range and its assumptions before you act. New facts then correct the forecast, and feedback stays in place, because forecasts err.

Three ways to decide, one display case

The model places every way of deciding into one of three schemes. Andrew tested all three on his croissants.

Scheme How Andrew decides What happens
By plan "A hundred, every day," whatever the weather or the day Tuesday: seventy sell, thirty are thrown out. Saturday: demand hits a hundred and thirty, thirty guests leave with nothing
By deviation "Forty were left yesterday, so I order sixty today" Always a day late. Wednesday he is twenty short. Saturday he orders a hundred and is thirty short
By forecast He logs each day's sales next to the weekday and the weather Tuesday near 70, Saturday near 130, and rain takes off about 15%

The third scheme sets the order from the forecast. A rainy Saturday gets 130 × 0.85, so about 110 croissants. By midday he adjusts if sales are off.

Waste fell. So did shortages. And he got the habit of looking ahead, which he then used on his cash reserve, his milk orders and the shift schedule.

Picture three drivers on one road. The first has locked the accelerator. The second brakes when the brake lights ahead come on. The third looks three cars ahead and lifts off the pedal before anyone brakes. Same road, three results. Why the second driver is always late is in the control cycle.

Faster is not better: the two-week test

Here is the trap. Once you start reacting to numbers, it is tempting to react to every number, fast.

Take two weeks of croissant sales, day by day: 100, 65, 98, 94, 114, 123, 108, then 91, 76, 82, 105, 107, 137, 100. Test four ordering rules on the second week. Add up how many croissants each rule missed.

Rule Croissants missed, week two
A hundred every day 100
As many as sold yesterday 130
Shift yesterday's order by about a third of yesterday's error 119
As many as sold on the same day last week 76

The fastest rule, "as yesterday", did worse than the fixed plan. It chased random jumps. The winner knew the pattern: the day of the week.

So the order of action is fixed. Find the pattern first and put it in the forecast. Then decide which deviation deserves a response. Only then speed up the response.

Decision log template: write the forecast before you act

A decision log is a record of the decisions you made, why you made them and what happened. A plain log stops at the first two. That turns it into a diary.

To steer, a decision log has to hold the forecast. Then you can compare it with the facts. Here is the template, eight fields, one line per decision.

  1. Decision and date. What you chose, in one sentence.
  2. Scheme. By plan, by deviation or by forecast. Be honest.
  3. Forecast as a range. The number you expect, with its spread and its condition.
  4. Assumptions. What must hold true for the forecast to work. Write them down, because an elegant calculation can rest on wrong assumptions.
  5. What you will ignore. The size of deviation that gets no response. Inside it, the levers stay put.
  6. Check point. When you look again, and what should have changed by then.
  7. Actual. Filled in at the check, never before.
  8. Correction. How the forecast changes after the gap.

The fifth field is the easiest to skip. Without it, the log invites you to chase every wobble, and the two-week test shows what that costs.

Decision log example: two entries

Here is Andrew's entry for a rainy Saturday:

Field Andrew's entry
Decision Order about 110 croissants for Saturday
Scheme By forecast
Forecast Saturday demand about 130; 15% fewer in rain, so about 110
Assumptions Rain all morning; no event nearby; the Saturday pattern holds
What I ignore Day-to-day noise in last week's numbers
Check point Midday: are sales on track for 110? Adjust if not
Actual Filled in at closing
Correction Update the rain effect if the gap repeats

And here is Elena, a freelance designer launching her first online book. Her goal is 21 sales a month. Every Monday she multiplies last week's visitors by her conversion rate.

Field Elena's entry
Decision Rewrite the page headline this week, or email subscribers
Scheme By forecast
Forecast 280 visitors × 1.5% = 4.2 sales a week, about 17 over four weeks
Assumptions Conversion holds at 1.5%; traffic holds at last week's level
What I ignore One slow day
Check point Next Monday: visitors and sales for the week
Actual Filled in next Monday
Correction Change the forecast if conversion moves

The forecast shows a shortfall of four sales before the month is over. She can act now. If she took stock only at the end of the month, she would learn about the shortfall when it was too late to fix.

That is the whole difference. One log records what happened. The other records what you expected, so the gap can teach you.

Bad business decisions and what they cost

A bad business decision does not look bad on the day. It looks like habit. "That's how it has always been done."

Run the numbers on the habit. By plan, Andrew bins thirty croissants on a Tuesday and turns away thirty guests on a Saturday. That is every week. In the test week, the fixed rule missed a hundred croissants. The "as yesterday" rule, which felt responsive, missed a hundred and thirty.

Now scale the same habit up to money. At his starting 9% margin, Andrew's cash reserve reaches two months of expenses at month 16 if nothing changes. With a forecast and a plan behind it, at month 10. Six months of steering by habit means six more months on less than two months of cash.

And the human cost, in one plain sentence: you keep calling it bad luck, and you keep looking for someone to blame.

Where the decision log stops

The log is a strong tool. It treats one symptom: the decision made blind and never checked. You now act on a written forecast and learn from the gap.

The deeper problem stays. The fifth field asks which deviation you will ignore. The log does not tell you where that line sits for each number, or what to do when two numbers break at once. And one log line covers one decision, not the whole loop it belongs to.

Two steps close that gap:

  • Zones and the red protocol. The exact thresholds for normal, warning and emergency on every number. And the order of rescue when two of them go red together. Chapter 4.
  • The full control cycle. Eight stages, from noticing pressure to holding the result. Only control by forecast carries all eight. Chapter 7.

Without them, each decision gets better. The system around the decisions still runs on habit.

Here is where the log sits in the whole system. Goals set what matters. The state and the error say where you stand. Levers and a plan give the options. The forecast, held in the log, decides which option to take now.

The likely objection: "My business is too small for a log." Andrew's first log was a croissant order. Elena's was one line every Monday. Before any forecast, the number underneath it has to be honest; that step is in gut feeling vs numbers.

Key takeaways

  • Decide by forecast: write the number, its range and its assumptions before you act.
  • Find the pattern first. Reacting faster to noise makes results worse, not better.
  • Every log line needs a "what I ignore" field and a check date.
  • Compare the forecast with the facts. The gap teaches more than the result.
  • Bad luck is often a pattern nobody wrote down.

Two choices for Monday

You have two choices. Keep ordering the same hundred, react to yesterday, and call the bin and the empty shelf bad luck. Or write tonight's decision as a forecast, with a range and a check time.

The log makes each decision visible. The zones and the full control cycle make the whole system steer.

Questions people ask

How do you make decisions under uncertainty?

Write a forecast before you act: the number you expect, its range and the assumptions behind it. Decide what size of deviation you will ignore, set a check date, then compare the forecast with the facts and correct it.

What is a decision log?

A record of the decisions you make, one line each. A useful one holds the forecast and the assumptions behind each decision, not only the outcome, so you can see where your thinking was wrong.

What should a decision log template include?

Eight fields work. The decision and date, the scheme you used, the forecast as a range and the assumptions. Then the deviation you will ignore, the check point, the actual result and the correction.
