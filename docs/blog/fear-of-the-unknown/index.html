Fear of the Unknown: Turn the Fog Into Two Lines With Dates

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

Fear of the unknown grows when the future has no numbers. Write two forecasts for one goal: where you land if nothing changes, and where you land if the plan works, each with a date. Price one bad surprise, write the result as a range with a condition, and check it against the facts every month. The fog becomes two lines you can steer between.

Nearly half of owners lie awake at night worrying about the business.

It's two in the morning. Your partner is asleep, back turned to you. You're running next spring in your head: the lease, the slow month, the bill that might land at the same time. Every version ends badly. None of them has a number.

You can list eleven ways next year goes wrong. You can't name one figure for March.

You can work. You've proved it for years. What breaks you is the fog, and you carry it alone, at night, with no system to put it in.

The answer to that fog comes from the Jirgenson Model: a control system that turns the future into lines with numbers and dates. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

Fear of the unknown is a forecast nobody wrote

Here is the rule the model starts from. You can control exactly as much as you can foresee.

So the fear has a precise cause. It isn't weakness. It's a missing forecast. The future stays a fog because nobody has drawn a line through it.

In the Jirgenson Model, predictability belongs to a pair: the object plus the person at the wheel. The same market is chaos to one person and rhythm to another. A forecast is not a prophecy. It is a hypothesis you write down, test against the facts and correct.

The model's rules are public. Read them yourself in the Jirgenson Model on GitHub.

Watch what the word "random" hides. Milk prices jump about for someone who looks at them once a month. For a supplier who knows the milking schedule, they move on a pattern. Before you call something a force of nature, ask one question: is it this unpredictable for everyone, or only for me?

Some uncertainty always stays. But a large slice of the fog is information you haven't collected yet. The same goes for whole futures, which scenario planning cuts into slices with sizes.

Fear of the future in numbers: the two-line forecast

This is the whole tool. It takes about ten minutes, one sheet and one goal. Each step below sits next to a real entry from the book's examples.

1. Pick the one goal the fog is hiding

Not "the business". One goal with a target and a deadline.

Andrew's coffee shop: a cash reserve of 2.0 months of expenses by month 12. Today it holds 0.5. That single row was the one that kept him awake.

2. Learn the mechanism before you call it random

Ask who sees this coming. Then measure a small sample.

Elena was publishing her first book with little experience. A perfect forecast of demand was impossible. A useful one was not. She sent a trial stream of visitors to the book's page and counted: every 100 visitors brought 1.5 buyers. At 1,400 visitors a month, that makes 21 sales, about €609 in revenue before fees and taxes. Chaos turned into a line she could check.

3. Write line one: "if I change nothing"

Take today's pace and carry it forward. Put a date at the end.

Andrew's margin was 9%. At that pace the shop adds about 0.1 months to the reserve each month. The goal arrives at month 16, four months past the deadline. By month 3 the reserve stands near 0.8: the red zone.

Alex, thirteen and saving for a bicycle, did the same sum. Pocket money alone gives him €120 by 1 September. He needs €300. That's €180 short.

4. Write line two: "if the plan works"

Same goal, same start, with your plan running.

Andrew's plan lifts the margin from 9 to 15%. The reserve grows by about 0.18 a month. The goal arrives at month 10, two months early. Alex adds the neighbours' jobs and sells his scooter: €300 by 1 September.

5. Price one bad surprise

Ask what a breakdown, a fine or a surprise bill costs in time.

For Andrew, a hit of 0.1 months of reserve pushes the goal back about a month at 9% margin. At 15%, about half a month. That's no reason to worry. It's a reason to keep the reserve growing.

6. Write it as a range with a condition

A single-number forecast misses nearly every time. Give yours a range and a condition.

Andrew's version: two months of reserve somewhere from month 10 to month 12, as long as the margin holds at 14% or more.

7. Set the date you compare it with the facts

Andrew compared forecast and fact once a month. Elena, short on experience, did it every week. The gap between the two taught them more than any guess. Pick the first leading indicator that moves before the result, and watch it in between.

Here is Andrew's sheet, filled in:

Field Andrew's entry
Goal Cash reserve 2.0 months by month 12
Now 0.5 months
If nothing changes About 0.1 a month; goal at month 16; red zone near month 3
If the plan works About 0.18 a month; goal at month 10
Price of one surprise 0.1 months costs about 1 month at 9%, half a month at 15%
Range and condition Goal between month 10 and 12, if margin stays at 14% or more
Check Forecast against fact, monthly

Two lines now stand where the fog was. One of them is bad. That's fine. A bad line you can see is a line you can move. The money side of the same sheet is laid out in forecast cash flow.

Say the bad line out loud, with one action

A forecast of a bad outcome can go two ways.

The loud way: "We're finished." No action follows. People hear it as a verdict, and the team starts to drift. The forecast then makes itself come true.

The responsible way: name the risk while there is still time, and attach an action to it the same day. Andrew said his first line out loud to Sara, his senior barista. In the same breath he chose the second line: a revised menu, a talk with the supplier. He also gave Sara the Friday shifts, because she could read Fridays better than he could. How to hand a skill like that from one head to a sheet is in predictability in business.

Do the same at home. Your partner already feels the fog. Show them both lines and the one action for this week. A fear shared as a number is lighter than a fear hidden as a mood.

Coping with anxiety at night: take the tired pilot off the wheel

In his first months Andrew put in sixty-five hours a week and made his calls late at night. Those hours held his worst mistakes: mixed-up figures, a supplier call put off, deadlines he got wrong.

A tired head reads the future like a broken gauge. At 2 a.m. every line points down, because the person drawing it can't see ahead. So move forecasting to a rested morning. Put your own hours on the panel as a goal, the way Andrew did. The night version of you is not the one who should steer. More on that in business owner burnout.

One plain line on health. The model treats chaos in the business, not an illness. If the fear stays for weeks, steals your sleep or comes with panic, that is more than a bad week. Talk to your doctor. In a crisis, in the US or Canada, call or text 988.

What six more months of fog cost

The numbers come first. Without a forecast, Andrew steers by mood, and mood doesn't change the pace. His reserve reaches two months at month 16 instead of month 10. That's six extra months one breakdown away from trouble.

His starting gap was 1.5 months of reserve. At expenses of €27,300 a month, that's €40,950 of cushion the shop doesn't have. Every one of those six months, a single surprise costs him a full month of progress instead of half.

And the person? Six more months of lying awake, solving the same unknown again each night, with nothing written down by morning.

Where two lines stop

Be honest about what you built. Two forecasts treat the symptom. You stop steering blind into the fog.

The cause sits one level deeper. Your line says the reserve will be 0.8 in month 3. Is that bad enough to act on today, or only to watch? Without set thresholds, you'll decide that by mood. And a forecast checked only when the calendar says so still reacts late.

Two steps close that gap:

  • Zone thresholds and the red protocol. Where green turns yellow and yellow turns red, set on a calm day, and what to do first when several numbers turn red at once. Chapter 4.
  • Control by forecast. How to act on where the line is heading, not on the calendar or on last month's result. Chapter 9.

Without them, the two lines show you the road. They don't yet tell you when to brake. A first step toward the thresholds is in KPI zones.

"The future can't be predicted." True, in full. A perfect forecast is impossible. A useful one is ten minutes away, and it beats a perfect fear. The two lines are the forecast step of one loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast.

Key takeaways

  • Fear of the unknown is often a missing forecast, not a weakness.
  • Write two lines for one goal: if nothing changes, and if the plan works, each with a date.
  • Price one bad surprise in time, and write the forecast as a range with a condition.
  • Say the bad line out loud, with one action attached the same day.
  • Fog is not the future. It's a forecast nobody has written down.

Two choices for tonight

You have two choices tonight. Lie awake again and run the future with no numbers, the same loop as last night. Or take ten minutes in the morning and write two lines for the one goal that scares you.

The lines show the road. The zone thresholds, the red protocol and control by forecast tell you when to act. They turn next Monday into a short check instead of a long dread.

Questions people ask

How do I stop fear of the unknown?

Give the unknown numbers. Pick one goal and write two forecasts with dates: if nothing changes, and if your plan works. Price one bad surprise. Then compare the forecast with the facts every month. The fear shrinks as the fog turns into lines you can check.

What should I do when I'm worried about the future?

Separate what you can learn from what stays uncertain. Ask who sees this coming, and measure a small sample before you call it random. Then write the forecast as a range with a condition. Tools for deciding inside the uncertainty are in [decisions under uncertainty](/blog/decisions-under-uncertainty/).

Is fear of the future the same as anxiety?

Not always. Worry about a real, unplanned risk is a signal to forecast and act. Fear that lasts for weeks, steals sleep or brings panic needs a doctor, not a spreadsheet. In a crisis, call or text 988 in the US or Canada.
