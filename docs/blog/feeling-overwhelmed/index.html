Feeling Overwhelmed? Pick the One Thing First, by Protocol

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

Feeling overwhelmed usually means everything feels red at once and nothing tells you what comes first. Use the red protocol: check for direct danger, freeze everything that doesn't help the red number, put your time, money and attention on that one number, and if two are red, save the one tied to your top goal first.

Four out of ten owners have hidden business stress from their own family.

It's your daughter's birthday. Candles, a cake, ten kids shouting. Your phone buzzes in your pocket for the third time in five minutes.

You smile for the photo. Behind the smile you're doing sums. The client who brings in most of your money hasn't paid. The bank sent a letter you haven't opened. A deadline lands tomorrow.

Your partner catches your eye across the table. "Are you here?"

You aren't. You're everywhere at once, and nowhere for long enough to fix anything.

Here is the turn. You can work. Nobody doubts that, least of all you. The trouble is that you carry every fire on your own back, with no order for which one burns first. So the order comes from chance: the loudest call, the latest email, whatever you remember at two in the morning.

That is what feeling overwhelmed is. Not too much work. Too many fires with no queue.

The Jirgenson Model gives the answer as a control system: a fixed order for the worst day, decided on a calm one. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

Why everything feels red at once

Look at an engine temperature gauge. Three sectors: green, yellow, red. You don't strip the engine to decide what to do. You look where the needle is.

Without zones, you run the week without a gauge. Every problem arrives with the same colour, the colour of panic. So every problem looks red. That's the absurd part: you can have five problems, only one of them dangerous, and treat all five as emergencies.

The queue of incoming problems is governed by chance. The queue of decisions should be governed by priority.

In the Jirgenson Model, the red protocol is the fixed order of action for a number that has crossed its permissible limit. Its one overriding goal is to bring that number back into the acceptable zone. Everything else waits.

This isn't ruthlessness. It's economy of effort. You have one body, one day and one bank account. The protocol spends them where the damage is closest. The business version, with a full crisis in euros, is in what to do first in a crisis.

How to stop feeling overwhelmed: the red protocol

Four steps. Learn them once, in a calm hour. Then they run on the worst day without asking your mood.

  1. Check for direct danger. People, safety, mandatory payments, the law. If any of these is at risk, protective action and escalation come first.
  2. Freeze secondary work. Anything that doesn't help bring the red number back has to wait. Frozen, not cancelled.
  3. Concentrate. Time, money and attention go to the red number and to the levers that act on it.
  4. If two numbers are red, order them. First the one that threatens the nearest damage you can't undo. Judge it by timing, scale, probability and how fast the lever acts. Then, other things equal, the one tied to the goal you would give up last.

Elena's two reds, filled in

Elena is a freelance designer. She has three goals, in this order. One: an average income of at least €3,000 a month by month 14. Two: no single client above 30% of income. Three: no more than 35 hours a week.

Today one client brings in 70% of her money. Her lines for that number: normal up to 33%, acceptable up to 50%, emergency above 50%. So that row is red.

Now suppose that in the same week, the tax authority freezes her account. Two reds. This is the moment that buries people: two disasters, both urgent, both screaming.

Here is how the protocol sorts it:

Step Elena's entry
1. Direct danger? The frozen account touches mandatory payments and the law. Protective action and help come first.
2. Freeze The plan to cut her biggest client's share waits. So do new projects.
3. Concentrate All attention goes to the account: the facts, the documents, the call.
4. Two reds Income is goal number one. The frozen account hits it directly. The account goes first; the client share comes second.

Elena doesn't drop the client problem. She puts it on hold, with a clear head, and returns to it once the account is open. One thing first. Then the next. How she later brings that client share down is in client concentration for freelancers.

When nothing is red

The protocol also tells you when to calm down. Alex, a boy saving €300 for a bicycle, should have €170 by 1 July. He has €140. The error is minus €30. His lines: up to €10 behind is normal, 11 to 40 is acceptable, more than 40 is an emergency.

Minus 30 is yellow. No panic. He knows the cause: the old scooter hasn't sold yet. He keeps a close eye on it.

Yellow means look for the cause while it's cheap. Green means leave it alone. Where to draw your own three lines is the subject of KPI zones. Only red earns the whole protocol. Much of what overwhelms you on a bad Tuesday is yellow wearing a red coat.

Signs of overwhelm in how you run things

Overwhelm shows up in your decisions first:

  • You start three fixes at once, so you never know which one worked.
  • You answer problems in the order they arrive.
  • You spend money from a red number on a yellow one.
  • You move your own limits in the middle of the storm.
  • You can't say, in one line, what you will defend to the end.

The third sign does the quietest damage. Money taken from a red number to calm a yellow one makes the red worse, and it feels like progress while it happens.

How to sort a whole night of worries into colours is shown in small business owner stress.

A plain line about health. The protocol treats the chaos in the business, not an illness. If the weight doesn't lift after a bad week, or you can't sleep or eat, a doctor or a crisis line is the right help.

How to handle stress when it all costs money

Put the overwhelm into euros.

Elena's income averages €1,300 a month against a goal of €3,000. That is €1,700 short every month. Six more months of fighting fires in the order they arrive, with no time for the plan, leaves her €10,200 below her own goal. And with 70% of her money from one client, one email can take most of her income away.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, shows the other side. His cash reserve stood at half a month of expenses against a goal of two. With monthly expenses of €27,300, that gap is €40,950. A broken coffee machine is a nuisance for a shop with two months of reserve. For a shop with two weeks, it's an emergency.

The human cost fits in one sentence: six more months of dinners where you smile and count in your head.

"But everything is urgent"

It feels that way. That's the point.

Urgency is a feeling. A zone is a line you drew on a calm day. The two don't agree, and the line is usually right. When Elena's two reds arrived, one of them still had to wait, and waiting was the right decision.

Ask three questions of any new problem before it gets your attention. A fuller filter for urgent tasks is in the Eisenhower matrix alternative. Which goal does it belong to? Which zone is that number in now? Is it more important than what I'm doing at this moment? Many interruptions fail the third question.

Next Monday, with the protocol in place, the phone buzzes during dinner and you know what it is: yellow, a cause to find on Tuesday. You put the phone face down.

Where the protocol stops

Be honest about what the four steps can and can't do. The protocol treats the symptom: on the worst day, you stop running in circles.

But it leans on two things it doesn't create. Step 4 asks which goal you would give up last. If you've never put your goals in order, a tie between two reds goes back to panic. And step 3 asks for "the levers that act on it". If you've never listed what you can change yourself, today, you'll push on the result and get nowhere.

Two steps of the system close that gap:

  • The refusal order. Your goals as a short list, ranked by what you would give up first. It breaks every tie between two reds. Chapter 1.
  • The list of levers. What you can move yourself, today, for each goal, and what is only weather. Chapter 5.

Without them, the protocol tells you to concentrate. It can't yet tell you on what, when two things matter.

The protocol is one part of a loop: goals in order, the real state, the gap, levers, a plan, a forecast. Each part takes a weight off your back and puts it on paper. The ranked list starts with the test in how to set priorities when everything is urgent. The lever list grows from business levers.

The model's canon is free and public on GitHub, so you can check every rule yourself.

Key takeaways

  • Feeling overwhelmed is too many fires with no queue, not too much work.
  • Danger first. Freeze the rest. Concentrate on one red number.
  • Two reds? The nearest damage you can't undo first, then your top goal.
  • Yellow is a cause to find. Green is left alone.
  • Decide the order on a calm day, so the bad day only follows it.

Two choices for this week

You have two choices this week. Keep carrying every fire on your back, answering whoever shouts loudest, and hope the next birthday is calmer. Or write the four steps on one card tonight and let them pick the one thing first.

The card gets you through the worst day. The refusal order and the lever list make sure it knows what to save and how.
