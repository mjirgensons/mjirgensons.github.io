Finding Direction: Walk Back From One Goal to This Week

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

Finding direction starts at the end, not with today's to-do list. Write one end state with a number, a unit and a date. If two goals pull apart, decide which you would give up first. Then walk backwards, asking what must be true right before each step, until you reach this week. The step closest to today is your direction.

It's Thursday, 10 p.m. You answered forty emails today. You worked sixty hours this week.

Your partner asks a simple question from the doorway: "Where is all this going?"

You open your mouth. Nothing comes out.

Four out of ten owners have thought about giving it all up.

Here is the irony. You answer forty questions a day from clients, staff and suppliers. You can't answer the one that matters at home. You are not lazy. You are busy with no heading.

You can work. That was never the problem. You carry every goal, promise and "someday" in your head, with no system to order them. So each day goes to whoever shouts first, and at night the pile comes back.

The Jirgenson Model gives you the answer: pick the port, then walk back from it to this week. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

One plain line first. If the emptiness or low mood lasts more than a bad week, see a doctor or call a crisis line. That help comes first. This is a tool for the chaos in the work, not a treatment.

Finding direction starts with one port

Imagine a captain who loves two ports and wants to reach both. A ship can't sail two ways at once. So he dashes back and forth, burns fuel and arrives nowhere. You choose the port before you put to sea, not in the middle of a storm.

Busy is a speed. Direction is a port.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, started his list with one sentence: "For the business to grow and bring me joy". He read it again and saw there was no way to check it. How much growth is enough? At what figure does joy count? A week later he couldn't say if it had worked.

That sentence is what a lack of direction looks like on paper. It sounds like a goal. It has no end point.

In the Jirgenson Model, the backward move starts from a precise end state. At every step it asks one question: what must be true right before this? The answers form a chain of conditions. The step closest to today is what you do this week.

How to get direction: the heading sheet in five steps

You need one sheet and about twenty minutes. Here is the full tool, filled in with Elena, a freelance designer who wants a steadier income and is writing her first online book.

1. Write the end state with a number, a unit and a date

"A good income" has no number. "3,000" has no unit. "€3,000 a month" has no date. "A three-month average income of at least €3,000 a month by month 14" is a port.

Then ask "what for?" of every item. Andrew's draft held "launch loyalty cards" and "hire a second barista". Both were methods, not goals. Loyalty cards serve regular customers. The barista serves quality. A method written as a goal locks you onto one road before you have seen the map. More on this check in how to set measurable goals.

2. If two ports pull apart, decide which goes first

Elena had three goals: income, no client above 30% of it, and her own hours. They fight. So she asked which she would give up first if things got tight. Her hours went first; her income stayed to the end.

That order is a permission. While writing the book, from month 1 to month 3, she may work up to 55 hours a week. It is a decision with a time limit, not a slip. The ten-minute version of this question is the refusal test.

3. Walk backwards, one condition at a time

Start at the port. At each step ask: what must be true right before this? Elena's chain:

When What must be true
Month 14 Income of €3,000 a month
Month 11 Steady book sales of 21 a month
Month 9 1,400 page visits a month, 1.5% of them buying
Month 6 The first hundred newsletter subscribers
Month 4 The launch
Month 3 The manuscript is ready
Month 1 The outline is drawn up and the price is set

4. Stop at this week

The row closest to today is your direction for the next seven days. Elena's first action was the outline and the price, not "become an author". Two things she could finish by Friday.

That is the moment the fog lifts: a distant goal becomes an assignment for the week.

5. Check that the chain closes in numbers

Alex is thirteen and wants a €300 bicycle by 1 September. The scooter he will sell brings a one-off €60, so €240 must come from regular sources. On 1 June he has €90. That leaves €150 over three months: €50 a month.

His levers bring €20 of pocket money and €30 of paid jobs: €50 a month. The chain closes exactly, with nothing to spare. So any dip calls for a new lever. He knows that in June, not on 31 August.

Lack of direction or lack of focus? Forward versus backward

Plan forward from today, and the options multiply like an avalanche. Every client, course and side project gets a "maybe". That is a lack of focus from the inside.

Plan backward from the port, and you ask the same question at every step. A chain appears, and it shows you what to do now.

Side by side (an illustration):

Forward from today Backward from the port
The question "What could I do next?" "What must be true right before this?"
What you get A growing list of maybes One chain of conditions
This week Whatever arrives first The row closest to today
A missed turn Guilt about the past A fresh route from where you stand

Your phone's navigation app works the same way. Miss a turn, and it doesn't scold you. It takes the spot where you stand and plots a fresh route. The technique has a mathematical relative, Richard Bellman's dynamic programming, but you need none of its maths.

Finding focus in business: two tests before you trust the chain

Direction in business needs two honesty checks. Skip them, and a flawless chain leads to the wrong place.

Is today written down precisely? If your starting point is wrong, the route is built from someone else's position. "We have about two months of cash" is a guess. "We have 0.5 months of expenses in reserve" is a start. Never mistake an open window on a high floor for an exit.

Does the port belong to a living system? Every goal sits inside something bigger: a street, a city, an industry. If that bigger system is fading, the best route inside it ends in a dead end. For Andrew's second location, a new university building near the shop worked with him, not against him.

If your list holds goals that cancel each other out, fix that first with mutually exclusive goals. If it holds fifteen goals, cut it with too many goals.

Priorities at work: what the nearest row does on Monday

A new request arrives on Monday. Ask: does it move the row closest to today? If yes, it goes in. If no, it waits, or it goes.

Andrew's five-year dream was ten coffee shops. He walked back from it. The chain ended at today with one plain line: hire a senior barista and start writing the operating procedures. If he didn't free his hands for that now, ten shops in five years had almost no chance. The mistake shows up today, not in year five.

What another year without a heading costs

Money first. In a rough estimate, Andrew's second location needs €45,000. At a 15% margin, his shop brings in €4,500 a month, €54,000 over twelve months. Take out €8,000 for a manager, and €46,000 is left. The chain made the number visible. Without it, €45,000 stays a wish.

Now drift. If nothing changes, Andrew's cash reserve reaches its goal at month 16. With a plan, at month 10.

And at home: another year of sixty-hour weeks heading nowhere you chose.

"My life is too messy for a chain"

The chain doesn't need a tidy life. It needs a clear end and an honest start. A thirteen-year-old built one for a bicycle. You can build one for a business, a book or a family budget in twenty minutes, on paper. For the whole life version, see how to prioritize your life.

Where the heading sheet stops

Be honest about what you built. The heading sheet treats a symptom: being busy with no port.

It does not keep you on course. The backward move guarantees neither that the goal is reachable nor that the route is the best one. Life will push you off the chain, and you won't always know whether a bad month is noise or a real drift. You also won't know when to hold the course and when to turn.

Two steps close that gap:

  • Zones for every row of the chain. Where normal ends and the emergency starts, set on a calm day. Chapter 4. A short version: KPI zones.
  • Steady mode and manoeuvre. The corridor along your route, and the launch conditions that say when a big turn may start. Chapter 10.

The chain is one step of the whole loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. It connects the goal to the plan.

Key takeaways

  • Busy is a speed. Direction is a port.
  • Write the end state with a number, a unit and a date, and strip out methods.
  • When goals collide, decide which goes first, before the storm.
  • Walk back one condition at a time until you reach this week.
  • Check the chain closes in numbers, and that today is written down precisely.

Two choices for Thursday night

You have two choices tonight. Answer another forty emails tomorrow and hope a direction shows up on its own. Or take one sheet, write the port, and walk back to Friday.

The chain gives you the heading. The zones and the corridor keep you on it when the month goes wrong.

Questions people ask

How do I find direction when I feel lost?

Write one end state with a number, a unit and a date. Then ask what must be true right before it, and repeat until you reach this week. That last answer is your direction. If the lost feeling lasts weeks and comes with low mood, talk to a doctor.

What causes a lack of direction?

Rarely laziness. More often it is a goal with no end point ("grow and enjoy it"), or two goals pulling apart with no order between them. Fix the end point, decide which goal goes first, and the daily choices get easier.

How do you get direction in business?

Start from a precise end state, such as a second location in 18 months with a set margin and reserve. Walk back to today in steps of months. The step closest to today tells you what to do this week. Check your starting numbers first, because a wrong start gives a wrong route.
