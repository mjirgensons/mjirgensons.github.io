Goal hierarchy: a prioritization method that survives a bad week

Martin Jirgenson30 Oct 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer

A goal hierarchy puts your goals in a strict order, decided in advance: which one you would give up first and which you would defend to the end. Above the goals sit constraints (law, safety, contracts) that are never traded; below them, a rule for spending what is left. When two goals collide, the higher one wins, without a meeting.

A week in a small coffee shop. On Monday the milk supplier raises prices. On Tuesday the senior barista asks for another person on Friday shifts. On Wednesday a salesman offers loyalty cards at a discount. On Thursday the bank balance looks thinner than it should.

Each request is reasonable. Each sounds urgent. And the owner, tired and short of time, will most likely say yes to whoever asks most persuasively. A month later he discovers he has run out of money before he ran out of time. Nobody decided that outcome. Fatigue did.

A ranked set of goals exists so that the decisions of that week were made earlier, on a calm evening, by the person who owns the business.

Goal hierarchy: four layers, not one list

Most prioritization advice gives you one list. The trouble is that a business has things of very different kinds pulling on it, and putting them in one list mixes rules with wishes. A working hierarchy has four layers:

Layer What it holds How it behaves
1. Constraints Law, safety, contracts, the load a person can bear Never traded. They define which actions are allowed at all
2. Minimum levels The floor below which the result is no longer viable Must be held before anything else is improved
3. Improvement goals Measurable goals in a strict order Ranked by what you would give up first and last
4. Allocation rule How to spend what remains By urgency, risk and cost

If saving the top goal would mean breaching a constraint, the constraint wins: find another way, or stop and change the conditions. Putting cash first does not override common sense; skimping on equipment maintenance brings in money today and raises the risk of a breakdown tomorrow.

In the Jirgenson Model, the goal vector is an ordered list of at most nine measurable goals. The order is set by the refusal test: if you had to give goals up one at a time, which would go first, and which would be the last one left? Of two plans, the better is the one that wins on the highest-ranked goal where they differ.

A goal hierarchy example

Here is the coffee shop owner's third layer after one evening's work, with the order written in:

Rank Goal Target Deadline
1 (defended last) Cash reserve at least 2.0 months of expenses M12
2 Margin 15% M6
3 Regular customers 40% of receipts M12
4 Quality no more than 2 complaints a week M3
5 (given up first) Owner's time no more than 40 hours a week M12

He stared at the fifth row for a long time. Putting his own time last surprised him: he had known he was tired for ages, but had never said out loud that he would give up rest for the business. Now it was on paper. And the reserve went to the top for a brutal reason: without it the shop closes, and then neither customers nor quality nor margin matter.

Writing it took fifteen minutes. The next morning his senior barista looked at it and said she would have put quality first, because customers see quality and do not see the reserve. Neither was wrong. A ranking like this is not a truth. It is a choice, and someone else's order fits you no better than someone else's shoes.

Why rank beats weights

Many prioritization methods ask you to score goals (reserve 40%, margin 30%, and so on) and add the scores up. It feels rigorous. In a crisis it fails, because a weighted sum lets a big gain on a small goal buy back a loss on the big one. Ten extra regular customers can "compensate" for a reserve that has slipped below safety.

A strict order does not allow that trade. You compare two options on goal one; only if they tie there do you look at goal two. It is less flexible and much harder to fool, which is exactly what you want at eleven at night when the numbers are bad.

A priority matrix alternative: Eisenhower side by side

The Eisenhower matrix is the best-known prioritization tool: sort every task by urgent or not, important or not, then do, schedule, delegate or drop. It is excellent for a to-do list. Here is what happens when you run the coffee shop's week through it, next to the ranked goals (an illustration built on the book's own scenario):

This week's request Eisenhower matrix Ranked goals
Supplier raises milk price Urgent and important: do now Touches goal 2 (margin). Margin is yellow: find the cause, renegotiate or adjust the menu
Extra barista on Fridays Urgent and important: do now Costs money from goal 1 (reserve), which is red. Not now; revisit when the reserve is back above one month
Discounted loyalty cards Important, not urgent: schedule Serves goal 3 (regulars, yellow) but spends money from goal 1 (red). Wait
Thin bank balance Urgent and important: do now Goal 1, red. This is the week's only real priority

Three of the four requests land in the same Eisenhower box, "urgent and important", which is where most small business weeks live. The matrix cannot rank inside that box. The hierarchy can, because it knows which goal each request touches, what colour that goal is in, and which goal wins. One item gets the week; the others wait without guilt.

That is the honest comparison. Eisenhower sorts tasks. A ranked list of goals sorts the goals the tasks serve, and then the tasks sort themselves.

Goal prioritization starts by removing methods

Before ranking anything, check that every item is a goal. The owner's first rough list had eight items, and three were methods: "launch loyalty cards", "hire a second barista", "buy a new coffee machine". Ask what for? of each and the real goal appears: regular customers, quality, quality. Eight became five. Ranking methods is how a list ends up defending a coffee machine over a cash reserve.

Then check for the five defects of a goal list: goals that cannot be met together; a necessary requirement left off (taxes, safety); goals that change every Monday; a secondary goal rescued at the cost of the main one; and duplicates under two names ("satisfied customers" and "few complaints") that quietly get double weight. The first defect, goals that cannot coexist at all, deserves its own test, worked through in competing priorities vs conflicting goals.

Where this does not reach

The ranking tells you what matters more; it does not tell you how much of one goal is worth how much of another once the minimums are met. It does not make your goals the right ones, and an order that never changes can go stale: review it every six months or year, and record the change deliberately, never by drift. And where there are partners, employees or customers, your order has to be reconciled with theirs; the hierarchy is where that conversation starts, not where it ends.

Key takeaways

  • A goal hierarchy has four layers: constraints, minimum levels, ranked goals, and a rule for what is left.
  • Rank goals with the refusal test: what would you give up first, and what last?
  • A strict order beats weights in a crisis, because it cannot be bought off by gains on small goals.
  • Eisenhower sorts tasks; ranked goals sort the goals the tasks serve, including inside "urgent and important".

The fifteen minutes that end the Monday negotiation

What I find most freeing about this method is that it moves the hard decision to the one moment when you are able to make it well. On the bad Wednesday you do not have to choose between the loyalty cards and the reserve. You already did, with a clear head, weeks ago.

Questions people ask

What is a goal hierarchy?

A strict ranking of your goals, decided in advance, that says which goal wins when two collide. In the Jirgenson Model it sits under constraints that are never traded and above a rule for spending whatever resources remain.

What is the best prioritization method for a small business?

Rank goals with the refusal test (what you would give up first and last), give each goal a colour zone, and let a new request in only after asking which goal it serves, what colour that goal is, and whether it outranks what you are doing now.

What is a good alternative to the Eisenhower matrix?

For tasks, the matrix works well. For decisions between goals, a strict ranking works better, because it can rank options that all look urgent and important by asking which goal each one serves.
