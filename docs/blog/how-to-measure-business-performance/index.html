How to Measure Business Performance When Your Numbers Lie

Martin Jirgenson31 Oct 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer

How to measure business performance: put a current number next to each goal, then audit that number: who counted it, how, over what period, and what it might miss. Last, compare its error with the room you steer in. If the error is more than a third of that room, fix the measurement before you trust the figure.

Andrew had a hiring plan, an advertising campaign and a margin of twenty per cent. Only the first two were real.

That week he meant to take on a second barista and pay for ads. Every sum behind both decisions rested on the twenty. When he finally checked, his margin was 9%.

Now picture your own desk. You can quote last month's revenue to the euro. You still cannot say whether your margin is nine or twenty.

Nobody lied to Andrew. The number did, quietly, the way an unchecked number always can.

Why business numbers lie

A figure fails you in three ways. Each has a name, and each looks innocent.

  1. Memory. People recall the loud days and forget the quiet ones. "Good on the whole" drifts upward.
  2. Rounding and good intentions. "About twenty" feels better to say than "nine and a half". So that is the figure people give.
  3. What goes unrecorded. A cost nobody writes down does not exist for the report. It still exists for the money.

Andrew's margin fell through all three holes at once. He remembered the busy mornings. He rounded up. And he never counted waste: sour milk, pastries thrown out at the end of a shift. None of it reached a report, so none of it reached his margin.

A number without a source is a mood with a decimal point.

In the Jirgenson Model, the state vector is the list of current values that mirrors your goals row by row. Each value has one definition, one primary source, one person responsible, and a known error.

How to measure business performance: the five-question audit

Measuring performance does not mean collecting more figures. It means testing the few you already steer by. Take each goal with its current value and put five questions to it.

Here is the audit on Andrew's margin row, before and after.

Question Before the audit After the audit
1. Who counted it? Nobody; Andrew's impression The accounting system, fed by the till export
2. How, exactly? "Roughly what is left over" Operating profit as a % of revenue, waste included
3. Over what period? Unclear The full month
4. Which leak could hide in it? Memory, rounding, unrecorded waste Write-offs now count as a cost
5. Is its error small enough? No: off by 11 points Yes: about 1 point of scatter

Questions one to three share a simple test. If nobody can answer them, you hold an impression, not a number. The fewer people and steps between the event and the record, the better. A receipt in the till beats anyone's memory of the receipts.

Question four points at the three leaks above. Ask which one this figure is most exposed to.

Any business health check template you use needs a column for this audit. Without it, the table looks precise and steers you wrong.

Question five is the one people skip. It needs its own section.

Is the error small enough to steer by?

No measurement is perfect. The till misses a receipt. The accountant rounds. A customer who walks out in silence leaves no trace. You cannot remove this error, only shrink it. So stop asking whether it is precise. Ask whether its error is acceptable for this goal.

The rule of thumb is short. A number's error should be no more than a third of the distance from target to the trouble line.

Andrew's three rows show how it works.

  • Margin. Target 15%, trouble line 5%. That is 10 points of room. While waste went uncounted, his figure was off by 11 points. The instrument was worse than useless. Once write-offs went into the cost, the monthly scatter fell to about 1 point. Fit to steer by.
  • Cash reserve. He knew the balance of €13,650 to within about €500, because payment dates shift. That is about 0.02 months against a full month of room. Fit.
  • Complaints. Target 2 a week, trouble line 8, so 6 of room. If Sara logs only half the unhappy customers, the true count sits anywhere from 5 to 10. That error, about 2.5, is over a third of the room. Andrew added a second channel: a mark at the till.

When the error is too big, fix the measurement. Never widen the tolerance to suit a bad instrument. The precision of your records must not decide how much damage you accept.

The usual habit against a measured state

Here is the same coffee shop described two ways.

Goal The habit After the audit
Margin "Around twenty" 9%
Regular customers "Lots" 25% of receipts
Complaints "Not many" 5 a week, with a written definition

The left column feels like knowledge. Not one entry in it can be checked a month later. The right column can. That makes it the start of any business health check worth the name.

A freelancer runs the same test. Elena did not trust the glow of a good month after a big order. She pulled six months of bank statements. Her income swung between €800 and €2,200, an average of €1,300. Her best client brought in €910 a month on average: 70% of her income. One good month does not make an average.

What six more months of guessing cost

Andrew's phantom twenty per cent was about to pay for a hire and an ad campaign. Within a few months the shop would have run out of money. Nobody would have understood why.

The real numbers are blunt. A margin six points short of target means €1,800 a month less profit: €2,700 instead of €4,500. At 9%, his reserve reaches two months of expenses only at month 16. With the margin plan working, at month 10. That is six extra months without a safe cushion.

A thin margin also makes every accident dearer. At 9%, one breakdown costing a tenth of a month of expenses pushes the goal back a full month. At 15%, about half a month.

Behind those figures sits an owner working 65 hours a week, deciding late at night, with numbers that flatter him.

Where the audit stops

The audit delivers the second step of the loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. You now hold true readings. That treats the symptom: you stop steering by moods.

The cause of bad decisions is still in place. A clean 9% does not tell you whether it is a problem or a crisis. It does not tell you what to pull either.

Two steps close that gap.

  • Error and zones. Where the green, yellow and red lines sit for each row. What to do when several rows turn red at once. Chapter 4.
  • Levers. Which parameters you can change yourself, and which only look like levers. Chapter 5.

Without them, you own an honest panel and no rule for acting on it. If you want the one-evening table that comes before this audit, with a source for every row, start with the financial health check.

Too much maths for a small shop? No. Every chapter runs at three levels: basic, practical and advanced. The basic level here is three questions: who, how, over what period. The third-of-the-room rule is the practical step. The formulas stay optional.

Key takeaways

  • Measure performance by auditing the numbers you already steer by, not by adding more.
  • Ask every figure: who counted it, how, over what period, and which leak hides in it.
  • Keep each error under a third of the room between target and trouble.
  • Too big an error? Fix the instrument; never widen the tolerance.
  • An honest reading tells you where you are. Zones and levers tell you what to do.

Two choices tonight

You have two choices tonight. Keep steering by "around twenty" and hope the bank agrees. Or put your key business metrics through the audit and see which ones survive.

The audit gives you true readings. The zone lines, the red protocol and the list of levers turn those readings into action. Next Monday you could open the meeting with numbers nobody argues about.

Questions people ask

How do you measure business performance in a small business?

Write a current number next to each goal. Then audit it: who counted it, how, over what period, and what it might miss. Keep its error under a third of the distance from target to trouble.

What are the key business metrics to track?

The ones tied to your goals. For a coffee shop: cash reserve in months of expenses, margin with waste included, share of regular customers, complaints a week, and the owner's hours. Small business KPIs work only with one definition and one source each.

How accurate do my business numbers need to be?

Accurate enough to tell normal from trouble. If you manage margin to one point and your records are off by three, you are managing noise. A rough count will do when you only need to know whether more or fewer than half your customers return.
