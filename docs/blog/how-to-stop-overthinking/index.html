How to Stop Overthinking at Night: Give Every Worry a Slot

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

How to stop overthinking: stop answering every thought the moment it arrives. Write each worry on one sheet next to the goal it belongs to. Mark its zone: green waits, yellow gets a cause to find, red gets action. Then settle the sheet in one fixed slot, such as a short Monday review, on a rested morning instead of at night.

Nearly half of owners lie awake at night worrying about the business.

It's three in the morning. Your partner's back is turned to you, breathing slow. You're doing the same sum for the fourth time. Payroll on Friday. The supplier's new price list. The regular who hasn't been in for two weeks.

The phone glows on the nightstand. You check the bank app again. The number hasn't moved since midnight.

By breakfast you've decided nothing. Forty laps of the same loop.

Here is the turn. You can work. You've proved that a thousand times. What breaks you is carrying every open question in your head. No place to put it. No rule for when to answer it. Your mind becomes the warehouse, the alarm and the judge, all at once, all night.

That is not a weakness of character. It is a control scheme, and it is the worst one on offer.

The Jirgenson Model gives the answer as a control system, not a pep talk. Thoughts get a place, a zone and a time. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

Why the night loop never ends

Take Andrew, who runs a coffee shop on a corner. He orders croissants from a local bakery. For two weeks he tests four ordering rules against real sales.

Ordering rule Total miss in week two
Take a hundred every day 100 croissants
Order as many as sold yesterday 130 croissants
Smooth: shift yesterday's order by a third of yesterday's error 119 croissants
Order as many as sold on the same day last week 76 croissants

Look at the second row. The fastest rule, "as yesterday", did worse than a fixed plan that never looked at reality. It chased every random jump.

That is your night. Every thought that arrives gets an instant answer. Then the next thought arrives and overturns it. The loop isn't thinking. It's correcting noise.

In the Jirgenson Model, control by deviation means you measure the gap and correct it. It always reacts after the event. Speed it up without a rule for which deviations deserve an answer, and it starts correcting noise.

The winning rule knew the pattern: the day of the week. So the order of action is fixed. First find the pattern. Then decide which deviation deserves a response. Only then respond.

How to stop overthinking at night in three steps

This is the whole tool. Fifteen minutes the first time, two on any later night. You need one sheet and a pen by the bed.

1. Park every thought on one sheet

Write each worry in one line. Next to it, write the goal it belongs to. A thought that belongs to no goal is noise. Cross it out.

This is where the pattern appears. Five worries often turn out to be two goals in different clothes.

2. Give each thought a zone

Ask three questions of every line. Which goal does it belong to? Which zone is that number in now? Is it more important than what I'm doing at this moment? At three in the morning, what you're doing is sleeping.

  • Green: the number is within tolerance. Leave it alone. Fixing what works is cost without a goal.
  • Yellow: worse than normal, no damage yet. Write "find the cause" and a day.
  • Red: past the permissible limit. This one gets action, and first.

3. Give the sheet a fixed slot

Pick one time a week when you're rested. Andrew and Sara use Monday. Their review comes down to three questions. Which numbers changed this week? Which zone are they in now? What needs to be done about it?

If a thought comes back at night, it already has a place. It's on the sheet. It has a slot. You can let it wait.

Andrew's sheet, filled in

Here is the sheet with Andrew's numbers from the day he started. The night thoughts are an illustration; the numbers and zones are his.

Night thought Goal Now vs goal Zone What it gets
"One breakdown and we're done" Cash reserve 0.5 months vs 2.0 Red Action first, Monday morning
"We work hard and keep nothing" Margin 9% vs 15% Yellow Find the cause: waste, weak menu items
"The regulars are drifting off" Regular customers 25% vs 40% Yellow "Find out" on the task list
"That review on Saturday" Complaints per week 5 vs 2 or fewer Yellow Check the cause at the Monday review
"I'm never home" Owner's hours 65 vs 40 Yellow Stays on the list

Five loops. One red. Four that can wait until Monday without guilt.

Andrew's first idea was loyalty cards. The sheet said no: cards cost money, and the reserve was red. He put them aside without guilt. His clear-headed order decided, not fatigue.

The same habit helps in a bad month. When the supplier raised prices, the coffee machine broke and a barista quit, Andrew decided nothing that evening. He booked a meeting with Sara for the morning and went to bed. That night is described step by step in business triage.

Signs of overthinking in a business

The signs show up in your decisions before they show up in your face. Watch for these:

  • You decide the same question again every night, and the answer changes with your mood.
  • You pull two levers at once, so you never learn which one worked.
  • You move your own red line in the middle of a crisis.
  • Every new problem jumps the queue because it arrived last.
  • You check the bank app more often than you check the margin.

The fourth sign is a queue run by chance instead of priority. Why the most tired person in the building makes the worst calls is the subject of decision fatigue.

Fatigue corrupts a forecast like a faulty instrument. Andrew learned it the hard way. In his first months he worked sixty-five hours a week and decided late at night. Those were the hours of his worst mistakes: mixed-up figures, delayed calls to the supplier, wrong deadlines.

One honest line about health. The sheet treats the chaos in the business, not an illness. If you can't sleep for weeks, or the dread stays when the numbers are fine, see a doctor. In a crisis, call a crisis line.

The rules behind the sheet aren't a secret. The model's canon and its operating prompt are free and public on GitHub, so you can check every rule yourself. Short videos on the model are on the Steer, Don't Guess YouTube channel.

What the loop costs in money

Andrew's margin stood at 9% against a goal of 15%. On revenue of €30,000 a month, that is a profit of €2,700 instead of €4,500. The gap is €1,800 every month. Six more months of deciding by night mood is €10,800 that never reaches the reserve.

The reserve tells the same story. If nothing changes, Andrew reaches two months of reserve at month 16. With the plan working, at month 10. Six extra months one breakdown away from trouble. At a 9% margin, one breakdown costs him about a month of progress. How to count your own reserve in months is in cash reserve in months of expenses.

And the human cost, in one sentence: six more months of a partner who sleeps next to someone who is still at work.

"I can't switch my brain off"

You don't have to. That's the point of the slot.

The loop runs because each thought has nowhere to go. Telling yourself to stop thinking is like ordering money to appear in the account. It doesn't obey. A place and a time do. You aren't fighting the thought. You're filing it.

Next Monday, you sit down rested, read the colours and pick one red line. The rest of the week, the sheet carries the alarm so your head doesn't have to.

Where the sheet stops

Be honest about what you built tonight. The sheet treats the symptom: thoughts stop circling because they have a place.

The cause of the loop is still there. You don't yet know the exact number at which yellow becomes red, so some nights a yellow line will still feel red. And the "what if" at three in the morning is about the future, which the sheet doesn't show.

Two steps of the system close that gap:

  • Zone thresholds, set in a calm moment. Three lines for every number, decided before the bad night. Chapter 4.
  • Two forecasts with dates. "If nothing changes" against "if the plan works", so "what if" becomes a month on a line. Chapter 8.

Without them, the sheet tells you where to put a thought. It doesn't tell you how serious it is, or where it leads. A first taste of looking ahead is in forecast cash flow.

The sheet is one piece of a loop: goals, the real state, the gap, levers, a plan, a forecast. Each piece moves a weight out of your head and onto paper. Owners who carry the weight in their heads end up where small business owner stress begins. How the three zone lines work is shown in KPI zones. The Monday slot itself grows into a fixed rhythm in meeting cadence.

Key takeaways

  • The night loop is control by deviation without a rule: it corrects noise.
  • Park every thought on one sheet, next to the goal it belongs to.
  • Green waits. Yellow gets a cause to find. Red gets action, first.
  • Settle the sheet in one fixed slot, on a rested morning, never at three in the morning.
  • A thought with a place and a time can wait.

Two choices for tonight

You have two choices tonight. Lie down with the whole business in your head and run the loop until the alarm rings. Or write five lines on a sheet, give each a colour and a slot, and let the sheet keep watch.

The sheet stops the loop. The zone thresholds and the two forecasts make sure the next bad night already has its answer, decided on a calm day.

Questions people ask

How do I stop overthinking at night?

Keep a sheet by the bed. Write each worry in one line next to the goal it belongs to, and mark its zone: green, yellow or red. Then give the sheet a fixed slot on a rested morning. A thought with a place and a time can wait until then.

What are the signs of overthinking in a business owner?

You decide the same question every night and the answer changes with your mood. New problems jump the queue because they arrived last. You pull two levers at once, or move your own red line in a crisis. These are decision signs, not a diagnosis.

How do you overcome overthinking for good?

Decide the hard parts once, in a calm moment. The model sets zone thresholds, the order of your goals and two forecasts in advance (Chapters 1, 4 and 8). Then a bad night meets a decision that already exists, and the loop has nothing left to argue about.
