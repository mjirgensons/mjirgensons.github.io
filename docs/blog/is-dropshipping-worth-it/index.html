Is Dropshipping Worth It? Find Your Real Margin First

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

Is dropshipping worth it? Only if your real margin, after every fee, refund and ad, stays above a line you wrote down in advance. Take the number from the bank payouts, not the store dashboard. Count the costs that never show up as a payment. Check that the error in the number is small enough to steer by. Then write the stop condition on a calm day.

When a young business hits trouble, two out of three owners pour in their own money.

It's 2 a.m. Your partner is asleep. You're at the kitchen table with your phone, moving money from the family savings to the ad account. Again.

The dashboard says the week was good. The bank says something else. You can't explain the gap to yourself, let alone to the person asleep in the next room.

Here is the irony. The store counts every click to the cent. The cost of each sale gets counted by feel, or not at all.

You can work. What breaks you is carrying every number in your head, with no system to hold them. The dream store turns into a second job that eats the first job's pay.

So, is dropshipping worth it? The answer comes from the Jirgenson Model: one real margin from one source, checked against a line you set in advance. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

Is dropshipping worth it? The dashboard can't tell you

Revenue says what moved through the store, not what stayed. In dropshipping a supplier ships the product, and you sell it and pay for the traffic. So the costs sit in six places at once: the product, shipping, ads, apps, payment fees and refunds.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, was sure his margin held at around twenty per cent. When he measured it, it was 9%. He had never counted waste. Sour milk. Pastries written off at the end of the shift. The costs were real, and they never showed up in any report, because nobody wrote them down.

Your refunds, chargebacks and free replacements are his pastries. The formula is easy: how to calculate profit margin works it in euros. The hard part is the costs you never see.

In the Jirgenson Model, the state is the measured value of each goal, row by row, from one named source and one written definition. It turns "a good week" into a number. An instrument shows; it does not scold.

The real margin sheet, filled in

This is the tool. One sheet, one evening, six steps, each with the coffee shop's entry beside it. The method fits anything that sells.

1. Write the definition next to the number

A number without a definition changes with whoever counts it. So write down what goes in.

Andrew's entry: margin means operating profit as a percentage of revenue. Your entry, as an illustration: the money paid out to the bank, minus product, shipping, ads, apps, fees and refunds, as a share of sales.

Andrew and Sara did the same with complaints, so that a month later they would count the same way.

2. Give the number one source

Every parameter needs one primary source. Other sources may only cross-check it. If the store counts revenue one way and the bank another, the argument over which figure is right eats more energy than the business.

Andrew's source: the accounting system that the till exports into. Your source: the bank payouts and the card statement. Not the dashboard.

3. Count the costs that never become a payment

Whatever goes unrecorded doesn't exist for the report. It still exists for the money. Shorten the path between the event and the record: a receipt in the till beats anyone's memory of the receipts.

Andrew's missing row was waste. Once he counted it, twenty became 9. Yours may be refunds that land three weeks after the sale, or the replacement you sent for free. A simple log, like the one in how to track write-offs, catches them on the day they happen.

4. Test whether the number is fit to steer by

Ask one question: is the error in my number smaller than my distance to trouble? The rule of thumb: the error should be no more than a third of the distance from the goal to the red line.

Andrew's margin goal was 15%, and his red line was 5%. That is a distance of 10 points. While waste went uncounted, his number was off by 11 points. The error was bigger than the whole distance. He was steering by noise.

Once he counted the waste, the month-to-month scatter fell to about 1 point. A tenth of the distance. Now the number was fit to steer by.

Your test: if refunds swing your weekly margin by more than the gap you're trying to read, fix the count before you judge the store.

5. Put the goal and the state side by side

Write what you want next to what you have. Row by row, as numbers. Here is Andrew's sheet at month zero.

Goal Target Now
Cash reserve 2.0 months 0.5 months
Margin 15% 9%
Regular customers 40% 25%
Complaints per week 2 or fewer 5
Owner's hours per week 40 or fewer 65

The figure that hit him hardest was the 9. His first impulse was to close the spreadsheet and go and make coffee. He didn't. It was an instrument reading, not a verdict. The same gap shows up in any busy but not profitable business: a full room and an empty till.

6. Write the stop condition today

Before the next ad top-up, write down when you stop, revise or carry on. Tie it to a measured number and a period, never to a feeling. The rule: write the conditions for stopping in advance, while your head is clear.

Andrew used the same rule as a start condition. His second location would launch only once the reserve held at two months for three months in a row. He invented no date. The numbers would set it.

Your line, as an illustration: three months in a row below my minimum, from the bank, and I stop the ads.

That is the whole tool. Andrew's first sheet took him two hours.

Low profit margin: what six more months cost

Look at Andrew's money first. At 15% the shop brings in €4,500 a month. At his real 9%, it brings in €2,700. That is €1,800 short, every month. Six more months of guessing cost €10,800 against the goal.

The reserve pays too. At 9%, it reaches two months of expenses at M16. With the plan working, at M10. That is six extra months one breakdown away from trouble, and the months of runway post shows why that number matters more than revenue.

Andrew nearly paid in full. That week he had planned a second barista and an ad campaign, all resting on a margin that didn't exist. Within months the shop would have been running out of money, and no one would have known why.

The human cost fits in one sentence. Six more months of 2 a.m. transfers you can't explain to the person you're doing it all for.

If the worry has followed you into your sleep for weeks, not days, talk to a doctor or a crisis line. The sheet treats the chaos in the business, not an illness. The post on small business owner stress covers the business side of that weight.

Shopify no sales, or no margin? Two different alarms

"No sales" is loud. "Sales without margin" is quiet, and it costs more. A store with no orders tells you in a week. A store with orders and a thin margin hides it for months, because the dashboard keeps showing growth.

A store with orders and no margin is a hobby that sends you invoices.

An illustration: one store, two Sundays.

The guessing Sunday: "Revenue's up. The ads are working. Next month will turn." No source, no definition, no line.

The measured Sunday: "Real margin from the bank, below my minimum for the second month. One more, and the line I wrote in May applies." One look, and you know what Monday holds.

If orders have stalled, the problem is a lever, not a margin. The Shopify statistics post lists the levers you can pull today.

This is one piece of a loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. The sheet is the state. Without it, every later step steers by feel. The model's canon is public and free on GitHub.

"I don't have time for a spreadsheet." Paper works. A rough table today beats a perfect system in six months.

When to walk away: what the sheet does not fix

The sheet tells you where you stand. It treats a symptom: you stop guessing your margin.

The cause of the 2 a.m. transfers stays in place. You still don't know the point where a thin margin stops being a warning and becomes an emergency. And you can't tell if the niche itself is fading, so that a perfect store sits inside a sinking market.

Two steps close that gap:

  • Error and the three zones. Where your margin turns yellow and red, set on a calm evening, and the red protocol for the day two rows go red at once. Chapter 4.
  • The backward move. A chain of conditions from the goal back to this week. And a check that the goal belongs to a living market. Chapter 15.

Without them, the sheet tells you the truth. It doesn't tell you when to act. The traffic-light zones post shows the gauge. Is dropshipping not worth it for you, or worth one more quarter? Those two steps decide it with a clear head. For the hardest version of the question, see when to give up on a business idea.

Key takeaways

  • The dashboard shows revenue. The bank shows what stayed.
  • Write a definition beside every number, and give it one source.
  • Count the costs that never become a payment: refunds, chargebacks, replacements.
  • If the error in your number is bigger than a third of the distance to red, fix the count first.
  • Write the stop line on a calm day, before the next top-up.

Before the next ad top-up

You have two choices tonight. Keep moving family money into the ad account and hope next month turns. Or take one evening, find the real margin, and write the line.

The sheet shows the truth. The zones in Chapter 4 and the backward move in Chapter 15 tell you when to act and what to do this week.

Questions people ask

Is dropshipping worth it?

Only your own numbers can say. Take the real margin from the bank, after product, shipping, ads, apps, fees and refunds. Compare it with a minimum you wrote down in advance. If it stays below that line, the store costs more than it pays.

Why does my Shopify store have no sales?

No sales means a lever problem: traffic, the product page, the offer or the price. List what you can change, and test one lever at a time. Chapter 5 of the book covers the full lever list.

When should you quit dropshipping?

When the stop condition you wrote on a calm day is met. Not at 2 a.m., and not after one bad week. Write it as a number and a period, such as three months below your minimum, and check it on a fixed day.
