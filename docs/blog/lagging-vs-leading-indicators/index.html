Lagging vs leading indicators: see the result before it lands

Martin Jirgenson30 Oct 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer

The main difference is timing: lagging indicators, such as monthly revenue or profit, tell you what has already happened. Leading indicators, such as enquiries, bookings or margin per receipt this week, point to what is likely to happen next, while you can still act. Give every financial goal at least one leading indicator, check it weekly, and test that it really predicts the result.

Picture a house with one safety device: a fire alarm. It works perfectly. It rings loudly, every time, the moment the living room is in flames.

That is how most small businesses are run. The monthly profit figure arrives, accurate to the cent, three weeks after the month that produced it. By the time it says something is wrong, the something has already happened, been paid for and been forgotten. Running a business on numbers like that alone is living by the fire alarm: it goes off when the house is already on fire.

The fix is not a better alarm. It is a smoke detector, and a window you can see the street from.

Lagging vs leading indicators: the difference in one sentence

Lagging indicators tell you what has already happened. Leading indicators point to what is likely to happen, early enough that you can still change it.

Revenue for the month, profit for the quarter, the year's margin: all lagging. They are the final score, and you need them, because they are what the goals are made of. Enquiries this week, bookings for next month, sign-ups for tomorrow: those are leading. They are the play while it is still being played.

In the Jirgenson Model, every financial goal needs at least one leading indicator: a figure that carries information about the likely result over a stated horizon. The link between the leading indicator and the result is a hypothesis, so it must be tested, not assumed.

That last sentence is what separates this approach from most KPI advice. A leading indicator is not a magic number. It is a bet that one figure moves before another, and like any bet it should be checked against what actually happens.

Leading indicators examples: the same goal, both kinds of number

Here is the clearest way to see the difference: take real goals and put the lagging and leading numbers side by side.

Goal Lagging indicator (the score) Leading indicator (the play) How often
Coffee shop margin 15% by month six Margin for the month Average margin per receipt (receipt revenue minus its direct costs) Every week
A 5% price rise that must not lose customers Profit for the month Number of receipts in the first two weeks after the change During those two weeks
Freelancer income €3,000 a month Three-month average income Visitors to the book page, share who buy, newsletter subscribers Every week
€300 bicycle by 1 September Amount in the piggy bank Odd jobs lined up for the coming week Every Sunday

Read across any row and you see the pattern. The lagging number on the left answers "did it work?" The leading number in the middle answers "is it working?", and it answers weeks earlier.

Take Andrew's coffee shop. His goal was a 15% margin by month six, starting from 9%. If he had watched only the monthly margin, he would have had six data points before the deadline and no idea, between them, whether his plan was doing anything. Instead he watched margin per receipt every week. At the first formal check, in month two, the margin had reached 10.5%, above his revision threshold of 10%. Not proof, and he knew it: the season, the sales mix and the supplier's prices could all have played a part. But it was a signal early enough to matter.

Why lagging indicators alone are not enough

There is a scale of information value worth keeping in your head. From most to least useful for a decision:

  1. Information about processes that may create a threat or an opportunity (a new university building going up next door).
  2. A threat or an opportunity has appeared.
  3. It has begun to materialise; a trend is visible.
  4. There is a deviation, but within permissible limits.
  5. The deviation is at the edge of what is permissible.
  6. The parameter has gone beyond the limit (the monthly profit figure arrives, and it is already red).

Lagging indicators live at the bottom of that list. By the time monthly profit is red, you are at step six, and every option left is an emergency option. Leading indicators live at the top. They give you the same information while the choice is still calm.

And early signals are not only bad news. A new university building going up next to Andrew's coffee shop was a step-one signal of opportunity. Whoever sees it at step one prepares in advance. Whoever sees it at step six enters the market along with everyone else.

Switch on the headlights

Leading indicators are how you see a factor before it hits you: enquiries, capacity use, the flow of guests. Think of a car on a night road. With no headlights, the driver learns about the bend from the ditch.

Andrew did not wait for the supplier's invoice to find out that prices were rising. He noticed earlier, in two places: in a conversation with the supplier's account manager, and in the weekly margin per receipt, which dipped before the monthly figure could. Two early sources, one human and one numerical, pointing the same way. That is what recognising a factor looks like in practice.

Leading vs lagging KPI: how to choose and test one

A good leading indicator has four properties. You can check it often (weekly or faster). It moves before the result does. It responds to your levers. And you have tested that it actually predicts the result. Here is how to find one:

  1. Start from a lagging goal. "Margin 15% by month six."
  2. Ask what has to happen first. For margin to rise, each sale has to carry more profit. That points to margin per receipt.
  3. Make it weekly and single-source. Margin per receipt, from the till export, every Monday. One number, one source, one rhythm.
  4. Write down the expected link. "If weekly margin per receipt rises, monthly margin should follow within the month."
  5. Test it. After a month or two, compare. If the leading number moved and the result did not, the link is weaker than you thought, and you choose another indicator.

Freelancers get a particularly clean version. Elena's simple funnel: 1,400 visitors a month to her book page, times 1.5% who buy, times €29, gives about €609 in revenue. Each of the three numbers is a leading indicator for her income goal, and each points to a different lever: more visitors (promotion), a higher share who buy (the page itself), or the price.

Where this does not reach

A leading indicator is a heuristic, not a law. Its link to the future result has to be tested and can break when conditions change: footfall, for example, predicts sales only until something else changes what passers-by do once they are inside. More leading indicators do not mean more control either. One good one per financial goal, checked every week, beats a dashboard of twenty nobody reads. And no indicator replaces the lagging result: the final score is still the score.

Key takeaways

  • Lagging indicators tell you what happened; leading indicators tell you what is likely to happen while you can still act.
  • Every financial goal needs at least one leading indicator, checked weekly.
  • Test the link: write down what the leading number should predict, then compare after a month or two.
  • Early signals can be opportunities too. Whoever sees them first prepares first.

From fire alarm to smoke detector

The most practical change you can make this week costs nothing. Take your most important financial goal, write next to it one number that would move first, and look at that number every Monday. Andrew built his entire system on a shared spreadsheet fed by a weekly till export, nothing more, and his Monday briefing shrank from an hour to fifteen minutes, because the figures showed everything and there was nothing left to argue about.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between lagging and leading indicators?

Lagging indicators, like monthly revenue or profit, report what has already happened. Leading indicators, like enquiries, bookings or margin per receipt this week, point to what is likely to happen next, early enough to act.

What is an example of a leading indicator for a small business?

For a coffee shop aiming at a higher margin: average margin per receipt, checked every week from the till export. It moves before the monthly margin does and responds directly to price, menu and purchasing changes.

How do you know if a leading indicator works?

Write down what it should predict and over what horizon, then compare after a month or two. If the indicator moved and the result did not, the link is weaker than assumed, and you choose another.
