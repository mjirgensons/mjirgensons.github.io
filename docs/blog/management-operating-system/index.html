Management Operating System: The 8-Stage Check for Owners

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

A management operating system is the closed loop a business runs through every week: notice the pressure, name the situation, set ordered goals, plan, build owners and routines, observe, improve, then finish or maintain. In the Jirgenson Model that loop has eight stages. Skip one, and someone else runs it for you: the bank, a competitor or a crisis. You can find your empty stage in fifteen minutes.

Andrew took one day off. His whole management system took the day off with him.

He had goals. He had measured numbers, colour zones and a one-page plan for the margin. Yet the spreadsheet sat untouched. The plan hung on the wall. The only part of the business that moved was Andrew.

You know the feeling. You bought the software. You built the manager dashboard. And the week you go away, nothing on it changes, because nobody looks.

A dashboard nobody reads on Monday is a poster with numbers on it.

The tools were never the problem. The loop that runs them was missing. The answer comes from the Jirgenson Model: control as a closed cycle of eight stages. Every stage has an owner and a date. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

What a management operating system is

In lean manufacturing circles, the phrase means the routines, meetings and measures a company runs on. The idea is sound: a business needs a rhythm, not a pile of tools.

The Jirgenson Model makes that rhythm precise. It treats management as a closed loop: you act, you measure the result, and the result changes the next action. That is closed loop control, the same idea that keeps a ship on course.

In the Jirgenson Model, control is a full cycle of eight stages. Stages 1 and 8 are always present. The stages between them can be merged or split, but none can be dropped. A skipped stage does not vanish: either nobody runs it, or somebody else runs it, and rarely in your favour.

Here are the eight stages, with the question each one must answer.

No. Stage The question to check
1 Notice the pressure Is the threat or chance real, not imagined?
2 Name the situation Would a second observer name it the same way, by the same numbers?
3 Set the goal vector Is there an ordered list of measurable goals?
4 Build the plan and test foresight Is there a plan per goal? Can I foresee the result?
5 Build structures Does every area have a named owner with authority and resources?
6 Observe and coordinate Who looks at the gap between goal and fact, and when?
7 Improve the plan Does the gap between forecast and fact change the plan?
8 Finish or maintain Who guards the result once the goal is reached?

Stages 1 to 4 decide where to go and by what rule. Stages 5 to 7 carry out that rule and adapt it. Stage 8 closes the loop and opens the next one.

The empty stage always has an owner

This is the rule that turns the table into a test. A business cannot avoid any of the eight functions. It can only choose who performs them.

Skip stage 3, and the market, the bank or a competitor sets your goal for you. Skip stage 6, and your debts start watching you instead. Skip stages 1 and 2, and a crisis names the situation for you, at the worst moment.

So the real question is never "do we have a system?". It is "who runs each stage of ours?".

That is systems thinking in business, brought down to a single sheet. You stop asking which tool is missing. You ask which stage of the loop is run by someone outside your control.

The 15-minute stage audit

You need one sheet of paper and the project that hurts most right now. Here are the steps.

  1. Write the eight stage names down the left side. Use the table above.
  2. Write one sentence per stage. What happens at this stage in your project, today? Be literal.
  3. Mark the empty stage. It is the row where your sentence is a wish, a "we should", or blank.
  4. Name who runs it now. The bank, the supplier, a key client, a crisis, or nobody.
  5. Decide one action that fills it. One action, with a date and a name. Not a project.

The third and the sixth stage are empty most often: no measurable goals, only wishes, or no regular look at the numbers.

Here is Andrew's sheet from his coffee shop, month by month.

Stage Andrew's sentence
1. Pressure The cash reserve is melting, and the owner is burning out (M0)
2. Situation Not "a bad season": no cash cushion, and a 9% margin instead of the twenty he believed (M0)
3. Goals Five goals in refusal order, reserve first (M0)
4. Plan One page for the margin; other plans wait while the red row leads (M0 to M1)
5. Structures A shared spreadsheet with five numbers; the till feeds it weekly; each number has an owner (M1)
6. Observe Andrew and Sara meet at the spreadsheet every Monday
7. Improve One item he meant to drop stays: it brings the regulars in (M2)
8. Maintain Reserve at 2.0 months for three months in a row, then maintenance mode

Look at stage 5. It is where an accountability chart belongs: every number has a named owner, with the authority and resources to act. Before month 1, that owner was always Andrew. That is why his day off stopped everything.

A to-do list versus a closed loop

Compare two ways to run the same Monday. This is an illustration from Andrew's case.

The to-do list. Andrew drops the low-margin item from the menu, ticks it off and moves on. A month later he notices fewer regulars. He has no idea why. He brings back the loyalty cards he had shelved, and pays for them with money from a red reserve.

The closed loop. Andrew and Sara sit at the spreadsheet with three questions. Which numbers changed this week? Which zone are they in? What needs doing? The till answers the first, the colours the second, a two-line list the third. The drop in regulars shows up in month 2, not month 6. The plan changes, and the item stays.

The loop also has a speed, and you can count it. In Andrew's shop, noticing a change takes up to 7 days. Deciding takes 1 day, and changing the menu 7 more. Guests need about 30 days to respond. One full turn takes about 45 days.

So his first check of the margin plan sits at month 2, not month 1. Check earlier, and you measure noise, not your lever.

Where the manager dashboard fits

The manager dashboard is the instrument of stage 6. It shows the gap. It does not set goals, write plans or name owners.

A tool is not a lever. With a lever you move the process. With a tool you see and calculate. Confuse the two, and you buy a better screen for a loop that has no stage 3.

"I already run a known method" is a fair objection. Keep it. A meeting rhythm from another method fills stage 6. Its goal format can fill stage 3. The audit only shows which stages your method leaves to chance.

What an empty stage costs

The numbers are plain. Andrew's shop turns over €30,000 a month, so his costs come to €27,300. His reserve of 0.5 months is €13,650. The goal of 2.0 months is €54,600. The gap is €40,950.

At a 9% margin, each month adds about 0.10 months to the reserve. The goal arrives at month 16. With the margin plan working, at month 10.

Now remove stage 6. The menu error stays hidden until month 6 instead of month 2. That is four more months of a plan running on a wrong guess, while the reserve sits under one month. One breakdown in that window costs him a full month of progress.

And the human cost: an owner at sixty-five hours a week. He cannot take a day off, because he is stages 5, 6 and 7 at once.

Where the audit stops

The audit is complete as a tool. It finds the empty stage, names who runs it now and gives you one action to fill it. Use it today.

But it treats a symptom. The cause sits deeper, in stage 4: can you foresee the result at all? A loop that only reacts to last month runs all eight stages too late. It brakes when the brake lights ahead come on.

Two steps of the system fix the cause. They are not taught here.

  • The three ways to control. By plan, by deviation and by forecast, and why only the third can carry all eight stages. It shows which scheme your loop runs on now. Chapter 9.
  • The seven steps of entering control. What to do when the supplier, the machine and a barista all fail in one month. The first urge then is to decide anything at once. Chapter 12.

Without them, every stage has an owner, and the whole loop still steers by the rear-view mirror.

Key takeaways

  • A management operating system is a closed loop of eight stages, not a software licence.
  • A skipped stage still runs. The bank, a competitor or a crisis runs it for you.
  • Stages 3 and 6 are empty most often: wishes instead of goals, and nobody looking on Monday.
  • The dashboard serves stage 6. The accountability chart serves stage 5. Neither is the whole system.
  • Count your loop's speed before you judge a plan. Andrew's turn takes about 45 days.

Your Monday choice

You have two choices. Keep the dashboard, the software and the to-do list, and let the bank run the stage you skipped. Or spend fifteen minutes tonight on eight sentences and find the empty one.

Questions people ask

What is a management operating system?

It is the loop a business runs to reach its goals. Notice the pressure, name the situation, set goals and plan. Then build owners and routines, observe, improve, and finish or maintain. The Jirgenson Model counts eight stages and drops none.

What is closed loop control in business?

You act, measure the result, and let the result change the next action. An open loop follows a plan without checking reality. A closed loop compares goal and fact every week and corrects the course.

What is a control system in management?

A control system is the set of goals, measures, owners and routines that keeps work on course. *Steer, Don't Guess* builds it as eight stages, from noticing the pressure to keeping the result.
