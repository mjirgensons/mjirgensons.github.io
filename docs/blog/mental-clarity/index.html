Mental Clarity: Move the Business Out of Your Head, Onto a Sheet

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

Mental clarity, for someone running a business or a family budget, means knowing where you stand without holding every number in your head. The Jirgenson Model gets you there with one sheet: five rows, each with a goal, today's value from one source, the gap and a colour zone. Built in an evening, it reads in a minute, so your head stops working as the warehouse.

Your daughter blows out the candles. Everyone claps. You clap too, a second late.

Because in your head you're at the bank. The supplier's invoice is due Thursday. Payroll is Friday. The tax date is somewhere in there. You smile for the photo and count.

Four founders out of five hide their stress. Three out of four never ask for help.

Your partner notices anyway. They always do. "Where are you?" they ask later. You say "here". You both know it isn't true.

Your head has become a warehouse. Every invoice, every promise, every number is stored there, with no shelves and no labels. You can work. You prove it every day. What wears you down is carrying the whole business in your skull, with no system to put it in.

The Jirgenson Model gives the answer to that weight. It moves the business out of your head and onto one sheet, so your mind can be at the table again. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

What is mental clarity, for someone who runs things?

For an owner, mental clarity isn't a calm mood you reach by trying harder. It's a plain state: you know where you stand, you know what's urgent, and you don't need to remember it all to know it.

The usual advice says breathe, journal, take a walk. Fine for a bad afternoon. None of it pays Friday's payroll. And a walk with the business still in your head is a walk with the business.

In the Jirgenson Model, clarity comes from objects, not from effort. Each part of the control loop becomes a thing you can hold: a goal table, a data source, a dashboard, a list. Until the tool exists on paper, that part of the loop works only in your head, and in a crisis your head drops it.

Here is the irony. You have a till that counts every coffee and a bank that counts every cent. And you still keep the real picture in the one place with no backup: your memory at 2 a.m.

Lack of clarity is a storage problem

A lack of clarity rarely means you lack information. You have too much of it, in the wrong place. The figures sit in five apps, two notebooks and one tired brain. Each source tells a slightly different story. So you hold all the versions at once, and they argue.

The model's rule here is short: one parameter, one source. If the till says one revenue and the accounts say another, you'll spend more energy on the dispute than on the shop.

That fight happens inside you every evening. One source per number ends it.

How to get mental clarity in one evening: the one sheet

The tool is a table with five rows and five columns: parameter, goal, now, error, zone. It takes about 20 minutes once you have the numbers. Here's how to build it, with Andrew's coffee shop filled in at each step.

1. Pick five things your head keeps checking

Write down the five numbers you check most often in your mind. Turn each into a goal with a value and a deadline. Leave room for yourself: owner's time, health and money count too, and they're the ones left off most often.

Andrew's five: cash reserve, margin, regular customers, complaints, his own hours.

2. Give each row one source

Name where each number comes from. Andrew's reserve and margin come from the accounts, fed by a weekly till export. Complaints come from a notebook behind the counter. Hours come from a time sheet. No source yet? Write down what you need to set up. Paper is fine.

3. Fill in "now" honestly

Write today's value, not the one you hope for. Andrew's first instinct was to write the best month. The accounts said otherwise.

4. Subtract: the gap is the signal

Error means actual minus goal. Here is Andrew's sheet at M0:

Parameter Goal Now Error Zone
Cash reserve, months 2.0 0.5 minus 1.5 Red
Margin, % 15 9 minus 6 Yellow
Regular customers, % 40 25 minus 15 Yellow
Complaints per week 2 or fewer 5 plus 3 Yellow
Owner's hours per week 40 or fewer 65 plus 25 Yellow

When Andrew first saw his gap column, he expected panic. He felt relief instead. The problem had taken shape.

5. Colour each row by a line set in advance

A gap alone doesn't say if it's urgent. Minus six points of margin: is that bad? You need lines, set on a calm evening, never in the middle of the fire. Andrew drew his that same evening:

Parameter Green Yellow Red
Cash reserve, months 2.0 or more 1.0 to under 2.0 under 1.0
Margin, % 14 or more 5 to under 14 under 5
Owner's hours per week 42 or fewer over 42, up to 70 over 70

Green means leave it alone. Yellow means look for the cause while it's still cheap. Red means bring it back first; everything else waits. More on drawing the lines is in traffic light dashboard.

6. Fix one time to read it

Pick one slot a week. Andrew and Sara read the sheet every Monday. The sheet lives in one shared spreadsheet both can see. Within a month their Monday talk shrank from an hour to a quarter of one. There was nothing left to argue about. How often each row needs a look is in how often to review KPIs.

Five rows on one screen. It reads in a minute, and you see at once where the fire is. One red row, four yellow. That's a picture you can carry to a birthday party without carrying the numbers.

Finding peace of mind at home: the same sheet works for a family

The sheet isn't only for shops. Alex is thirteen and saving €300 for a bicycle by 1 September. His sheet has four columns: date, plan, actual, zone.

Date Plan Actual Zone
1 June 105 90 Yellow
1 July 170 140 Yellow
1 August
1 September

His data source is the piggy bank itself. He counts what's inside every Sunday. He doesn't lie awake doing sums. The sheet holds them.

If you run a family budget with a partner, put the sheet where both of you can see it. One shared page ends a lot of evening arguments, as couples budget shows.

The objection you're forming: "I don't have time for a dashboard." You already spend the time. You spend it every night, recomputing the same five numbers in your head. The sheet takes one evening, then a minute a week.

What six more months in the warehouse cost

Andrew's sheet turns the noise into money. His reserve gap of 1.5 months, at monthly expenses of €27,300, is a hole of €40,950. His margin gap of six points on €30,000 of revenue is €1,800 of profit missing every month.

Without the sheet, those holes stay invisible until the bank calls. Leave the margin at 9%, and two months of reserve arrive at M16. Run the plan, and they arrive at M10. That's half a year longer with no cushion.

Then there's the row everyone skips: 65 hours a week against a goal of 40. That's 25 hours a week that don't happen at home.

And the cost you can't put on a sheet: another birthday where you clap a second late.

A note on health. The sheet clears the business clutter. It does not treat anxiety, burnout or low mood. If the fog and the sleepless nights last longer than a bad week, see a doctor. In a crisis, call a crisis line (988 in the US and Canada).

Where the one sheet stops

Be clear about what you built. The sheet shows where you stand and which row is on fire. It treats the symptom: your head stops being the warehouse.

The cause of the next sleepless night is still in place. You can see the red reserve. You don't yet know which levers you can pull yourself, today, to move it. And the sheet shows today, not where the reserve will be in three months.

Two steps close that gap:

  • The list of levers. What you can change yourself, how each lever moves a number, and who owns it. Chapter 5. A start is in business levers.
  • Two forecasts. One line for doing nothing, one for the plan working, each with numbers and dates. The bad month appears on paper before it arrives. Chapter 8. See how to forecast cash flow.

Without levers, the sheet tells you where it hurts and leaves your hands empty. Without forecasts, it rings after the damage. The sheet is one part of a whole loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. The full set of tools behind it is in management system for a small business.

Key takeaways

  • Mental clarity, for an owner, is a storage problem: move the numbers out of your head.
  • One sheet: five rows, five columns. Parameter, goal, now, error, zone.
  • One number, one source. That rule ends the argument inside your head.
  • Draw the zone lines on a calm evening, never in the middle of a fire.
  • A head is a poor warehouse and a good captain.

Two choices for this week

You have two choices. Keep the whole business in your head, and recount it at every dinner and every birthday. Or spend one evening putting five rows on one sheet.

The sheet is the picture. Levers and forecasts are the hands and the eyes ahead.

Questions people ask

What is mental clarity?

For someone running a business or a household, mental clarity is knowing where you stand without holding every figure in your head. You know what's urgent and what can wait. It comes from writing your goals, today's numbers and their zones on one sheet. A sibling walk-through is in [business stress](/blog/business-stress/).

How do you get clarity when your head is full of the business?

Write down the five numbers you check most often. Give each a goal, one source and today's value. Subtract to get the gap, then colour it green, yellow or red by lines you set in advance. Read the sheet once a week, at a fixed time.

How do I find peace of mind as a business owner?

Stop storing the business in your memory. Put it on one sheet that someone else could read too. Peace of mind follows a picture you trust: one red row is a task, not a catastrophe. If worry keeps you up for weeks, talk to a doctor.
