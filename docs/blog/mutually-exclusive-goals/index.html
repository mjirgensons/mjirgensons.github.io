Mutually Exclusive Goals: the Arithmetic Test Before You Plan

Martin Jirgenson31 Oct 2026 · 8 min read
Short answer

Mutually exclusive goals are goals that no single state of your business can satisfy at once: reach one and you miss the other, whatever the effort. Find them by giving each goal a number, then adding up what a pair demands from the same hours, cash or capacity. If the sum breaks a hard limit, drop, cap, sequence or condition one goal before you plan.

Andrew's two goals fitted on one line of paper. They did not fit into one week.

"No more than 40 hours a week." "Open a second location." He already worked 48 hours. The launch would add 15 more, every week, for three months. That makes 63 hours against a ceiling of 40.

Now look at your own list for next quarter. Every line is sensible. Grow sales. Cut your hours. Keep the cash. Hire nobody. Each Monday one of them slips, and you blame your discipline.

You bought the planner, the habit app and the course on focus. The fault sat in a sum nobody did.

What mutually exclusive goals are

Two goals are mutually exclusive when no single state of the business meets both. Not hard. Not ambitious. Impossible, inside the limits you have this quarter.

That is a different animal from competing priorities. A competing priorities example: Elena, a freelance designer, wants steady income and a 35-hour week. While she writes her first book, the two collide. She settles it by order: for three months she may work up to 55 hours, then the cap returns. Both goals survive, one after the other.

Conflicting goals of the exclusive kind have no winning order. Rank them any way you like; the sum still breaks. The full line between the two kinds is drawn in competing priorities vs conflicting goals.

In the Jirgenson Model, a goal list that holds mutually exclusive goals is offset (a), a badly defined goal. The set of states in which every goal is met is empty, so the plan has no destination. It is the traveller trying to board two trains that leave in opposite directions.

Effort cannot fix arithmetic.

The ten-minute test for mutually exclusive goals

Take a sheet of paper. There are four steps. Each one sits next to a real case from the coffee shop and its neighbours.

1. Put a number on every goal

A goal needs a number, a unit and a deadline. "Fewer hours" cannot clash with anything, because nobody can check it. "No more than 40 hours a week by month 12" can.

Andrew's entries: owner's time, at most 40 hours a week. Second location: 15 extra hours a week for the three launch months.

2. Write the hard limits beside them

A hard limit is what you cannot stretch this quarter: hours in a week, cash in the account, seats in the room, credit already drawn. Add the constraints too. The law, safety, contracts and the load one person can carry are not goals to trade. They decide which actions are allowed at all.

Andrew's entry: 48 hours already worked each week. A medium-sized company with a warehouse: credit lines fully drawn, no money for raw materials.

3. Add up what each pair asks of one resource

Take two goals that draw on the same hours, money or capacity. Add their demands. Set the total against the limit.

Pair Total demand Limit Verdict
Owner's hours and a second location 48 + 15 = 63 hours a week 40 hours Exclusive for three months
Sales up 50% and no new capital Raw materials for half as much again Credit fully drawn Exclusive
Alex's bicycle fund and unlimited games His odd jobs need evenings Two free evenings a week from June Exclusive

If the total fits under the limit, the goals only compete, and the refusal order settles them. If it breaks the limit, you have found a pair that cannot both exist.

4. Pick one of four exits

Choose before any work begins. The goal you would give up first is the one that bends.

  • Drop. Remove one goal. The warehouse company cannot have "sales up 50%" and "no new capital" together, so one of the two goes.
  • Cap. Shrink one goal until the sum fits. Alex, thirteen, kept football and the bicycle and capped games at ten hours a week.
  • Sequence. One goal first, the other after. Andrew made the second location wait until his reserve held at two months for three months in a row. Then he would revisit his hours on purpose, not by default.
  • Condition. Tie one action to a number. The same trap hides one level down, in plans. Sara noticed that one plan raised prices while another launched discount cards. Andrew added a line: card discounts start once the margin reaches at least 14%.

That is the whole test. Numbers, limits, one sum per pair, one exit.

Usual way, arithmetic way

Here is the same pair handled twice. The left column is an illustration.

The usual way (illustration) The arithmetic way
Monday Both goals on the wall, both "top priority" 63 against 40, written on one line
Month 1 Long weeks, guilt about the hours goal The second location waits for its condition
Month 3 A tired owner and a half-built launch Hours held; the launch ready on paper
The owner's verdict "I lack discipline" "I chose an order"

The usual way treats the clash as a character flaw. The arithmetic way treats it as a missing line of maths. Only the second can be fixed by Friday.

"Surely good goals should stretch me?" They should. A stretch goal has at least one state where everything is met, even if it is hard to reach. Mutually exclusive goals have none. Stretch pulls you forward; an empty set pulls you apart.

What an unresolved pair costs

Start with money. Andrew's margin plan needs four hours of his week and Sara's help. That plan is what lifts the margin from 9% to 15%.

The margin feeds the cash reserve. At 9%, the reserve climbs from half a month to two months by month 16. With the plan working, it gets there by month 10. A clash that eats those four hours puts the plan at risk and the shop back on the slow line. At 9% margin, one breakdown also costs a full month of progress. At 15% it costs about half.

Then the human cost. In his first months Andrew worked sixty-five hours a week. His worst mistakes came late at night: mixed-up figures, a delayed supplier call, wrong deadlines.

The enemy here is the advice to try harder. It sounds noble. It turns a sum into a guilt trip and sends a tired owner back into the same impossible week.

The gap this test leaves open

Be clear about what the test does. It treats a symptom: a goal list that cannot exist. The cause sits deeper.

Plans are never checked against one shared budget of hours and money. And the starting numbers the sums rest on may be wrong.

This is one step of a system. The loop runs goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast, and this test cleans only the first link. Two further steps close the gap.

  • One resource table for every plan. Plans that pass pair by pair can still claim the same hours and the same money together. The pair test cannot see that. Chapter 6 holds the method.
  • The other two offsets. A starting point you have measured wrong, and an environment that is going under. Either one breaks a plan before work begins, even with a clean goal list. Chapter 11 holds the checks.

Without them, you stop chasing an impossible pair. You do not yet know whether the possible plan can be paid for.

Key takeaways

  • Mutually exclusive goals have no state where both are met. No order fixes them.
  • Give every goal a number, write the hard limits, add up each pair.
  • Four exits: drop, cap, sequence, condition. The goal you would give up first bends.
  • Run the same test on plans, not only on goals.
  • Effort cannot fix arithmetic.

Two choices before Monday

You have two choices tonight. Keep both goals on the wall, work the 63-hour week and call the miss a lack of discipline. Or do the sum, pick an exit and plan only what can exist.

Next Monday a new request lands on your desk. You check it against one line of arithmetic, not against your mood.

The test clears the goal list. The resource table and the other two offsets decide whether the plan built on it can hold.

Questions people ask

What are mutually exclusive goals?

Goals that no single state of your business, project or life can meet at the same time, given your real limits. "No more than 40 hours a week" and "open a second location" are exclusive when the launch alone pushes the week to 63 hours.

How do you resolve conflicting goals?

Give each goal a number and a deadline, write your hard limits, and add up what each pair asks of the same resource. If the total breaks a limit, drop, cap, sequence or condition the goal you would give up first.

Are competing priorities the same as conflicting goals?

No. Competing priorities share money, time or people, but some order lets each one win in turn. Mutually exclusive goals cannot all be met in any order. The full control system, including the resource table for plans, comes on Kindle.
