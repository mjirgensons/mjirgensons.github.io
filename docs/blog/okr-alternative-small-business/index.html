An OKR alternative for small business: what OKRs leave out

Martin Jirgenson30 Oct 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer

A good OKR alternative for small business keeps what OKRs do well (few goals, measurable results) and adds what a small team needs most: a refusal order that says which goal wins when two collide, three zones for every number so you know when to act, and named levers for each goal. The Jirgenson Model is built that way and can sit on top of OKRs rather than replace them.

OKRs are a brilliant invention. Andy Grove built them at Intel, John Doerr carried them to Google, and the idea at their core is hard to argue with: a few ambitious objectives, each with measurable key results.

So why do so many small businesses try OKRs, love the first quarterly workshop and quietly drop them by the second? Not because the idea is wrong. Because a ten-person company, or a one-person one, hits the questions OKRs were never designed to answer. Which key result wins when two of them pull against each other? At what point is a number bad enough to drop everything? And on a tired Thursday, what exactly do I do?

OKR for small business: the same goals, written twice

The fairest way to compare is to take one real set of goals and write it both ways. Elena is a freelance designer. Her income swings between €800 and €2,200 a month, one client brings in 70% of it, and she works fifty hours a week. She is also preparing her first online book.

Here are her goals as a standard OKR (an illustration of the format, built from her real numbers):

Objective: Build an income that does not depend on one client. KR1: Three-month average income of at least €3,000 a month by month 14. KR2: Largest client's share of income no more than 30% by month 14. KR3: No more than 35 working hours a week.

That is a good OKR. Clear objective, measurable results, a deadline. Now the same three goals written as a goal vector:

OKR Goal vector
Order Three key results, implicitly equal 1. Income (defended last) · 2. Client share · 3. Hours (given up first)
Current values Not part of the format Income about €1,300 average; largest client 70%; 50 hours a week
When to act At the quarterly review Zones per number: client share normal up to 33%, acceptable above 33% up to 50%, emergency above 50%
Today's status "In progress" Client share at 70%: red zone, act now
Planned exceptions None Up to 55 hours a week allowed from month 1 to month 3 while writing the book, then back to 35
What to do Left to initiatives Levers: package prices, emails to former clients, two new channels, the book (manuscript, €29 price, a sales page)

Look at the fourth row. The OKR says "in progress". The goal vector says the most important thing in Elena's working life is in the emergency zone today, and the right column tells her what to do about it on Monday.

The OKR alternative: three things OKRs leave out

1. A rule for when key results collide

Elena's third key result says 35 hours. Writing the book needs more. In an OKR, that is a silent conflict: either she breaks KR3 and feels she is failing, or she protects it and the book never gets written.

The goal vector settles it in advance. Hours sit at the bottom of the order, the first thing she would give up. So the overtime is not a lapse; it is a decision made in a calm hour, with a limit (55) and an end date (month three). When the long weeks come, there is nothing to argue about. This is the refusal order, and it is the single biggest addition. If the idea is new to you, the difference between goals that merely compete and goals that genuinely cannot coexist is worked through in competing priorities vs conflicting goals.

2. Zones that tell you when to move

An OKR is graded at the end of the quarter. A small business cannot wait a quarter to learn that its biggest client has become 70% of revenue. Three zones per number (normal, acceptable, emergency) turn every key result into an early warning. Green means leave it alone, which matters more than it sounds: fixing what works is management's most common sin. Red triggers a protocol: freeze secondary work, put time and money on the red number and the levers that act on it, and if several numbers are red, deal first with the one that threatens the most imminent irreversible damage.

3. Levers, not just results

"Largest client no more than 30%" is a result. You cannot pull a result. You can raise package prices, write to former clients, open two new channels and publish a book. Naming the levers is what turns a key result into a Monday task, and it is where most OKR initiatives stay vague.

How it turned out

Two months in, Elena saw that service packages were selling better than one-off jobs, and rebuilt the whole plan around them. By month 14: main client €900, packages €1,200, repeat orders €300, the book about €600. Total €3,000. Main client's share: 30%.

Every key result met. But notice what got her there: a weekly look at the numbers, a red zone that demanded action from day one, and a plan she was allowed to change while the goals stayed fixed.

OKR vs balanced scorecard, and where each fits

Since this question usually comes next: the Balanced Scorecard (Kaplan and Norton) asks you to look at a business from several sides, finance, customers, internal processes, learning and growth. OKRs focus you on a few goals for the period with measurable results. They answer different questions, and both are strong.

The Jirgenson Model does not replace either. It gives each a place:

Method What it contributes What the model adds
OKR A small number of goals with measurable results A refusal order between goals, error zones, levers per goal
Balanced Scorecard Several sides of the business to measure (akin to the six areas of the goal vector) An explicit refusal order, three zones per parameter, a protocol for when several are red
MBO (Drucker) Agreed goals with personal responsibility A review rhythm within the period and control by forecast
PDCA A small cycle of checking and improvement Three control schemes; PDCA works as a local improvement loop inside the model

OKRs and MBO supply the wording of goals. The Balanced Scorecard suggests which sides of the business to measure. The model links them into one system and answers the questions that matter most when everything is on fire.

In the Jirgenson Model, the goal vector is an ordered list of at most nine measurable goals, each with a current value, a permissible deviation and a check rhythm. The order (the refusal order) says which goal is given up first and which last, and it decides every collision in advance.

Where this does not reach

If OKRs are working for your team, keep them; nothing here asks you to throw them out. The goal vector is designed for a person or a small team working mostly on paper, which is why it caps the list at nine. Large organisations with many teams need alignment mechanisms that this model does not try to provide. And neither OKRs nor a goal vector makes your goals the right ones: that still takes judgement.

Key takeaways

  • OKRs give focus and measurable results; small businesses usually also need an order, zones and levers.
  • A refusal order settles key-result collisions in advance, including planned exceptions like overtime with an end date.
  • Three zones per number replace "in progress" with "act now" or "leave it alone".
  • The Jirgenson Model can sit on top of OKRs, MBO and the Balanced Scorecard rather than replace them.

Keep the focus, add the steering

What I like most about this approach is its modesty: it does not ask you to abandon a method that works, only to add the three things a small business feels most sharply. Elena's story is the proof in miniature. Same goals as a good OKR. What made the difference was knowing, on any Monday, which number was red, which goal came first and which lever to pull.

Questions people ask

What is a good OKR alternative for a small business?

One that keeps OKRs' focus on a few measurable goals and adds a refusal order (which goal wins when two collide), three zones per number (when to act) and named levers (what to do). The Jirgenson Model works this way and can sit on top of existing OKRs.

What is the difference between OKR and Balanced Scorecard?

OKRs focus a team on a few objectives with measurable key results for a period. The Balanced Scorecard measures the business from several sides: finance, customers, internal processes, and learning and growth. They answer different questions and are often used together.

Why do OKRs fail in small businesses?

Often not because the goals are wrong, but because the format does not say which key result wins in a conflict, when a number is bad enough to act, or which concrete lever to pull on Monday.
