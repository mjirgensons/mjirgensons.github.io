One Person Business: Decide What You Give Up First, in Advance

Martin Jirgenson6 Nov 2026 · 8 min read
Short answer

A one person business has no partner to break a tie, so the order of your goals must exist before the hard day. Write three to nine goals, each with a number, a unit and a deadline. Put your own pay and your reserve on the list. Then rank them with the refusal test: what you would give up first, and what you would defend to the end.

Working alone? Three out of four solo businesses are gone within eight years.

Alone means nobody to argue with. It also means nobody to stop you.

Picture a Thursday. A client wants a rush job by Monday. Your own project has slipped again. The tax payment is due. You said yes to all three before lunch, because you were tired and saying no felt rude.

Here is the irony. A one person business has the shortest chain of command in the world. One person. And still no one decides what matters most, so the last email decides.

Fix the order once, on paper, with a clear head.

A one person business runs on an order, not on effort

Think of a captain who loves two ports. A ship can't sail both ways at once. He dashes back and forth, burns fuel and arrives nowhere. You choose the port before you put to sea, not in the storm.

Do it tonight. It takes 10 to 20 minutes. Here is the whole tool, filled in with Elena, a freelance designer who is writing her first online book.

1. Write what you want for the next year. Up to five items, in plain words. Elena: a steady income; no single client holding most of her money; fewer working hours.

2. Cross out every method. A goal says what you want. A method says how you will get it. Ask "What for?" of each item until a goal is left. Andrew's first draft for his coffee shop had eight items. Three were methods: loyalty cards, a second barista, a new coffee machine. Loyalty cards are there to bring customers back, so the goal is the share of regular customers.

3. Give each goal a number, a unit and a deadline. "A good income" has no number. "3,000" has no unit. "€3,000 a month" has no deadline. Elena: €3,000 a month as a three-month average, reached by month 14. Her biggest client at 30% of income or less, by the same month. A week of 35 hours at most.

4. Run the refusal test. Suppose you had half the time and money. Strike the goals off one by one. The easiest sacrifice goes first. Elena crossed out her hours first. Then the client share. Income stayed to the end.

5. Number the list in reverse. What you crossed out last is number one.

Crossed out Elena's goal Rank
First Hours: 35 a week at most, with up to 55 allowed in months 1 to 3 3
Second Biggest client: 30% of income at most, by month 14 2
Last Income: €3,000 a month or more, as a three-month average, by month 14 1

6. Write the constraints above the list. These are never given up in turn: the law, safety, contracts, the load a person can bear. Elena's: no emails to people who have not subscribed.

That is the tool. Look at row 3. Hours come first to go, so Elena may work up to 55 while she writes the book. Not a lapse. A decision made in advance, with an end date.

How to avoid the Thursday pile-up: when a request lands, ask which goal it serves and what number that goal holds. Then the list answers, not your fatigue.

In the Jirgenson Model, a goal vector is an ordered list of measurable goals, no more than nine for a person working without software. The refusal test sets the order: the goal you would defend to the end gets number one.

A self funded business puts the reserve on the list

Nearly two out of three businesses start on the owner's own savings.

Your savings are your stability margin. A broken coffee machine is a nuisance for a shop with two months of reserve. For one with two weeks, it's an emergency. For a solo owner, the laptop is the coffee machine.

So write the reserve as a goal, in months of expenses. Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, ranked his reserve number one. His reason was plain. Without a reserve the shop closes, and then nothing else on the list matters.

But number one doesn't override common sense. A small saving on equipment maintenance brings money today. It raises the risk of a breakdown tomorrow. Constraints and minimums sit above the ranking. Only the improvement goals get ranked.

How to avoid draining the savings by drift: check the reserve on a fixed day, not when the bank app scares you.

How to pay yourself: your pay is a goal row

Half of all owners have gone months without paying themselves.

Of the six areas of a goal list, one gets forgotten most: development and the owner. Your own time, health and money. Yet your own needs are often goal number one.

For Elena they are. Her income is her pay, and it holds the top row.

Write your pay the same way. One row, one number, one unit, one deadline. Then measure it as an average, never as your best month.

Elena's bank statements for six months showed income between €800 and €2,200. The average was €1,300. Take three months at €900, €1,800 and €1,200: the average is €1,300 a month. One good month does not make an average.

How to avoid the unpaid months: rank your pay before the business can rank it for you. If you place it last, do it on paper, by choice.

Counting goals against ordering goals

Two plans for the end of Andrew's year. Both meet four goals out of five. Illustration from his numbers.

Goal Plan X Plan Y
1. Reserve, target 2.0 months 2.0, met 1.8, not met
2. Margin, target 15% 15%, met 16%, met
3. Regular customers, target 40% 32%, not met 42%, met
4. Complaints, target 2 a week 2, met 1, met
5. Owner's hours, target 40 40, met 38, met

Count the ticks and the plans tie. Read the order and X wins. The first difference is in row 1, and X meets it.

A solo owner faces that choice every week. Without an order, the plan with more ticks looks better. With an order, you protect what you would defend to the end.

What a list without an order costs

Now take the other path. Elena keeps all three goals "equally important".

Her main client brings €910 of her €1,300 average: 70%. Her own red line for that share sits above 50%. She is past it today.

Her income goal is €3,000 a month. The gap is €1,700 a month. Six more months at the old average leave her €10,200 short of that line.

Andrew saw the same risk in his coffee shop. Had he kept five equal goals, the milk price would have risen within a week. Sara would have asked for another barista. He would have said yes, tired. A month later the money would have gone before the year did.

The human cost is plain. Fifty hours a week, and the evenings still go to whoever asked last.

"I'm one person. I don't need a system."

A person working without software keeps at most nine goals. Elena has three. The test takes one sheet of paper and up to twenty minutes.

That is the point of being alone. There is no meeting to hold. There is only a list to read.

Where the list stops

The goal list is the first link of one loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. It treats the symptom. You stop letting the last email set your priorities.

The cause can still win. You know income comes first, but not at what point a thin month turns into "act today". And a list of goals doesn't say what to do on Monday to reach them.

Two more steps finish the job.

  • Three zones and the red protocol. The line between a thin month and an emergency. Which row to save first when two cross it together. Chapter 4.
  • One page per goal. Levers, resources, constraints, a leading indicator and a deadline, so each goal has a plan you can check every week. Chapter 6.

Without them, the list gives you direction. It doesn't yet tell you when to turn or how hard.

Key takeaways

  • Alone, nobody breaks the tie. Write the order before the hard day.
  • Cross out methods. A goal carries a number, a unit and a date.
  • Rank with the refusal test: half the resources, cross out one at a time.
  • Put your pay and your reserve on the list as full rows.
  • If you don't decide what matters more, fatigue decides for you.

Two choices for this week

You have two choices. Keep saying yes in the order the requests arrive, and find out in a year which goal quietly lost. Or write three to nine ranked goals tonight, with your own pay among them.

Next Thursday the rush job lands, and you already know the answer.

The list gives you the order. The zones, the red protocol and one page per goal turn that order into weekly action.

Questions people ask

How do I run a one person business without burning out?

Write your hours as a goal with a number, and rank it with the refusal test. If you let it go first for a season, write the cap and the end date in advance. Elena allowed 55 hours for three months, then back to 35.

How much should a solo business owner pay themselves?

Pick the figure your life needs and write it as a goal with a deadline. Measure it as an average over three months, not your best month. Elena's target was at least €3,000 a month by month 14.

What goals should a one person business set?

Three to nine, across finance, clients, sales, quality, people and your own time and money. Each needs a number, a unit and a deadline. The full method, with zones and plans, is in the complete control system.
