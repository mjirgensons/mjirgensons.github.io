Pre Mortem: Find Out Why Your Plan Fails Before It Starts

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

To make it steer, sort each reason into a bad goal, a wrong starting number or a failing environment, run the check question for it, and turn every reason into one written line of the plan.

Fewer than one project in ten lands on time and on budget.

Now picture your plan as one of the nine. Where did it break: on the day it died, or on the day you wrote it?

Picture a driver at night. The fuel gauge says full. The sensor is stuck, and the tank is empty. He pulls out to overtake, and the engine dies halfway past the truck. His driving was fine. His starting number was wrong.

Now picture a second driver with a full tank and a burst coolant hose. Skill doesn't matter here. In two minutes the engine overheats, and the only right move is to stop.

Both drivers could have found the problem before they turned the key. That is the job of a pre mortem.

A plan that has never been tested against its own failure is a hope with a deadline. The answer to this pain comes from the Jirgenson Model. Imagine the failure, sort every reason by where the plan broke, and fix it in writing. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

What is a pre mortem?

A pre mortem is a planning exercise. Before the work starts, you imagine that the plan has already failed. Then you write down every reason why it failed.

A post mortem asks why the patient died. A pre mortem asks the same question before the operation, while you can still change the plan. That is the whole pre mortem definition. It is a known method, older than this model.

The usual output is a list of worries in the meeting notes. "Budget might run out." "Clients might be slow." The list feels useful. Then the plan goes ahead unchanged, because nothing on the list told anyone what to change.

In the Jirgenson Model, every plan rests on three anchor points: the goal, the starting state and the environment. An offset is a shift of one of them relative to reality. The exercise becomes a control step when each imagined reason lands on one anchor point. And each one ends in a written change to the plan.

So the pre mortem meaning changes. It is no longer a session of worrying. It is a sorting job with a check question for every pile.

Run a pre mortem in five steps

You need one sheet, one plan and about fifteen minutes. Here is the method, with a worked example next to each step.

The example is Elena, a freelance designer. Her income swings between €800 and €2,200 a month. One client brings in 70% of it. She works fifty hours a week. Her plan: write her first online book, launch it at month 4, and reach an average income of €3,000 by month 14.

1. Name the date of death

Pick the date on which the plan should have worked. Write one sentence as if it has already failed.

Elena's sentence (an illustration): "It is month 14. My income still swings, my big client still pays most of it, and the book barely sells."

2. Write every reason, fast

Ten minutes, no editing, no defending. Write as a person who has watched it fail.

Elena's list:

  • I ran out of hours in the writing months.
  • I planned with more income than I had.
  • The platform changed its rules in the launch month.
  • Visitors came to the page and did not buy.
  • I judged the book after one month and gave up.

3. Sort each reason onto an anchor point

This is the step the usual version skips. Every reason goes into one of four piles.

Pile The anchor that broke Check question
(a) Bad goal The goal contradicts itself Does my list hold goals that cannot be met at the same time?
(b) Wrong start The starting numbers are off Do my numbers match the data in the systems?
(c) Failing environment The larger system is fading Is the market around me growing or declining?
Other A cause outside the three Wrong cause and effect, short resources, poor execution, clashing incentives or unacceptable risk?

The fourth pile matters. The three offsets do not explain every failure. A reason that fits none of them still needs an answer.

4. Turn every reason into one line of the plan

A reason without a line in the plan is still a worry. Here is Elena's sheet after sorting:

Reason Pile What the check showed Line added to the plan
Ran out of hours (a) 120 hours of writing in three months, on top of fifty-hour weeks, against a goal of 35 hours Time goes first in her refusal order: up to 55 hours a week from month 1 to month 3, then back down
Planned with more income (b) One month brought €2,200, so she felt she earned about €2,000. Six months of bank statements said €1,300 Plan from the statements, not from the best month
Platform changed its rules (c) A factor she cannot control, not a dying market on its own Collect her own mailing list, and email only people who subscribed
Visitors did not buy Other: cause and effect The page is a lever; the share of buyers is a result Leading indicator of 350 visitors a week. If fewer than 1.2% buy two weeks running, rewrite the headline
Gave up after one month Other: execution A move from client work to a product takes months, not weeks First check at month 5, target at month 14. No verdict before then

Look at the last column. Every line is something she can do, check or refuse. None of them says "be careful".

5. Mark the "I don't know" answers

Go back through the check questions. Wherever your honest answer is "I don't know", you have found a risk. Write it down as a measurement to take before you start.

That rule turns ignorance into a task. Elena's "I don't know" was her real income. One evening with her bank statements closed it.

The usual version vs one that steers

Here is the same plan run both ways. It is an illustration.

The usual way Through the model's rules
Output A list of worries One line of the plan per reason
"Not enough time" "Let's try to manage it" A time limit in the refusal order, with an end date
"Income might be lower" Noted Income measured from six months of statements
"Sales might be slow" "We'll push marketing" A leading indicator and a written trigger
What happens next The plan starts unchanged The plan starts changed

The usual version finds the cracks and leaves them. The second one fills each crack before the first brick goes on.

Stress test the business plan: what skipping the check costs

The exercise finds the reasons. A stress test of the business puts a number on one of them. And the numbers are blunt.

Take Elena's offset (b). Her felt income was about €2,000. Her real one was €1,300. Had she planned on the feeling, she would have counted on €700 a month that did not exist. Across the three writing months, that is €2,100, while she turned down small orders to free up hours.

Now Andrew, who runs a coffee shop. Six months into his recovery plan, his margin hit 15% and he started to dream of a second location. Opening one costs about €45,000, or 1.8 months of expenses. His reserve stood at 1.35 months. He could not even have paid for the opening.

Say he had waited for a reserve of 2.3 months and then signed. Right after opening he would have had 0.5 months left. That is the red zone in the first month. He would have lost what six months of work had built.

And the human cost: the night before payroll, with a new lease signed and the old shop paying for both.

The question "how does this fail?" costs fifteen minutes. Skipping it would have cost Elena €2,100 and Andrew his reserve.

Where the exercise stops

It treats one symptom well: the surprise at launch. You start with a plan that has already met its own failure once.

The deeper problem stays. The exercise checks the plan once, on the day before you start. It does not tell you when to start or how far you may stray. Nor does it build the route back from the goal.

Two steps close that gap:

  • Launch conditions and the tolerance corridor. When a plan may start, written as a measurable event, not a date. And how far each number may drift on the way. Chapter 10.
  • The backward move. Walking from the goal back to today, so this week's first action falls out of the plan by itself. Chapter 15.

Without them, the plan survives the start. It still drifts in month three without anyone noticing.

Here is where it sits in the whole system. Goals, the state and the environment are the three anchors. This check covers all three before you pull a lever. Then the error, the plan and the forecast take over.

The likely objection: "My plan is small. I don't need a ritual." Elena's plan was one freelancer and one book. One check of her starting number showed €700 a month of income that did not exist. The smaller the business, the less room there is for that kind of gap.

Key takeaways

  • Imagine the plan already failed, then list why.
  • Sort every reason onto an anchor: the goal, the starting numbers or the environment. Keep a fourth pile for other causes.
  • Turn each reason into one written line of the plan, or it stays a worry.
  • Every "I don't know" is a measurement to take before you start.
  • A plan that never met its own failure is a hope with a deadline.

Two choices before you start

You have two choices. Start the plan tomorrow as it stands, and meet its failure in month six, when it costs real money. Or take fifteen minutes tonight, write the date of death, and sort the reasons.

The check gets the plan through the start. The launch conditions, the corridor and the backward move keep it on course after that.

Questions people ask

What is a pre mortem?

A planning exercise in which you imagine the plan has already failed and write down every reason why. You run it before the work starts, so the reasons can still change the plan.

What does pre mortem mean in business?

It means testing a business plan against its own failure before you spend money. Sort each reason into a bad goal, a wrong starting number or a failing environment. Then add one line to the plan for each.

How do you stress test a business plan?

Take the biggest cost in the plan and subtract it from your cash reserve at the moment you would pay it. If the reserve falls below one month of expenses, the plan is not ready to start. Check your starting numbers against real statements first.
