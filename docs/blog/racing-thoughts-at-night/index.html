Racing Thoughts at Night: Move the 3 a.m. Sums to the Morning

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

Racing thoughts at night about the business are often sums your tired head can't finish: it reacts to yesterday's jump and calls that a plan. Check for real danger, write the question in one line, and set a time to answer it. Then answer it on a rested morning with a forecast: the usual pattern, the tolerance, and two lines to compare.

It's 2:40 a.m., and your head is running the same sum for the fourth time.

If Thursday's payment lands late. If the supplier raises prices again. If the quiet Tuesday was the start of something.

Your partner is asleep beside you, back turned. You lie still so you don't wake them. Inside, the numbers spin like a slot machine that never pays out.

Nearly half of owners lie awake at night worrying about the business.

Here's what makes it worse. You can work. You've built something real with your own hands. The problem isn't effort. You carry every number, every risk and every date in your head, with no system to hold them. So at night, the head does the only thing it can: it keeps them all spinning.

The Jirgenson Model gives you the answer. Take the decision off the night shift. Give it to a rested morning, with a forecast instead of a fear. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

Racing thoughts at night run the worst rule in the test

There are three ways to steer anything: by plan, by deviation and by forecast.

  • By plan. You set a rule and follow it, whatever happens.
  • By deviation. You measure yesterday's miss and correct for it.
  • By forecast. You learn the pattern, look ahead and act before the miss.

The 3 a.m. head steers by deviation. It takes yesterday's jump and treats it as the new normal. One quiet day becomes a falling business. One late payment becomes a cash crisis.

Andrew's croissants show what that rule costs. He tested four ordering rules on two weeks of real sales at his coffee shop. Here is the total miss for the second week.

Ordering rule Total miss, croissants
"A hundred every day" (by plan) 100
"As many as I sold yesterday" (by deviation) 130
"Shift yesterday's order by a third of the error" 119
"As many as the same day last week" (by forecast) 76

Look at the second row. The fastest rule did worse than the fixed plan. It chased random jumps.

That's your night. A brain chasing yesterday's noise at speed, with no pattern to hold it. At 3 a.m. your head runs the "as yesterday" rule, and it's the worst rule in the test.

In the Jirgenson Model, control by forecast closes the loop through the expected future, not the recorded past. You find the pattern first. Then you decide which deviations deserve a response. Only then do you act, and new facts correct the forecast.

Stress and sleep: fatigue corrupts a forecast

In his first months, Andrew worked sixty-five hours a week and made decisions late at night. Those were the hours of his worst mistakes. He mixed up figures. He put off calling the supplier. He gave customers wrong deadlines.

Fatigue corrupts a forecast like a faulty instrument.

So the fix isn't to think harder at night. It's to stop deciding at night at all. That idea sits next to another one: decide once, while calm, so a tired head never has to.

The morning forecast: six steps, two times of day

The tool has a night half and a morning half. The night half takes two minutes. The morning half takes about fifteen. Each step below carries a real entry from the book's coffee shop.

At night

1. Check for real danger. Is there an immediate risk to people, to safety or to mandatory payments, or a legal risk? If yes, act on that now and tell someone who can help. If no, nothing gets decided tonight.

The evening Andrew learned that a barista had quit and the supplier had raised prices, he ran this check. No danger to people or payments. So he decided nothing that evening, arranged to meet Sara, his senior barista, in the morning and went to bed.

2. Park the question in one line. Keep a notepad by the bed. Write the question and the time you'll answer it. Andrew's line could read: "Can the margin carry the price rise? Answer at 8:00, with Sara."

The question now has a place. Your head doesn't have to hold it until dawn.

In the morning

3. Name the rule you used at night. Was it "as yesterday"? Usually the night sum takes one bad day and stretches it into a year. Write the jump as one line: what moved, by how much, since when.

4. Find the pattern. Pull the real history, not the feeling. Andrew recorded croissant sales by day and weather for a few weeks. The picture was clear: about 70 on a Tuesday, about 130 on a Saturday, 15% fewer when it rains. A rainy Saturday now gets an order of 130 × 0.85, about 110.

Quiet Tuesdays stopped looking like a crisis. They were Tuesdays.

5. Decide if the deviation deserves a response. Inside the tolerance, the levers stay put. Outside it, you act. Faster doesn't mean better: speed up the response without this rule, and you start correcting noise. Here's how lagging and leading indicators help you tell a trend from a jump.

6. Draw two lines and pick one action. Line one: if nothing changes. Line two: if the plan works. Then choose one lever and a check date.

In that bad month, Andrew's margin had fallen from 15% to 12%, and his reserve stood at 1.56 months of expenses. Line one, change nothing: the reserve first reaches two months at month 12. Line two, price up 5%: margin back to 15%, two months at month 11. He chose one lever, the price. Checks: receipts and complaints in three weeks, margin in a month and a half. You can draw the same two lines with how to forecast cash flow.

That's the whole tool. The night gets two minutes and one line. The morning gets the sums.

Anxiety at night: what the model does and doesn't do

The morning forecast moves business decisions away from the hours when your head is least fit to make them. It does not treat an illness, and it's no promise of sleep.

If you lie awake most nights for weeks, if panic comes in the dark, or if your mood keeps sinking, see a doctor. If you ever feel unsafe, call a crisis line, such as 988 in the US and Canada. That's the right help when it's more than a bad week.

What the sheet does is take one load off the pillow. Business sums get a time and a place, and the place is not your bed. The same move works by day; see small business owner stress for sorting every worry into a zone.

What deciding at night costs

Here is the same bad month decided two ways. It's an illustration from the book's example.

Decided at 2 a.m. Decided at 8 a.m. with a forecast
Hire a full-time barista to "plug the hole" One lever: price up 5%
Start hunting for a new supplier the same week Prices rise at every supplier, so the price moves instead
Two levers at once, no way to tell which worked One lever, its effect visible in weeks
Fixed costs rise while the reserve sits in yellow Margin back to 15% in the plan line

Now put money on it. Andrew's first forecast shows the scale. At a 9% margin, his reserve reached its two-month goal only at month 16. With the plan and a 15% margin, it got there at month 10. Six months of tired decisions keep you on the slow line.

A thin margin also makes every surprise dearer. At 9%, a breakdown or surprise bill of about a tenth of a month of reserve pushes the goal back by about a month. At 15%, it costs about half a month.

The human cost is simpler. You get up tired, you decide tired, and the next night the sum is worse.

Three weeks after his calm morning, Andrew noticed he couldn't remember the last time he had panicked.

"I can't switch my head off. Paper won't help."

It isn't meant to switch anything off. It gives the question an address. A thought with a time and a place stops knocking. A thought with nowhere to go keeps coming back.

The loudest enemy here is the habit of deciding by mood: management by panic at 3 a.m. and by relief at noon. The forecast doesn't care what time it is.

Where the morning forecast stops

The morning forecast is complete: you can use it tomorrow. But it treats a symptom. It moves the decision to a better hour. It doesn't tell you, in advance, which deviation is worth waking up for.

The cause sits in two missing pieces.

  • Zone thresholds and the red protocol (Chapter 4). Lines set on a calm day that say when a number turns from normal to emergency. Without them, step 5 is still a judgement call. See KPI zones.
  • The seven steps of entering control (Chapter 12). The sequence for a tangled bad month. It runs from calming the noise to a decision whose consequences you accept. Without it, a crisis still lands on you all at once. There's a short version in business triage.

The forecast is one part of a single loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. The night breaks when the loop has no forecast in it.

Key takeaways

  • At 3 a.m. your head steers by deviation, the worst rule in the croissant test.
  • Check for real danger first. If there is none, decide nothing tonight.
  • Park the question in one line, with the time you'll answer it.
  • In the morning: find the pattern, check the tolerance, draw two lines, pick one lever.
  • Fatigue corrupts a forecast like a faulty instrument.

Two choices for tonight

You have two choices. Lie there and run the sum a fifth time, steering your business by the hour of the night. Or stop guessing.

The morning forecast moves the sums to daylight. The zones in Chapter 4 tell you which number deserves an answer. The seven steps in Chapter 12 carry you through the month when everything breaks at once.

Tomorrow night, the question goes on the notepad, and the notepad waits for 8:00.

Questions people ask

How do I stop overthinking at night?

Give the thought a place. Check for real danger first. If there is none, write the question in one line with the time you'll answer it, and leave the sums for the morning. On a rested morning, look for the usual pattern before you react to yesterday.

Why do I get anxiety at night about work?

Many causes are possible, and a doctor is the right person to ask when it lasts. On the business side, night is when the head reacts to yesterday's jump with no pattern and no plan to hold it. A written forecast takes that load off the night.

Can stress cause insomnia?

Stress is one of the common reasons people sleep badly. If trouble sleeping lasts for weeks or affects your day, see a doctor. The model's part is narrower: it moves business decisions to a rested hour, the way Andrew moved his.
