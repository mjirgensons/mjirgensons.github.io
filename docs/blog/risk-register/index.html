Risk Register for a Small Business: Size Every Risk in Goal Units

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

A risk register is a table of what could knock you off course, with an owner and a response for each row. To make it steer, tie every risk to one goal, size it in that goal's units, mark its direction, and test whether a lever you control can cover it. If no lever can, the answer is a reserve or a different environment, not more effort.

A third of owners who shut down thought the business was doing fine when they closed it.

Fine is a feeling. It lasts until the coffee machine dies on a Friday. Then a barista quits by text, and the new price list lands the same morning.

Big companies keep a register with fifty rows and a colour chart that nobody opens after the board meeting. The small owner keeps his in his head. It has one row, and it says: something will come up.

Something always does. The question is whether you sized it before it came.

A list of worries steers nothing. The answer to this pain comes from the Jirgenson Model. Tie every risk to a goal, measure it in that goal's units, and check if a lever can cover it. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

What is a risk register?

A risk register is a table of the things that could go wrong. Each row names the risk, how likely it is, how much it would hurt, who owns it and what you will do. That is the plain risk register definition.

The common version scores likelihood and impact from one to five, multiplies the two and paints the cell. A breakdown gets "3 times 4 equals 12, amber". Then the sheet goes into a folder.

The trouble is the score. A 12 has no unit. You cannot subtract it from your cash reserve, or tell if it delays your goal by a week or a year.

Be honest about the limits first. The Jirgenson Model does not replace a risk register. Its own list of limits names one, with scenario, probability or range, time to impact, links between risks, measures and a person responsible. What the model adds is the link a plain register lacks: every row hangs on a goal, and every strength comes in that goal's units.

In the Jirgenson Model, a risk is an environmental factor you have spotted early: anything that acts on the path to a goal. Every factor has a direction and a strength. A row belongs on the register only when it names the goal it hits and its strength in that goal's units. Then you can set it against what your levers deliver.

So the risk register meaning shifts. It stops being a list of fears and becomes part of your control loop. The model behind it is open for anyone to check on GitHub.

Risk register template: eight columns

Open a sheet with eight columns. Fill one row per risk that acts on a goal you have written down.

  1. Risk. One event or condition, in plain words. "Coffee machine breaks down", not "operational risk".
  2. Goal it hits. One goal from your goal table, with its target. If a risk hits no goal on your list, it does not belong here yet.
  3. Direction. Against the goal, along it, or sideways. Yes, along. A register with only threats is half a register.
  4. Strength in goal units. Months of reserve, points of margin, hours a week, customers a day. Convert before you judge.
  5. Likelihood. A range or a plain word: any day, this season, unlikely this year. No false decimals.
  6. Can a lever cover it? Compare the strength with your strongest permitted lever. Yes, partly or no.
  7. Response. One of five: compensate with a lever, take it into account in the forecast, hold a reserve, use it, or change the environment.
  8. Owner and check. One name and one rhythm.

Column 6 is the heart of the template, and a likelihood score never asks it. When a lever can cover the risk, you plan the lever. When no lever can, effort is the wrong answer.

Risk register example: the coffee shop on the corner

Andrew runs a coffee shop on €30,000 of monthly revenue. At the start, his cash reserve stood at 0.5 months of expenses against a goal of 2.0. His margin was 9% against a goal of 15%. He worked 65 hours a week against a limit of 40.

Here is his register, with every strength taken from his own numbers:

Risk Goal it hits Direction Strength in goal units Lever covers it? Response Owner
Coffee machine breaks down Reserve, 2.0 months Against About 0.1 months per repair: at 9% margin the goal slips about a month, at 15% about half a month No lever stops it Hold a reserve; keep it growing Andrew, monthly
Supplier prices keep climbing Margin, 15% Against A trend of about 1% a month, felt at every supplier in the region Yes: price, up to 5% in one step Move the price with the market Andrew, weekly margin per receipt
Rain Margin, through waste Against, on rainy days About 15% fewer croissants sold Partly Take it into account: a rainy Saturday order of about 110, not 130 Sara, daily
Owner fatigue Owner's time, 40 hours Against 25 hours over the limit; late-night errors in figures and supplier calls Yes, slowly Hand the Friday shifts to Sara Andrew, weekly
Footfall falls 3% a month (illustration: a nearby office centre closes) Margin and reserve Against A year later revenue near €20,800; a €4,500 monthly profit turns into a loss of about €1,200 No: even prices at +5% fall short Change the environment: place, format or goal Andrew, quarterly
University building goes up nearby Regular customers, second location Along More customers coming Not needed Use it: build it into the second-location plan Andrew, quarterly

Read the sixth column. Two rows say no, and they need different answers. The breakdown needs money set aside. The footfall loss needs a different street. Extra hours fix neither. We post short cases like this one on Threads.

How to fill each row in fifteen minutes

Convert first, judge second

"The machine might break" sounds small. "The machine costs me a month of progress toward my reserve goal" does not. The model gives the conversion. At a 9% margin the shop adds about 0.1 months of reserve a month. A repair that eats 0.1 months therefore costs about a month of time. At 15% the same repair costs about half a month.

Same machine, same bill, half the damage, because the margin moved. So Andrew spends his effort on the margin, not on the machine.

Test the lever against the risk

Take the strongest lever you allow yourself. Andrew's plan permits a price rise of up to 5% in one step.

The supplier trend falls inside that limit, so the row reads "compensate". The footfall row does not. Even with prices pushed to the limit, the shop loses money every month. When the strongest factor points against the goal and outweighs every lever you have, tactics stop working. That is the moment to look at the street, not the menu.

Pick one of five responses

Every row ends in one verb. Compensate. Take into account. Reserve. Use. Change the environment. If you cannot pick one, the row is not finished.

No lever beats rain. So Andrew takes it into account: he orders from a forecast by day and weather. A rainy Saturday gets about 110 croissants, not 130.

Count your own shortages

Andrew's fatigue is a risk too: sixty-five hours a week, and figures mixed up late at night. A lack of time, knowledge or money acts on the path like rain. Give it a goal and an owner.

The cost of a register you keep in your head

Without the register, every surprise arrives as bad luck. And bad luck has a price you can count.

If nothing changes at a 9% margin, Andrew's reserve reaches two months at month 16. With his plan working, at month 10. That is six extra months with less than two months of cash. In those months, each breakdown pushes the goal back another month. Two repairs, and the goal slides to month 18.

Now the footfall row. Ignore it, and a €4,500 monthly profit becomes a €1,200 monthly loss within a year. The owner blames the staff, the menu, the prices. The cause sits on the street outside.

And the human cost, in one sentence: sixteen months of listening to the grinder before you look at the till.

Risk management for a small business: where the register stops

The register treats one symptom well: surprise. You see what can hit you, how hard, and whether you can answer it.

The cause of bad crisis decisions stays in place. A row tells you the strength of a risk. It does not tell you the exact reading at which you must act. And when two risks land in the same week, the register does not tell you which goal to save first.

Two steps close that gap.

  • Zones and the red protocol. Where normal ends and where you must act today. And the order of rescue when two goals go red at once. Chapter 4.
  • Recognising a factor before it strikes. Four techniques for seeing a risk early, and two forecasts in numbers: "if nothing changes" and "if the plan works". Chapter 8.

Without them, you know what can hit you. You still decide under pressure when to move and what to save. Both chapters come in the paperback as well as on Kindle.

Here is where the register sits in the whole system. Goals set the direction. The state says where you are, and the error says how far off. The register names the forces in between, and levers, a plan and a forecast turn them into action.

The likely objection is time. "I run a shop, not a bank." The register above has six rows, built from numbers Andrew already had. The factor exercise behind it takes fifteen minutes. The factor list behind it, with direction and strength for each push, is in external factors in business.

Key takeaways

  • A register earns its keep only when every row hangs on a written goal.
  • Size each risk in that goal's units: months of reserve, points of margin, hours.
  • Ask of every row: can a lever I control cover it?
  • Five responses: compensate, take into account, reserve, use, change the environment.
  • A score without a unit is a mood with a number on it.

Two choices before the next surprise

You have two choices. Keep the register in your head, call the next breakdown bad luck, and lose another month of progress each time. Or open a sheet tonight and give each risk a goal, a unit and a lever.

The register shows the forces. The zones, the red protocol and the forecasts tell you when to act and what to save first.

Questions people ask

What is a risk register?

A table of the events and conditions that could push you off course. Each row has a risk, its likelihood, its strength, an owner and a response. A useful one ties each row to one goal and measures strength in that goal's units.

What should a risk register template include?

Eight columns work for a small business. The risk, the goal it hits, its direction and its strength in goal units. Then its likelihood as a range, whether a lever can cover it, the response, and the owner with a check rhythm.

What is a good risk register example for a small business?

A coffee shop with six rows. A machine breakdown sized in months of cash reserve, supplier prices in points of margin, rain in croissants sold. Then owner fatigue in hours, a footfall decline, and a new building nearby that brings customers.
