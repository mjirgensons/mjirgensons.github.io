Scenario Planning for a Small Business: Three Futures, One Page

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

Scenario planning means preparing for several possible futures instead of betting on one forecast. For a small business: write three scenarios as actions, not moods. Give each the number you reach, the date you reach the goal and a rough probability. Then read the worst case. If your goal holds in every scenario, the decision is robust, and the exact odds stop mattering.

Ask yourself what next quarter looks like. You have one answer ready. That's the problem.

The future doesn't arrive as one line. It arrives as a pie cut into slices: the good version, the bad one, the one nobody raised at the meeting. Every slice has a size. Together they add up to one whole.

A plan built on one future is a bet you never agreed to make.

The usual answer to uncertainty is a five-year spreadsheet with figures to the cent. Precise to the decimal, and wrong by the summer.

The answer to a future you can't predict comes from the Jirgenson Model. Compare a few futures in numbers, check the worst one, and choose the move that holds in all of them. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

Scenario planning definition, in plain words

Scenario planning is a way of preparing for several plausible futures instead of betting on a single prediction. You describe a few distinct versions of what could happen. Then you test your decisions against each one.

That's what is scenario planning in one sentence. The trouble starts in practice. Scenarios turn into stories: "boom", "bust", "steady". Stories are pleasant to discuss and impossible to choose between on a Monday morning.

The model turns each scenario into an action with numbers.

In the Jirgenson Model, some future is bound to arrive, but it comes in variants, each with its own probability. To control is to enlarge the slice of the variant you chose as your goal and shrink the others. So you compare scenarios by three things: the chance of reaching the goal under each action, its cost and its risk of harm.

A scenario you can't act on is a weather report. A scenario tied to a lever is a decision waiting to be made. The model these rules belong to is open on GitHub.

Scenario planning in five steps, filled in

Here is the whole tool. Each step sits next to the coffee shop from the book, run by Andrew, on the morning after his worst month. His figures are a worked illustration.

The month: his supplier raised prices by about 8%, the coffee machine needed a repair, and a barista left. The margin fell from 15% to 12%. The reserve stood at 1.56 months of expenses, against a goal of 2.0.

He decided nothing that night. He went to bed and met Sara, who runs his Friday shifts, the next morning.

1. Name the goal and the horizon

One number, one date. Andrew's: a cash reserve of 2.0 months of expenses, looking ahead to month 12.

2. Write three scenarios as actions

Each scenario is something you do, not something you hope. Include "change nothing". It is always on the menu, and it is never free.

  1. Change nothing.
  2. Raise prices by 5%.
  3. Raise prices by 5% and hire a part-time barista.

3. Give each scenario a margin, a date and an end number

Scenario Margin Reserve hits 2.0 Three months in a row at 2.0 Reserve at month 12
1. Change nothing 12% month 12 month 14 2.11
2. Price +5% 15% month 11 month 13 2.27
3. Price +5% and part-time barista 13.5% month 11 month 13, higher quality 2.18

The "three months in a row" column matters. Touching a goal once is luck. Holding it is control.

4. Put a rough probability on each line

Andrew gave his own estimate: 0.3 for the first line, 0.4 for the second, 0.3 for the third. The slices add up to one.

Now the expected reserve at month 12: 0.3 × 2.11 + 0.4 × 2.27 + 0.3 × 2.18, about 2.2 months.

No probabilities at hand? Use ranges and plain words ("likely", "unlikely") instead. False precision is worse than an honest rough guess.

5. Read the worst case, then decide

The worst outcome is 2.11 months. That is still above the goal of 2.0, and far from the red line of one month. The goal holds in all three scenarios.

This is the point of the whole exercise. The decision is robust to an error in the probabilities. Andrew could be wrong about every one of his odds, and the choice would still stand. Robustness, not the exact probability, makes the forecast fit for action.

Andrew chose scenario 2. It reaches the goal sooner and ends highest. The part-time barista would add fixed costs while the reserve is still yellow. He wrote one condition next to the choice: if quality slips in two months, switch to scenario 3.

Three weeks later he noticed he couldn't remember the last time he had panicked. Andrew's shop runs through the whole book, on Kindle and in paperback.

The worst case scenario is a test, not a mood

The worst case scenario has a bad name. In a meeting it sounds like pessimism. In the tool above it is the cheapest safety check you'll ever run.

Ask one question of the worst line: does it cross a red line? If not, you can pick the best line with a calm head. If it does, the choice changes. Protecting that line comes first, and the scenario that keeps every number out of the red wins, even if it looks slower.

Then pull one lever at a time. Andrew started with the price alone, so he could see its effect. Other causes, such as the season, keep acting. So reading the result takes a comparison over several weeks.

A what if analysis on a pocket-money budget

The same tool works at the smallest scale. Alex is thirteen and saving for a €300 bicycle by 1 September. On 1 May he has €40.

What if he changes nothing and only collects his pocket money of €20 a month? By the autumn: €40 plus four times €20, so €120. He is €180 short.

What if he uses every lever: pocket money, odd jobs for the neighbours and selling his old scooter? €300, on time.

Nobody needs to push him. He sees in two numbers that doing nothing costs him the bicycle.

On 1 July he runs the what if again. He has €140. Add €50, the scooter's €60 and another €50: exactly €300, with nothing to spare. So he puts the scooter up for sale that day.

A what if analysis is the same move each time: one action, one number, one date. More short cases from the model appear on X.

What a one-line future costs

Now run the coffee shop on a single line, from the first month. At a 9% margin, with nothing changed, the reserve reaches two months at month 16: four months past the month 12 deadline. Around month 3, it still sits near 0.8 months, inside the red zone. With the margin plan, the goal arrives at month 10.

Six months of difference, and a one-line planner never sees it, because he never draws the second line.

The bad month shows the other cost. Say Andrew had decided that same night. He would most likely have hired a full-time barista to plug the hole. And he would have started hunting for a new supplier. Two levers at once. When the numbers moved, he could not have said which lever moved them.

The human cost is the night itself: decisions made at the hour you see least.

"I can't predict the future, so why model it?" is the fair objection. You don't need to predict it. You need a choice that survives being wrong. The worst case check gives you exactly that.

Where scenario planning stops

Be clear about what the table gives you. You compare futures, pick the move that holds in all of them and know your worst case. That treats the symptom: decisions made on one hopeful line.

The cause is still there. The table is a snapshot. It doesn't change how you steer between snapshots, so you still react to last month's figures instead of next month's. And when a scenario says the goal is out of reach, the table doesn't say whether to change the plan or the goal.

Two steps close that gap.

  • Control by forecast. Acting on the predicted state, not the recorded one, and letting new facts correct the forecast. Chapter 9.
  • Steady mode, manoeuvre and launch conditions. When an error means "change the plan" and when it means "change the goal". Plus the measurable conditions a big move waits for. Chapter 10.

The table is one link of a loop: goals in order, the real state, the gap, levers, plan, forecast. Scenarios live in the forecast; without levers they are only predictions.

Key takeaways

  • Write scenarios as actions, and always include "change nothing".
  • Give each one a margin, a date and an end number.
  • Put rough odds on them. Ranges are fine; false precision is not.
  • Read the worst case first. If the goal holds there, the choice is robust.
  • A plan built on one future is a bet you never agreed to make.

One line or three

You have two choices. Keep one forecast in your head and hope the year follows it. Or take twenty minutes tonight, write three scenarios as actions, and check the worst one.

The table picks a robust move today. Control by forecast and launch conditions keep you steering when the next month doesn't match any of the three lines.

Questions people ask

What is scenario planning?

A way of preparing for several plausible futures instead of betting on one prediction. In a small business, write three scenarios as actions and give each a result and a date. Then choose the move that still reaches the goal in the worst one.

What is a what if analysis?

A test of one change at a time: what happens to a key number if I do this? Each what if gets an action, a number and a date. Three of them side by side make a scenario table.

How do you plan for the worst case scenario?

Ask whether the worst line crosses a red line you set in advance. If it does not, choose freely. If it does, protect that line first and pick the scenario that keeps every number out of the red.
