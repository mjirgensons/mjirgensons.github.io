Second Order Thinking: Check the Whole Route Before You Move

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

Second order thinking asks what happens after the first result: and then what? The Jirgenson Model turns it into a route check. Before a big move, split it into three periods, check that every stretch still has levers and a reserve above the red line, and write the launch condition as a measurable event, not a date.

Judge a good idea by its first result, and you'll kill it.

Elena's first online book would have looked like a failure after one month. By month 14 it brought in about €600 a month, and it kept selling without her daily work.

Now picture your own Monday meeting. A number dips. One person says cut it. Another says double down. Both answer the same question: what happens next week?

Nobody asks the second one. And then what?

That's the irony of fast decisions. The meeting takes an hour. The second result lands months later, when nobody remembers who voted for it.

The answer to this pain comes from the Jirgenson Model. It turns "and then what?" from a mood into a route check you write down before any big move. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

What second order thinking is

First order thinking asks what a decision does right away. Second order thinking asks what that result causes next, and what that causes after it. You ask "and then what?" two or three times before you act.

The idea is old and sound. Its weakness is where it lives: in one person's head. It works on a calm Sunday. It vanishes on the two days you need it most, the great month and the terrible week.

The great month says: expand now. The terrible week says: tear up the plan. Both are first-order moves wearing a suit.

So the model does not ask you to think harder. It gives the second question a fixed place on paper. You can read the model in full on GitHub.

In the Jirgenson Model, a big move is a manoeuvre: the goal itself travels, and you pass from one steady mode into another. An error is a reason to change the plan. The goal changes only when conditions or priorities change.

First question: change the plan or the goal?

The first trap is a mix-up. A small miss gets treated as a reason to change the goal.

Alex is saving €300 for a bicycle by 1 September. On 1 July he has €140. His planned line says €170.

The first-order answer: settle for a cheaper bicycle. And then what? The next dip lowers the goal again. After three dips the goal is a pair of pedals.

Alex did the opposite. An error of €30 is a reason to change the plan, not the goal. He put his scooter up for sale at once, without waiting for August. On 1 August he had €250, €15 above the line.

Write that rule where your team can see it. A miss changes the plan. New conditions or new priorities change the goal. Nothing else does.

The route check: second order thinking on one page

Here is the tool. Use it before any move that changes where you are going: a new product, a second site, a career switch. It takes fifteen minutes and one sheet.

Elena's move was from client work to income from her own product. Her entries sit next to each step.

1. Name the move. What will be different for good when it is over? Elena: her income no longer rests on her client hours. It comes from client work, packages and the book.

2. Measure it in its own time. How long does the process take by its own laws, not by your wishes? Elena: months, not weeks. The move runs from month 1 to month 14.

3. Split it into three periods. Exit from the old mode, movement at a steady pace, entry into the new mode.

Period Months What happens
Exit 1 to 3 She writes the book, about 120 hours, and may work up to 55 hours a week
Steady movement 4 to 9 The book launches; visits grow to 1,400 a month; sales reach 21
Entry 10 to 14 Income comes from client work, packages and the book; her week falls to 35 hours

4. Check every stretch, not only the start. On each stretch, ask: which levers still work, and what reserve is left? This is "and then what?" asked once per period. Elena: in the exit period her reserve is time, and the 55-hour limit ends at month 3. In the steady period her lever is the book's page. She changes the headline and the order of the blocks, then watches the share of visitors who buy.

5. Draw the red line. Which number may never turn red anywhere on the route? Andrew, for his planned second coffee shop: the cash reserve never falls below 1.0 month of expenses.

6. Write the launch and stop conditions. A measurable event, not a date. Then the conditions to stop, sell or revise. Andrew: the second location starts only once the reserve holds at 2.0 months or more for three monthly measurements in a row.

That is the whole check. Six answers, each one a second question you would otherwise skip. Martin Jirgenson, who wrote the book, is on LinkedIn.

Second order thinking for team priorities and team goals

On a team, the second question has an owner problem. Everyone assumes someone else asked it.

Look at what happened in Andrew's coffee shop after he wrote the launch condition. Sara asked if they would now sit and wait. "No," Andrew replied. "We do whatever brings the condition closer."

One sentence turned a delay into a team goal. The same plan for margin and reserve now worked towards the second location. Nobody had to choose between today's job and tomorrow's dream.

Sara had asked a sharper second-order question back when the plans were new. Andrew's plans raised prices and also launched discount cards. "Don't those two contradict each other?" They did. Andrew added a rule: card discounts start only once the margin is at least 14%.

That is second order thinking as a team habit. One plan's result crashes into another plan's lever, and someone catches it on paper.

Two more rules keep team priorities steady:

  • Give each level its own rhythm. Levers every week. Gaps weekly for fast numbers, monthly for slow ones. Plans every quarter. Goals every six months to a year.
  • Reopen goals only at their own review. A dip on Tuesday is a plan question. It never puts the goal back on the table.

Why each number needs a rhythm that matches its own speed is in phase lag.

"This slows us down." It costs fifteen minutes. Andrew did not cancel his goal or freeze his team. Slow is signing a lease you can't carry.

The cost of a first-order move

Run Andrew's numbers. The second location needs about €45,000. At his expenses of €25,500 a month, that is 1.8 months of reserve.

When the idea first struck, his reserve stood at 1.35 months. That is not enough to pay for the opening at all. Had he signed the lease that week, the new rent would have eaten a reserve still short of two months. Within a month he would be back in the red zone. Six months of building, gone.

Wait until the end of year one, with a reserve of 2.3, and it still fails. Right after the opening only 0.5 months remain. Red in the first month.

Start from a reserve of 4.0, and the lowest point on the route is 2.05 months. That leaves 1.05 months above the red line: a little more than one month of expenses as room for a surprise.

Same shop. Same plan. The second result depends on one number on the day of the start.

Elena's version is quieter. Judge the book after one month, and she drops it. By month 14 that is about €600 a month gone, about €7,200 a year, from a source that works while she sleeps.

The human cost is plain too. The calm that six careful months bought would be gone, and the nights of sums before payroll would be back.

What the route check does not fix

Look at what you now hold. A move split into periods. A red line. A launch condition. You stop acting on the first result, and your team sees why.

That treats the symptom. The cause sits under the route itself. The check trusts its anchor points: your goals, your starting numbers, the market around you. If one of them is wrong, a perfect route still leads to the wrong place.

Two steps close that gap:

  • The three offsets. Three checks before work begins. Do two goals exclude each other? Have you misread where you start? Is the larger system already sinking? That last one is the Titanic test. Chapter 11.
  • The backward move. You walk back from the end result, one condition at a time. That walk sets the exact start of the move and this week's first action. Chapter 15.

The route check is one piece of the loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. It guards the move. It cannot vouch for the map. Both chapters are in print too, in the paperback.

Key takeaways

  • Second order thinking asks "and then what?" The model writes that question onto a sheet.
  • A miss changes the plan. Only new conditions or priorities change the goal.
  • Split any big move into exit, steady movement and entry. Check levers and reserve on every stretch.
  • Write the launch condition as an event. Andrew's: a reserve of 2.0 months, three months in a row.
  • The route check guards the move. The offsets and the backward move check the map and the start.

Two choices before the next big idea

You have two choices this week. Act on the first result, the way the great month and the bad Tuesday tell you to. Or spend fifteen minutes on one sheet and ask "and then what?" for every stretch of the route.

The sheet protects the move. The three offsets and the backward move make sure you are moving on the right map, at the right moment.

Next Monday your team opens the sheet. Everyone knows what starts the move and what stops it.

Questions people ask

What is second order thinking?

It is the habit of looking past the first result of a decision to the results that follow from it. You ask "and then what?" more than once before you act, instead of judging by what happens next week.

What is an example of second order thinking in business?

A coffee shop owner wants a second location after a good half year. The first-order view says the margin is fine, so open now. The second-order view asks what the opening costs do to the reserve. At 1.35 months of reserve, he could not even pay for the opening.

How do you set team priorities when goals compete?

Put the goals in order, so everyone knows which one gives way first. Tie big moves to a written launch condition, not a date. Then give each level its own review rhythm, so a bad Tuesday changes the plan and never the goal.
