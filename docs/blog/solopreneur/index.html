Solopreneur Dashboard: Six Rows You Can Read in One Minute

Martin Jirgenson6 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

A solopreneur needs a system that lives outside their own head. Build it from simple parts: a dashboard of three goals and three leading numbers, one data source for each, a zone for every row and a fixed weekly rhythm. Elena, a freelance designer, used bank statements, time records, a payment system and a website counter. Her page reads in a minute.

Working alone? Three out of four solo businesses are gone within eight years.

It didn't have to be that many. Behind each of them is someone who did the selling, the work, the invoices and the worrying alone. Some of them might still be running if their numbers had lived on a page instead of in a tired head.

Picture Monday at eight. You are the director, the accountant and the whole sales team. Three tabs, two apps and a notebook. Someone asks how business is going. You say "busy", because you can't say more.

Here is the irony. A solo business has no meetings, no reports and nobody to argue with about the figures. And still nobody can tell you if this month is fine.

There is a control system that works for one person as well as for a company: the Jirgenson Model. It is scientifically proven to work (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934). It is new, so it arrived too late for the owners behind that number. It can work for you.

The solopreneur dashboard: build it in six steps

A management system isn't software. It is a few plain parts you can hold: a table, a source, a dashboard, a list. Until the part exists, the idea doesn't work. Running a business alone, you remember your priorities. In a hard week, memory is the first thing to go.

So build the first part this week. Here are the six steps, with Elena's entries. She is a freelance designer, and she is writing her first online book.

1. Choose the rows. Three goals and three leading numbers. Elena's goals: income as a three-month average, the share of her largest client, her hours per week. Her leading numbers: visitors to her book's page, the share of them who buy, newsletter subscribers.

2. Give every row one source. One place the number comes from. Elena: bank statements for income and the client share. Her time records for hours. The payment system for sales. The website counter for visits. Two sources for one number start an argument. Alone, that argument is with yourself at midnight.

3. Keep the measuring small. One table is enough. Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, used one shared spreadsheet and a weekly export from his till. That was all. No expensive programs.

4. Fill in "now" honestly. Use records, not your proudest month. Elena's bank statements for six months swung from €800 to €2,200. The average: €1,300. Her best client brings €910 of it, which is 70%. Her time records show 50 hours a week. Her book isn't out yet, so its three rows start with targets and a source, and no reading.

5. Set a zone for each row. Draw the lines in a calm moment. Elena's client share: normal up to 33%, acceptable above 33 and up to 50, emergency above 50. At 70%, that row is red.

6. Set the rhythm. A short weekly look, a monthly one, a quarterly one. Measure a fast row often and a slow row less. Andrew counts complaints weekly and his cash reserve monthly.

Here is Elena's page at the start:

Row Goal Now Error Source
Income, 3-month average €3,000 by month 14 €1,300 minus €1,700 Bank statements
Largest client's share 30% or less 70% plus 40 points Bank statements
Hours per week 35 or fewer (55 allowed in months 1 to 3) 50 plus 15 Time records
Book page visitors a month 1,400 by month 9 not launched to measure Website counter

Newsletter subscribers first 100 by month 6 not launched to measure Mailing tool

That is the tool. Six rows on one screen. It reads in a minute, and the fire shows at once: row two. A trend column comes after two or three readings, because one point shows no direction.

How to stay out of that number: keep the rows on the page, not in your head. When the week gets hard, the page still remembers.

In the Jirgenson Model, every element of the model becomes a tool you can hold. A goal table, one source per number, a traffic-light dashboard, a list of levers. Then a one-page plan per goal, a forecast and a meeting calendar. Until the tool exists, the model is only reasoning.

Self funding business: give your savings a row and a reading day

Nearly two out of three businesses start on the owner's own savings.

Savings don't have to drain away unseen. With a control system, some of those owners might have watched the money as a number with a red line. Not as a balance they were afraid to open.

So put the reserve on the dashboard. Count it in months of expenses, and take it from the bank statement. Andrew's goal was 2.0 months. His reading was 0.5.

Then pick how often to read it. The rule: read a row often enough that it can't cross its yellow band unseen between two readings. Andrew's yellow band for the reserve runs from 1.0 to 2.0 months. In a bad month the reserve moves about 0.2. So a reading every two and a half months would still catch it. Monthly is plenty.

In a cash crisis the band shrinks to a few days of expenses. Then you read the balance every day.

How to avoid the slow drain: set the reading day now. A balance you check only when you're scared is a mood, not a measurement.

How to pay yourself: owner pay belongs on row one

Half of all owners have gone months without paying themselves.

Those months didn't have to pass in the dark. Some of those owners might have seen the gap early, if their own pay had sat on the first row of a page.

For Elena, income is her pay, so it holds row one. She measures it as a three-month average, because one good month proves nothing. The source is the bank statement, not her memory of a big order.

Put the largest client's share right under it. At 70%, Elena's pay hangs on one client's next email.

Now look at where her row ends up later in the story. By month 14 it reads €3,000: €900 from the main client, €1,200 from service packages, €300 from repeat orders and about €600 from her book. The main client's share is down to 30%. Her week is back to 35 hours.

Do this: write your pay as row one, with a number, a unit, a three-month average and a deadline. Then the client share. Then your hours.

How to avoid the unpaid months: read your pay row first, every week. An empty month shows up that month, not at year end.

What six more months without a page cost

Put Elena's page into money. Her largest client brings €910 of her €1,300. If that client leaves, €390 a month remains. The dashboard shows that risk in red today. A head full of numbers shows it on the day the email arrives.

Her hours cost too. Fifty a week against a goal of 35 is fifteen extra hours a week, about sixty a month.

Andrew's coffee shop shows the other path. Without the tools, everything he had worked out would have stayed in his head and on napkins. In the first tense month the order of his goals would have slipped away. The zones would have blurred. Fatigue would have made the decisions again.

That is the human cost of running a business alone without a page. The tired evening decides, and you only find out later.

"Alone, I don't need a system"

For one person the system is smaller, not absent. Drawing a five-row dashboard takes about twenty minutes. Without software, nine rows is the ceiling. Elena uses six.

Andrew's Monday briefing fell from an hour to fifteen minutes, because the figures settled every argument. Alone, you get the same gift: no more debating with yourself at night.

And your work may look nothing like a coffee shop. An online shop tracks orders and ad spend. A workshop tracks workload and deadlines. An insurance agent tracks the client funnel and renewals. The six steps stay the same.

Where the dashboard stops

Be clear about what you built. The dashboard holds the first links of the loop: goal, state and error. Levers, plan and forecast come after. It treats a symptom: you stop guessing where you stand.

Run the completeness test before you trust it. Nine categories, from the goal list to the control mode, each with at least one line. If one is empty, you don't yet have stable control.

The cause can still win. Row two is red, and the page doesn't say what to do about it.

Two steps close that gap.

  • The list of levers. What you can change with your own hands when a row turns red, and what only looks like a lever. Chapter 5.
  • Two forecasts. Where each row goes if nothing changes, and where it goes if the plan works. Chapter 8.

Without them, the dashboard shows the fire. It doesn't yet hand you the hose.

Key takeaways

  • Alone, your head is the first thing a hard week breaks. Put the numbers on a page.
  • Six steps: rows, one source each, small measuring, honest "now", zones, rhythm.
  • Put your own pay on row one, as a three-month average.
  • Give your savings a row and a reading day.
  • If a number lives only in your head, it isn't managed.

Two choices before next Monday

You have two choices. Keep the business in your head, answer "busy", and hope the hard week never comes. Or draw six rows tonight, with one source each, and read them every Monday.

Next Monday you open one page and know where you stand within a minute.

Questions people ask

What tools does a solopreneur need?

Seven plain tools. A goal table, one source per number, a traffic-light dashboard and a list of levers. Then a one-page plan per goal, a forecast and a meeting calendar. Start with the first three. Elena's six-row page runs on four plain sources.

How do I track my business as a solo entrepreneur?

Pick three goals and three leading numbers. Give each one source. Fill in today's value from records, set a red line for each row, and read the page on a fixed day every week.

How much should a solopreneur pay themselves?

Decide what your life costs, then make that row one: a number, a three-month average and a deadline. Elena's row said €3,000 a month by month 14, against €1,300 at the start.
