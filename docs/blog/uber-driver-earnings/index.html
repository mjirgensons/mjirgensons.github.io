Uber Driver Earnings: What the Hours Cost Your Family

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer

Your real Uber driver earnings are what stays after every cost of driving, divided by every hour the work takes, waiting included. Take the bank statement as the source, not the app. Write a definition for each number. Count the car's wear, which sends no bill until it does. Then put the result beside your goals, including your hours at home.

Your daughter blew out the candles at 7:40. You watched it on a video, parked outside a closed mall, waiting for the next ping.

The app says it was a good week. Big numbers, a badge for hours online. It doesn't say what the week cost.

Here is the irony. The app counts every ride to the cent. Nobody counts the evenings you weren't home.

You can work. Sixty hours, more if the week needs it. What breaks you is carrying every number in your head: fuel, tyres, the car loan, the school run, the rent. No system holds any of it, so your head holds all of it.

The Jirgenson Model gives the answer as a control system. One real number per goal, from a source you trust. Your family's hours on the same sheet. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

Uber driver earnings: the app shows revenue, not pay

The app shows what came in. Your pay is what stayed, divided by the hours it took.

Andrew, who runs a coffee shop, found that gap in his own books. He was sure his margin held at around twenty per cent. When he measured it, it was 9%. He had never counted waste: sour milk, pastries written off at the end of the shift. The costs were real. They never showed up in any report.

Your fuel, your phone and the car's slow wear are his pastries.

The side hustle income post measures the six-month average. This one goes a level down, to each hour behind the wheel.

In the Jirgenson Model, the state is the measured value of each goal, from one named source and one written definition. The full state is wider than the readings. It also holds obligations, dependencies and the wear on the equipment.

Real margin per hour: the sheet, filled in

This is the tool. One sheet, one evening, six steps, each with the coffee shop's entry beside it. Your entries are illustrations.

1. Define what counts as an hour

Andrew and Sara once argued over complaints. Sara's notebook held only "the ones I remembered". A customer who said nothing and left was never counted. So they wrote a definition beside the number: a complaint is any customer who says out loud they're unhappy, or sends the order back.

Do the same for your hours. Your definition, as an illustration: from leaving home to coming home. Waiting in a car park counts. Driving to the busy zone counts. Cleaning the car counts.

2. Define earnings and give them one source

Andrew's entry: margin is operating profit as a percentage of revenue, from the accounts that the till feeds.

Your entry: money in the bank from the app, minus every cost of driving. The source is the bank statement. The app screen may only cross-check it. One parameter, one source, or the argument over which figure is right eats more energy than the work.

3. Count the costs that never become a payment

The car loses value every kilometre and sends no bill until the day it does. Write down the delivery driver expenses as they happen: fuel, insurance, the phone, cleaning, tyres, repairs.

Andrew's missing row was waste. Once he counted it, twenty became 9. A short log like the one in how to track write-offs catches each cost on the day it happens.

4. Look behind the panel

Two coffee shops with the same five readings can face two different futures. One lease ends next year. One machine is worn out.

Two drivers with the same weekly figure differ the same way. One has a paid-off car. The other has a loan and worn tyres. Add two rows: months left on the car loan, and the date of the next big repair.

One more row protects the rest. A number changes how people behave. Track only rides per hour, and the oil change starts to slip. So add a protective row: service done on time, yes or no.

5. Divide by the hours

Now take the month's real profit and divide it by the month's hours. Here is Andrew's version, as plain arithmetic on the book's illustrative numbers, before tax and his own drawings. His revenue was €30,000 a month. He worked 65 hours a week, about 280 a month.

What he believed What he measured
Margin about 20% 9%
Profit a month €6,000 €2,700
Hours a month about 280 about 280
Profit per hour about €21 under €10

The same hours. Less than half the worth. Your own row is the one the app never shows.

6. Put the family row on the sheet

A business list covers six areas: finance, clients, sales, processes, people, and the owner. That last area holds your own time, health and money. It is the one people forget.

Andrew's row: hours a week, goal 40 or fewer, now 65. When he ran the refusal test, he put his own time in last place and surprised himself. He had known for ages he was tired. He had never said out loud that he would give up rest for the business. Now it was on paper.

Your row, as an illustration: home by 7 p.m. four nights a week. A number you can check, not a wish.

That is the whole tool. Andrew's first sheet took him two hours.

Low profit margin per hour: what six more months cost

Look at Andrew's money first. At 15% his shop brings in €4,500 a month. At his real 9%, €2,700. That is €1,800 short, every month, or €10,800 over six months.

The hours cost more. 65 a week against a goal of 40 is 25 extra hours, every week. Over six months, that is about 650 hours away from home.

And Andrew nearly paid in full. The week he measured, he had planned a second barista and an ad campaign. The plan rested on a margin that didn't exist.

The human cost fits in one sentence. Six more months of birthdays watched on a phone in a car park.

If the tiredness hasn't lifted for weeks, or the signs of exhaustion follow you into your sleep, see a doctor. When it feels like more than a bad week, a crisis line is the right help too. The sheet treats the chaos in the work, not an illness. The post on business owner burnout covers the business side of that weight.

Working too much, or working blind?

The app pays you for the ride. You pay for the hour.

Here is the contrast, as an illustration. One driver, two Sundays.

The guessing Sunday: "Good week. Roughly enough, I think. Maybe one more Saturday." No source, no costs, no hours.

The measured Sunday: "Real profit from the bank, every cost counted, divided by every hour. Under my line for the third week, and home by seven only twice." One look, and you know what to change.

This is one piece of a loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. The sheet is the state. Without it, every later step steers by feel. The model's canon is public and free on GitHub.

"I'm a driver, not a business." The same six steps work for a coffee shop, a freelancer and a family budget. A one person business is still a business, and you are its only machine. If the driving is extra money on top of a job, the extra income post shows how to write what it is for.

Always working: what the sheet does not fix

The sheet tells you where you stand. It treats a symptom: you stop guessing your pay.

The cause of always working stays in place. You still don't know the exact point where your hourly figure stops being a warning and becomes an emergency. And you don't yet know which of your numbers you can change, and which belong to the app.

Two steps close that gap:

  • Error and the three zones. Where your per-hour figure and your home hours turn yellow and red, set on a calm evening. Chapter 4.
  • Levers. What you control and what you don't, and three questions to put to each lever before you pull it. Chapter 5.

Without them, the sheet tells you the truth. It doesn't tell you when to act, or what to pull. The traffic-light zones post shows the gauge, and business levers shows the list.

Key takeaways

  • The app shows revenue. The bank shows what stayed.
  • Define an hour from leaving home to coming home.
  • Count the car's wear before it sends the bill.
  • Divide real profit by real hours. That is your pay.
  • Put your hours at home on the same sheet as the money.

Questions people ask

How do I calculate my Uber driver earnings per hour?

Take the money in from the bank, not the app. Subtract every cost of driving: fuel, insurance, the phone, cleaning, tyres and repairs. Divide by every hour from leaving home to coming home, waiting included. That figure is your pay.

What are delivery driver expenses?

Every cost of earning the fare: fuel, insurance, the phone, cleaning, tyres, repairs and the car's wear. Log them on the day they happen. The wear is the cost people miss, because it sends no bill until a repair does.

How do I stop working too much?

Put a number on it. Write your hours at home as a goal, with a target and a date, beside the money. Then run the refusal test on your list. The book's Chapter 1 shows the test, and Chapter 4 sets the line where the hours turn red.
