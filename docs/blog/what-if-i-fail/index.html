What If I Fail? Find the Edge Before You Take the Leap

Martin Jirgenson15 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

What if I fail has no answer until the failure has a size. Before the leap, write the end state, the edge you never cross, what the leap costs and how much room it leaves. Write the start as an event, not a date, and the stop line now. Then walk back from the goal to this week. The fear shrinks to a known amount.

Half of all adults say the fear of failing would stop them from starting.

It's late. The lease is in a drawer, unsigned. Or the resignation letter. Your partner asked one question at dinner: "And what if it doesn't work?" You laughed it off. Now it's 1 a.m., and the question plays on a loop.

You have no answer. Only a feeling, and it says: everything. The house, the savings, the kids' plans.

You can work. That was never the doubt. What crushes you is a leap with no edge, carried in your head, with no system to hold it.

The answer comes from the Jirgenson Model: a control system that puts a size on the risk before you jump. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

What if I fail is a question with no edge

A fear with no boundary is infinite. "What if I fail?" grows every night because nobody has said how much failing would cost, or when you'd stop.

So the fix isn't courage. It's geometry. Draw the edge, then plan back from the goal to today.

The backward move works like a navigation app. It knows where you are and where you're going. Miss a turn, and it plots a fresh route from where you stand.

In the Jirgenson Model, the backward move starts from the end result. At each step it asks: what must be true immediately before this? A chain of conditions comes out, and its first row is what you do now. It needs a precise end, an honest start and a check of resources at every step.

That last part matters. A route built from the wrong starting point leads you to the wrong place with perfect precision. Never mistake an open window on a high floor for an exit.

Find the edge first: the leap sheet, filled in

This is the whole tool. One sheet, about twenty minutes. The filled-in entries come from Andrew's coffee shop, at the moment he wanted a second location.

1. Write the end state, with numbers

Andrew wrote: in 18 months, at month 30, the second location is open. The first one still holds a margin of at least 14%, including a manager's cost. The reserve is at least two months of expenses. He works no more than 45 hours. The new shop reaches its 15% margin within six months of opening.

2. Draw the edge you never cross

Pick the one number that must stay out of the red for the whole leap.

For Andrew it was the cash reserve. Never below 1.0 month, from the first day of the leap to the last. Below a month, there's no time left to negotiate, borrow or decide. The full logic of that line is in cash reserve in months of expenses.

3. Price the leap in your own units

Turn the cost into the same unit as the edge.

The second location needs about €45,000. Andrew's monthly expenses are €25,500. So the leap costs about 1.8 months of reserve in one go. After the opening, the new shop loses a little more for three months: 0.3, then 0.2, then 0.1 months.

4. Test your starting point against the edge

This step answers "what if I fail" with a number.

Reserve at the start Right after the opening Verdict
1.35 months Not enough for the opening itself Don't jump
2.3 months 0.5 months Red in the very first month
4.0 months 2.2, lowest point 2.05 Room to spare

At 4.0, the lowest point stays 1.05 months above the edge. That's a little more than one month of expenses. The leap can absorb a surprise of that size and still hold the line. That's the most the leap can lose before it breaks you. Not "everything". A known amount.

5. Write the start as an event, not a date

A date is a wish. An event is a condition you can check.

Andrew didn't cancel the dream. He wrote: the leap starts once the reserve holds at 2.0 or more for three months in a row. His senior barista asked if they'd wait. "No," he said. "We do whatever brings the condition closer."

6. Write the stop line and the check date now

Decide on a calm day what makes you revise, and what makes you stop.

The model's rule for the whole leap: if the reserve leaves the corridor and heads for the edge, there are two moves only. A controlled return, or a halt. And the new shop gets judged on margin at six months after opening, not before. Any earlier is too soon to tell.

Set the check in the process's own time, not your nerves' time. Elena, a freelance designer, moved from client work to her own book over fourteen months. Had she demanded a result in month one, she'd have decided the book didn't work.

7. Walk back from the end to this week

Now the chain. Start at month 30 and ask what must be true before each step.

When What must be true
Month 30 Second location opens
Month 29 Test week
Month 27 Fit-out complete
Month 24 Equipment bought; second team trained
Month 21 Manager for the first shop hired and trained
Month 18 Premises found; lease with a demand-test clause
Month 12, today Reserve at 2.0 or more; one more month needed

The first row is the answer to "what do I do now?" Hold the reserve at two months. That's all this week asks. A blank version of this chain is in backward planning template.

Afraid of failing? Give the failure a size

Run Elena's leap the same way. Her end state: €3,000 a month at month 14, from clients, packages and her book.

Walk back. Month 11: 21 book sales a month. Month 9: 1,400 page visits at a 1.5% conversion. Month 6: the first hundred newsletter subscribers. Month 4: launch. Month 3: manuscript ready. Month 1: outline drawn and price set.

Her edge was her own time. During the writing months she allowed herself up to 55 hours a week, and no more. Her first action this week wasn't "become an author". It was the outline and the price.

A dream asks for courage every night. A plan asks for one task on Tuesday. The longer version of the method is in backward planning.

Fear of losing everything is a missing edge

"Everything" is what the mind says when nobody has drawn a line. Write the edge, and "everything" becomes a figure with a stop rule attached.

Take that figure home. Tell your partner the three lines: "We start when the reserve has held for three months. We never go below one month. If we head for it, we stop or pull back." That's a family deal, not a gamble. If a personal guarantee is part of the leap, put the house on the sheet too, as in personal guarantee.

One plain line on health. The model treats chaos in the business, not an illness. If the worry keeps you awake for weeks or turns into panic, that is more than a bad week. Talk to your doctor. In a crisis, in the US or Canada, call or text 988.

What six more months of "what if" cost

Two ways to lose, and both cost money.

Leap blind, and the numbers turn on you fast. Suppose Andrew had signed the lease at 1.35 months of reserve. Within a month the new rent would have pushed his reserve back into the red. Six months of building, gone. At 2.3 months, the opening leaves 0.5: half a month of expenses as cushion.

Freeze, and the cost is slower. Andrew's longer goal was ten shops in five years. Walking back showed it plainly: unless he freed his hands for writing procedures today, the chance was close to zero. The mistake shows up today, not in year five.

And the human cost: six more months of a drawer you don't open and a question you can't answer at dinner. The runway side of the same sum is in months of runway.

Where the edge stops

The sheet treats the symptom. The fear gets a size, a start rule and a stop line.

The deeper cause can still sink you. Is the end state possible at all, or do two of its goals fight each other? Is your start figure true? Is the market you're leaping into growing or dying? A flawless route inside a doomed market still loses. And once the leap starts, you need to steer it month by month inside the corridor, not only at the start.

Two steps close that gap:

  • The three offsets. A contradictory goal, a wrong starting point, an enclosing system that's dying, and how to check each by calculation. Chapter 11.
  • Steady mode and manoeuvre. The tolerance corridor along the whole route, its checkpoints, and when to change the plan versus the goal. Chapter 10.

Without them, the edge keeps you from falling. It doesn't yet tell you if the ground ahead holds. A first test of the ground is in when to give up on a business idea.

"Planning kills the courage." The opposite. Andrew's goal never died; it got a condition. The leap sheet is the plan step of one loop: goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast. Fear has no size until you give it an edge.

Key takeaways

  • "What if I fail?" has no answer until the failure has a size.
  • Draw the edge first: the one number you never let go red during the leap.
  • Price the leap in the same unit, and test your starting point against the edge.
  • Start on an event, not a date. Write the stop line on a calm day.
  • Walk back from the goal; the first row is this week's task.

Two choices before you sign

You have two choices. Keep the leap in a drawer and the question on a loop, or jump on a good night with no edge at all. Or take twenty minutes and draw the edge, the start rule and the stop line on one sheet.

The sheet makes the leap safe to start. The three offsets and the corridor keep it safe on the way.

Questions people ask

What if I fail at my business?

Then you fail by a known amount, if you drew the edge first. Write the line you never cross, price the leap against it, and set a stop rule on a calm day. A failure you sized in advance is a setback. One you didn't is the fear you feel tonight.

How do I overcome fear of failure before a big leap?

Give the fear a size. Find the most the leap can lose before it breaks your edge. Start only when a measurable condition is met. Walk back from the goal to one task this week. For the starting decision itself, see [fear of failure in business](/blog/fear-of-failure-business/).

Is it normal to be scared of failing?

Yes. The fear is a signal that the risk has no edge yet. Draw it, and the signal gets quieter. If the fear keeps you awake for weeks or brings panic, talk to a doctor.
