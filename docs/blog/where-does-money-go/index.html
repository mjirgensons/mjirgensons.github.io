Your partner asks one quiet question at dinner, and you can't answer it.

"Where did the money go this month?"

You worked every day. The till was busy. And the account is thinner than last month, and you can't say why.

Half of all owners have gone months without paying themselves.

Later that night you sit at the kitchen table with the bank app open. You run the same sums for the third time. The phone lights your face while the house sleeps.

Here's the uncomfortable part. You can work. You prove it every day. What you can't do is carry every number in your head, with no system, and still know where the money went.

That isn't a character flaw. It's a missing instrument.

The Jirgenson Model gives you the answer as a control system: goals in order, measured numbers, zones and levers on one sheet. It is scientifically proven that the Jirgenson Model works (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934).

Where does money go? Three liars hide it

Money rarely vanishes through one big hole. It leaks through three liars inside your own numbers.

  1. Memory. The head keeps the busy Saturdays and drops the empty Tuesdays. So "good on the whole" looks better than the month was.
  2. Rounding. "About twenty" feels kinder than "nine and a half". So the kind figure wins.
  3. What goes unrecorded. A cost nobody writes down never reaches the report. The bank still takes it.

Andrew's coffee shop had all three. A queue formed at the door every morning. By the end of each month the money had somehow vanished.

He was sure his margin held at about twenty per cent. When he measured it, the number was 9.

The gap was waste. Sour milk. Pastries thrown out at the end of the shift. Every write-off ate the margin and never reached a report, because nobody counted it. If that sounds like your shop, here is how to track write-offs before they eat the month.

In the Jirgenson Model, your position is the state vector. Each goal gets its measured value beside it, in the same order. Each row needs a definition, a main source and a named counter. A figure without all three is not a number. It's an impression.

The leak check: one evening, one sheet

You need a sheet of paper or one spreadsheet tab. Add your bank statements for the last three months and, if you run a business, last month's accounts. Plan on one evening. Six steps follow, each with a real entry.

1. Write the money rows

Five rows at most. A business starts with the cash reserve and the margin. A family starts with income, spending and savings.

Andrew wrote two money rows: reserve and margin. Emma and Paul, a couple with a family budget, wrote three: income, spending, savings.

2. Give each row one definition

Write what goes in and what stays out. Without a definition, the number depends on who is counting.

Andrew's margin: operating profit as a share of revenue, waste included. That one phrase, "waste included", moved his number from twenty to nine. The arithmetic behind it is in how to calculate profit margin.

3. Give each row one source and one name

One primary source per row. Other sources only cross-check. If the accounts count revenue one way and the till another, the fight over the right figure eats more energy than the business.

Row Andrew's source Emma and Paul's source
Cash Bank statement, counted by Andrew Bank statements, savings account
Margin or spending Accounting system fed by the till export Three months of bank statements
Income Till software Bank statements, both salaries

4. Ask the three questions

Put three questions to each figure. Who did the counting? By what method? Covering which weeks? A missing answer turns the figure into an impression.

Andrew's "twenty" failed on method. The waste was never in the count.

Elena, a freelance designer, failed the third. A big order made a month feel good. Her bank statements told the story: income swung between €800 and €2,200 over six months. The average was €1,300. One good month does not make an average.

5. Hunt the unrecorded cost

Ask one more question: what leaves the account and never reaches a report?

For Andrew it was waste. For Emma and Paul it was drift. Their income is €4,000 a month. Over one quarter, their spending rose 10%, to €4,400. Nobody decided that. Subscriptions and eating out crept up, and every month they ended €400 short.

6. Write the value, or a date

Fill in each row as a number. Where there is no number, write the source and the date you'll have it.

An empty source is not a failure. It's the first hole you've found, and now it has a date.

Money anxiety often hides a missing number

Worrying about money feels like a mood. Often it's a number you have never measured. Your head keeps the account open all night because no sheet holds it.

Two enemies sit here: panic ("all is lost") and bravado ("we'll pull through"). Both put feelings in place of facts.

When Andrew saw the 9, his first impulse was to close the spreadsheet and go make coffee. Then he remembered the one rule that matters here. It was an instrument reading, not a verdict. An instrument doesn't scold. It tells you where you are.

For the first time in two years, Andrew saw his coffee shop as a whole.

A sheet helps with the chaos in the numbers. It does not treat an illness. If worry about money keeps you awake for weeks, or your mood keeps sinking, see a doctor. If you ever feel unsafe, call a crisis line, such as 988 in the US and Canada.

Low profit margin: what one uncounted cost does

Here is what guessing would have cost Andrew. That same week he meant to take on another barista and pay for adverts. The whole calculation rested on a margin that did not exist. Within a few months the coffee shop would have run out of money, and nobody would have understood why.

Put his real numbers into money. Revenue is €30,000 a month and the margin is 9%, so expenses run at €27,300. The reserve of 0.5 months is €13,650. The goal of two months is €54,600. The shortfall is €40,950.

At 9%, one month adds €2,700 to the reserve. That's about a tenth of a month. If nothing changes, the reserve reaches its goal only at month 16. With the margin raised to 15%, it gets there at month 10. Six months of guessing is six months on the slow line. Here's why months of expenses is the unit to measure cash in.

Emma and Paul face the same arithmetic at home. Savings of €2,000 and a gap of €400 a month: if nothing changes, the savings are gone in five months. Sooner, if spending keeps rising.

The human cost fits in one sentence. Every one of those months ends with two tired people at the kitchen table, each sure the other one spent it.

The usual evening against the leak check

You can quote the price of every drink on your menu. You can't tell your partner where last month's money went. The villain isn't you. It's the number nobody counts.

Here is the same evening run two ways. This is an illustration.

The usual evening The leak check
Open the bank app and scroll Open three months of statements, one row at a time
"Margin is about twenty" "Margin is 9%, waste included"
Argue about who spent it One source, one name, nothing to argue about
Close the laptop at 1 a.m. with no answer Finish with a number or a date in every row

Emma and Paul quarrelled at first. Then they took their state from the bank statements, and the argument turned into a table. Their next step is in household budget in deficit. And if two of you share one account, a couples budget puts both names on the same page.

"I have no time for a measuring system"

You need one sheet, not a system. The first version takes an evening, on paper. A perfect system in six months is worse than a rough table today.

The model's rules are open to anyone: the Jirgenson Model on GitHub. Short videos on the same idea are on the Steer, Don't Guess YouTube channel.

Where the leak check stops

The leak check answers "where does money go", and you can run it tonight. But it treats a symptom. A reading tells you where you are. It doesn't tell you when a row is bad enough to act, or what to pull when it is. The cause sits deeper: no zones, and no list of levers.

Two steps fix that for good.

  • Zone thresholds and the red protocol (Chapter 4). They decide, in advance, when a number turns from normal to emergency. Without them, the reading never rings an alarm before the account runs dry. Here's a short look at KPI zones.
  • The list of levers (Chapter 5). It separates what you can change from rent, weather and moods. Without it, you'll know where the money went and still pull the wrong thing. See business levers.

The reading is one step of a single loop: goal, state, gap, levers, plan, forecast. A full shop with an empty account, the kind in busy but not profitable, breaks at the second step.

Key takeaways

  • Money leaks through three liars: memory, rounding and what goes unrecorded.
  • Every money row needs one definition, one source and one name.
  • Ask each figure: who counted it, how, and over what period?
  • "Source undefined" is a result. It shows you the hole.
  • An instrument reading, not a verdict.

Questions people ask

Where does my money go every month?

Into the costs nobody counts and the numbers you round. Take three months of bank statements and give each money row one source. Then ask who counted each figure, how, and over what period. The leak shows up as a gap between what you believed and what the statement says.

How do I stop worrying about money?

Put the number on paper, so your head doesn't hold it all night. Measure your cash, spending and income from one source each. A measured bad row is easier to carry than an unmeasured fear. If the worry lasts for weeks or affects your sleep and mood, talk to a doctor.

How to pay bills when money runs short?

First check for danger: mandatory payments come before anything else. Then decide the order of your goals once, on a calm day: what you'd give up last gets number one. The book's Red Card shows the full sequence for the worst day.
