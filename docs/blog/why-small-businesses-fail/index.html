Why Small Businesses Fail: Two Forecasts That Show It Coming

Martin Jirgenson6 Nov 2026 · 10 min read
Short answer

One in five new businesses doesn't even live to see its first birthday.

It didn't have to be that number. Behind it are people who opened early, paid their suppliers and believed next month would turn. Some of them might still be open if they had seen the trouble three months ahead, while there was time to steer.

Picture your own first year. The till says the week went well. You know last month to the euro. Ask where the cash will be in March, and you get a guess.

Here is the irony. The rear-view mirror gets a monthly report, a bookkeeper and a meeting. The windscreen gets a hunch.

There is a control system built for that windscreen: the Jirgenson Model. It is scientifically proven to work (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23964934). It is new, so it came too late for the businesses behind these numbers. It can help yours.

Why small businesses fail before the owner sees it: draw two forecasts

You control exactly as much as you can foresee. So give yourself something to foresee with. It takes about ten minutes per goal.

Here is the whole tool, with Andrew's coffee shop, "The Corner", beside each step (illustrative numbers).

1. Pick the goal you would defend to the end. Andrew picked his cash reserve, counted in months of expenses. Goal: 2.0 months by month 12. Today: 0.5.

2. Write your assumptions in one line. Andrew's: all operating profit goes into the reserve, and monthly expenses stay flat. An assumption on paper can be checked. One in your head only surprises you.

3. Draw line one: if I change nothing. At a 9% margin, each month puts roughly 0.1 months into the reserve. He needs 1.5 more. So the goal lands at month 16. The deadline was month 12. By month 3 the reserve sits near 0.8, below his red line of one month.

4. Draw line two: if my plan works. Andrew's plan lifts the margin from 9 to 15%. Then each month adds roughly 0.18. The goal lands at month 10, two months early.

5. Put both lines in one table.

Month Line one: change nothing Line two: the plan works
M0 0.50 0.50
M3 0.80 0.86
M6 1.09 1.35
M10 1.49 2.05
M12 1.69 2.41

6. Price one breakdown on each line. A repair or a fine worth 0.1 months of reserve pushes the goal back about a month at 9%. At 15% it costs about half a month. That is no reason to panic. It is a reason to keep the reserve growing.

7. Say it as a range, with a condition. A forecast as one number is almost always wrong. Andrew's honest version: "The reserve reaches two months between month 10 and month 12, as long as the margin stays at 14% or more."

8. Check the forecast against the facts once a month. The gap between line and fact is your teacher. Correct the line, never the facts.

That is the tool. Two lines, one table, one sentence with a condition.

How to stay out of the number: never decide on spending from one line alone. Ask which line your next decision pushes you towards.

In the Jirgenson Model, predictability belongs to a pair: the business plus the person at the wheel. The same shop is a mystery to a novice and a rhythm to an experienced manager. A forecast is a hypothesis you test and correct, not a prophecy.

Business survival over five years: say the bad line out loud, then act

Open a shop on Monday, and half the ones that opened with you are gone in five years.

That isn't fate. Plenty of warning fits inside five years. Some of those owners might have made it with an honest forecast, said early.

A bad forecast can go two ways. The first is a loud prophecy: "We're done for." That is panic, not a forecast, and it spoils every decision after it. The second is to name the risk while there is time, and attach an action in the same breath.

Andrew did the second. He said line one out loud to Sara and chose line two at once: a revised menu and a talk with the supplier.

Suppose instead he had told the team, "We'll go bust in three months." Sara starts looking for another job. The baristas work at half strength. The reserve falls faster. The same forecast, without action, makes itself come true.

Do this: when you share a bad line, share the plan in the next sentence. "If nothing changes, we cross the red line in March. Here is what we change on Monday."

How to avoid it: a risk named early and backed by action buys you time. A risk named late buys you nothing.

The business failure rate over ten years: who is at the wheel?

Ten years in, two out of three new businesses have vanished. Which side are you on?

Of every ten businesses that opened in 2000, fewer than two are still open today.

Those numbers didn't have to be that steep. A decade tests something no business plan mentions: who can see ahead. With a control system, some of those owners might have handed that sight to the right people. The odds could look different.

Think of one car. To a nervous passenger it's a lump of metal out of control. A professional drives it calmly. Same physics, different person at the wheel.

The Corner learned this on a Friday evening. A new trainee stood at the coffee machine in panic. The queue grew. Orders got mixed up. Sara stepped in, and twenty minutes later the queue was gone. She wasn't faster. She knew what came next.

Then she made that knowledge portable. She and the trainee drew a map of Friday on one sheet: the time, what happens, what to do five minutes before. The office wave lands soon after half past five. By then the second batch is grinding and the milk is open.

Do this for your hardest day:

  1. Ask who sees it coming. Find the person who foresees that area best.
  2. Put that person at the wheel of it. Andrew gave Sara the Friday shifts.
  3. Turn their knowledge into a map: the time, the event, the step five minutes before.

How to avoid it: you can't pick your side by hoping. You can pick who steers.

Why small businesses fail in year two: the tired forecaster

The most dangerous year isn't the first one. It's the second.

The second year doesn't have to break you. By then the opening energy has gone and the long hours have stayed. A control system might have shown some of those owners the price of fatigue before the accounts did.

In his early months Andrew worked sixty-five hours a week. He made decisions late at night. Those were the hours of his worst mistakes. He mixed up figures, put off the call to the supplier and gave wrong deadlines.

Fatigue corrupts a forecast like a faulty instrument. So put your own hours on the goal list, with a number. It protects every forecast you make.

Do this: write your hours per week beside your other goals. Andrew's goal: 40 or fewer. His reading: 65.

How to avoid it: draw your lines with a clear head, never at midnight. And switch on the headlights: watch a number that moves before the result, such as enquiries or the flow of guests. Andrew spotted his supplier's prices rising before the statement came, in the weekly margin per receipt.

What six more months on line one cost

Stay on line one and the bill is concrete. At month 12 the reserve sits at 1.69 months instead of 2.41. The goal itself waits until month 16.

In money, the gap is the margin. At revenue of €30,000 a month, a 9% margin keeps €2,700. A 15% margin keeps €4,500. That is €1,800 a month, or €10,800 over six months.

Every breakdown also costs almost twice the time on line one.

The human cost is plain. Another half year of sixty-five hour weeks, with the big calls made after dark.

"Forecasts are always wrong"

As a single number, yes. That is why the tool asks for a range and a condition.

"Random" means you describe something with odds, not that it has no causes. Milk prices jump about for someone who checks them monthly. The supplier who knows the milking schedule sees them coming. So learn the mechanism first. Some uncertainty will always stay. Plan with it instead of waving it away.

Where two forecasts stop

In the full loop (goal, state, error, levers, plan, forecast), the forecast is the last link. On its own it treats a symptom. You stop driving with your eyes on the mirror.

The cause can still win. Line two is only as good as the plan behind it. If that plan leans on things you can't move, line two is a wish with a chart.

Two steps close that gap.

  • The list of levers. What you can change with your own hands, and what only looks like a lever. Chapter 5.
  • One page per goal. The levers, resources, constraints and deadline that make line two real. Chapter 6.

Without them, the forecast shows you the cliff. It doesn't yet build the road around it.

Key takeaways

  • You control exactly as much as you can foresee.
  • Draw two lines per goal: change nothing, and the plan works.
  • Say a bad forecast early, with the action attached.
  • Put the person who sees furthest at the wheel of each area.
  • A forecast is a hypothesis, not a prophecy.

Two choices before next month

You have two choices. Keep steering by last month's statement, and learn about the slide when it lands. Or draw two lines for your main goal tonight, and pick one.

Next Monday you know where March is heading. So does your team.

The forecasts show the road ahead. The list of levers and one page per goal decide what you do on it. If you haven't measured where you stand today, begin with the small business failure rate and the evening table.

Questions people ask

What is the main reason small businesses fail?

The reasons differ from shop to shop. One you can fix yourself is foresight: deciding on last month's numbers instead of next quarter's. Draw two forecasts for your main goal and act on the gap between them.

How do you forecast whether a small business will survive?

No forecast can promise survival. Draw two lines for your cash reserve: change nothing, and the plan works. Write the result as a range with a condition.

How far ahead should a small business forecast?

Start with the next three months, in numbers and dates, and update the line every month. The complete control system keeps a rolling forecast of three to six months.
