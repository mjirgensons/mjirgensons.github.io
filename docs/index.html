What this book is

Every day the business decides your day: the breakdown, the late supplier, the client who hasn't paid. You solve problems in the order they arrive and call it being busy. It is guessing, with extra steps. The bill for it comes at the end of the month, in the bank balance.

Steer, Don't Guess replaces guessing with instruments. It is one method, about 250 pages, for owners, managers and freelancers who are the only thing holding it together. It is not a motivational book and it does not say "delegate more". It gives mechanics: goals in an order you chose, your real position in numbers rather than feelings, red lines drawn before the crisis, and a hard wall between the levers you can pull and the weather you can only watch.

The Jirgenson Model is a management method built on three questions: where do you want to go (a short list of measurable goals in refusal order), where are you now (the same list filled in with measured facts), and what can you actually move to close the gap (levers, kept apart from conditions). Everything else in the book is a way to do this regularly, precisely and on course. The Jirgenson Model explained →

What you will learn

  • The refusal test. The one question that turns a wish list into an order, so a crisis never chooses for you: if circumstances force you to drop goals one by one, which goes first, and which do you defend to the last?
  • One number, one source. How a coffee-shop owner who was "doing fine" found his real margin once the write-offs were counted.
  • Red lines before the crisis. Three zones, normal, acceptable and emergency, that tell you when to keep going and when to drop everything. And a fourth, "undefined", for the moment you have no data and must not pretend it is green.
  • Levers versus weather. What you can actually pull (prices, the menu, terms, hours) and what you can only account for (the season, a competitor, your own lack of time), so you stop spending energy on the second kind.
  • Three ways to control. By plan, by deviation, by forecast, and why the first two always leave you reacting too late.
  • Why good plans fail. Three offsets to check before acting: a badly defined goal, a wrong starting state, a dying environment.
  • The backward move. Planning from the goal back to this week, with a table that answers "what must be true one step before the result".
  • The Red Card. A one-page protocol for the day everything burns.

Who it is for, and who it is not for

For owners of small businesses, managers, freelancers and families who are tired of being the only thing holding it together, and who are willing to read their numbers. The method works for a thirteen-year-old saving for a bike and for a two-chair dental practice, because every chapter is written on three levels: a simple everyday example, a practical business case, and a formal level with definitions.

Not for anyone shopping for a guarantee. Forecasts can be wrong. Data can be incomplete. The method does not replace knowing your trade. It replaces guessing.

Proof, not promises

The book ships with the model's canon and a ready operating prompt for an AI assistant. With them, an assistant stops giving generic advice and works with your numbers in a fixed order of analysis. This was tested, not asserted. Ten realistic business crises, from a food truck 72 hours from losing its permit to a freelancer six days from rent, each answered by the same AI with the method and without it, then judged blind by a stronger model against an answer key fixed in advance. Left to itself, the AI slipped an invented fact into seven answers in ten. With the Jirgenson Model, one answer in a hundred and twenty. It won 30 blind pairings out of 30, scored 88.8 out of 100 against 56.2 (58% higher), beat a good generic consultant prompt by 31%, and broke zero rules in 120 blind reviews. What early readers say

Six early reviews agree on the same points, including the critical ones. "Most management books give advice. This one gives mechanics." The refusal test is the idea reviewers quote most. The distinction between levers and conditions is called the most liberating in the book. Every reviewer notes that the author promises nothing, and one adds that this caution is what makes the approach credible.

What they criticise: the terminology is dense in places (goal vectors, state vectors, zones, three offsets), some examples repeat, and readers fluent in OKR and PDCA will find parts familiar. Questions readers ask

Is the Jirgenson Model like OKR or PDCA?

It sits on top of them. OKR gives you objectives and key results; PDCA gives you a cycle. The Jirgenson Model adds what both leave out: an explicit refusal order among goals, zones with red lines set in advance, a strict split between levers and conditions, and control by forecast instead of by looking in the rear-view mirror. Chapter 14 shows where OKR, Balanced Scorecard, MBO, Theory of Constraints, PDCA and EOS fit inside it.

Do I need to know maths or control theory?

No. Every chapter has a simple level with an everyday example and a practical level with a business case. The formal level, with definitions and one formula for the backward move, is there for readers who want it and can be skipped.

Does the AI prompt work with ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini?

The canon and the operating prompt are plain text files and work with any capable language model: you attach the canon, send the prompt, then describe your situation. The published blind test used one model family; results with others may differ, and the book says so.

What is the refusal test?

A single question: if circumstances forced you to drop your goals one by one, which would go first, and which would you defend to the last? The last one standing is your real priority. Most people cannot answer in ten seconds, which means fatigue, haste and whoever shouts loudest are answering for them.
